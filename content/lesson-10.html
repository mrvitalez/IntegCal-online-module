8.1Arc Length

How long is a curved path? You cannot lay a ruler along a curve, but you can approximate it with straight pieces, just as Riemann sums approximate area with rectangles (Stewart §8.1).

Divide \([a,b]\) into \(n\) pieces and join the points \(P_i = (x_i, f(x_i))\) on the graph by straight segments. The polygonal path has length \(\sum |P_{i-1}P_i|\), and as \(n \to \infty\) this approaches the length of the curve. By the Mean Value Theorem, each segment has length \(\sqrt{1 + [f'(x_i^*)]^2}\,\Delta x\) for some \(x_i^*\) in the piece. That gives the arc length formula: if \(f'\) is continuous on \([a,b]\),

\[L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \bigl[f'(x)\bigr]^2}\,dx = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr)^2}\,dx.\]

You can think of it as Pythagoras on a tiny scale: a small step \(dx\) across and \(dy\) up has length \(ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}\), so \(L = \int ds\).

Example. For \(y = \tfrac23 x^{3/2}\) on \([0,3]\), \(y' = \sqrt{x}\), so \(L = \int_0^3 \sqrt{1 + x}\,dx = \tfrac23\bigl[(1+x)^{3/2}\bigr]_0^3 = \tfrac23(8 - 1) = \tfrac{14}{3}\).

A warning about difficulty. The square root makes most arc length integrals impossible to evaluate exactly; even \(y = x^2\) or \(y = \sin x\) leads to hard or non-elementary integrals. Exercises with exact answers are usually built so that \(1 + (y')^2\) is a perfect square. For instance, if \(y = \tfrac{x^2}{4} - \tfrac{\ln x}{2}\), then \(1 + (y')^2 = \bigl(\tfrac{x}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2x}\bigr)^2\), so on \([1, e]\), \(L = \int_1^e \bigl(\tfrac{x}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2x}\bigr)dx = \tfrac{e^2 + 1}{4}\). Otherwise, use Simpson's Rule or a calculator. Either way, a quick sanity check is available: a curve is never shorter than the straight segment joining its endpoints. For \(y = \tfrac23 x^{3/2}\) the endpoints \((0,0)\) and \((3, 2\sqrt3)\) are about 4.58 apart, and indeed \(14/3 \approx 4.67\) is a little more.

Curves \(x = g(y)\). If it is easier to describe the curve as \(x = g(y)\), \(c \le y \le d\), swap the roles: \(L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + (dx/dy)^2}\,dy\). This is the better choice when \(dy/dx\) blows up somewhere on the curve (a vertical tangent), or when solving for \(y\) would be messy. Whichever variable you use, the limits must be values of that variable.

The arc length function. Measuring from a fixed starting point \(P_0(a, f(a))\), the distance along the curve to the point \((x, f(x))\) is

\[s(x) = \int_a^x \sqrt{1 + \bigl[f'(t)\bigr]^2}\,dt.\]

By the Fundamental Theorem, \(\frac{ds}{dx} = \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}\). For \(y = \tfrac23 x^{3/2}\) starting at \(x = 0\), \(s(x) = \tfrac23\bigl[(1+x)^{3/2} - 1\bigr]\).

Common mistake \(\sqrt{1 + (y')^2}\) is not \(1 + y'\). You may only drop the square root after showing that the expression inside is a perfect square, and then you take the positive square root.
Key equations
\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \bigl[f'(x)\bigr]^2}\,dx \]
\[ L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{dx}{dy}\Bigr)^2}\,dy \]
\[ s(x) = \int_a^x \sqrt{1 + \bigl[f'(t)\bigr]^2}\,dt \]

8.2Area of a Surface of Revolution

Rotate a curve (not a region) about an axis and you get a surface, like the outside of a vase or a lampshade. Stewart §8.2 finds its area by the same strategy as arc length: approximate the curve by straight segments, find the area of the simple surface each segment sweeps out, add, and take a limit.

A straight segment rotated about an axis sweeps out a band, a slice of a cone (frustum). Its lateral area is \(2\pi r \ell\), where \(\ell\) is the slant length of the band and \(r\) is its average radius. For a tiny piece of the curve \(y = f(x)\) rotated about the \(x\)-axis, \(r \approx f(x)\) and \(\ell \approx ds = \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}\,dx\). So the surface area is

\[S = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x)\sqrt{1 + \bigl[f'(x)\bigr]^2}\,dx \qquad (f \ge 0).\]

A compact way to remember all cases is \(S = \int 2\pi r\,ds\), where \(r\) is the distance from the curve to the axis:

  • about the \(x\)-axis, \(r = y\);
  • about the \(y\)-axis, \(r = x\);
  • and \(ds\) can be written as \(\sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\,dx\) or \(\sqrt{1 + (dx/dy)^2}\,dy\), whichever is easier.

Example. Rotate \(y = \sqrt{x}\), \(0 \le x \le 2\), about the \(x\)-axis. Here \(y' = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\), so

\[\sqrt{x}\,\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{1}{4x}} = \sqrt{x + \tfrac14},\qquad S = 2\pi\int_0^2 \sqrt{x + \tfrac14}\,dx = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\Bigl[\bigl(\tfrac94\bigr)^{3/2} - \bigl(\tfrac14\bigr)^{3/2}\Bigr] = \tfrac{13\pi}{3}.\]

Notice how the factor \(f(x)\) combined neatly with the square root; that happens often, so simplify before integrating.

A familiar check. Rotating the upper semicircle \(y = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\), \(-r \le x \le r\), about the \(x\)-axis gives a sphere. Here \(f(x)\sqrt{1 + (f')^2} = r\), so \(S = \int_{-r}^{r} 2\pi r\,dx = 4\pi r^2\), the formula you learned in school.

About the \(y\)-axis. Rotating \(y = x^2\), \(0 \le x \le 1\), about the \(y\)-axis gives a bowl. The radius of each band is now \(x\), so \(S = \int_0^1 2\pi x\sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\,dx\). The substitution \(u = 1 + 4x^2\) turns this into \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\int_1^5 \sqrt{u}\,du = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\bigl(5\sqrt5 - 1\bigr) \approx 5.33\). Again, the right choice of radius made the substitution work.

Common mistake Using \(2\pi f(x)\,dx\) instead of \(2\pi f(x)\,ds\). Volume slices can use \(dx\) because a thin slab's thickness is \(dx\), but a surface band is tilted, and its width is the slant length \(ds\). Dropping the square root underestimates the area.

Also keep straight which formula you are using: volumes of revolution (Module 4) involve \(\pi r^2\) or \(2\pi r h\) and \(dx\); surface areas involve \(2\pi r\) and \(ds\).

Key equations
\[ S = \int_a^b 2\pi f(x)\sqrt{1 + \bigl[f'(x)\bigr]^2}\,dx \]
\[ S = \int 2\pi y\,ds \ (x\text{-axis}), \qquad S = \int 2\pi x\,ds \ (y\text{-axis}) \]

8.5Probability

Calculus is a natural tool for probability when a quantity can take any value in an interval, such as a waiting time, a height, or the lifetime of a battery. Such a quantity is a continuous random variable \(X\) (Stewart §8.5).

Every continuous random variable has a probability density function (pdf) \(f\). Probabilities are areas under its graph:

\[P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx.\]

A function \(f\) is a pdf exactly when \(f(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x\) and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1\) (the total probability is 1). For example, \(f(x) = kx(3 - x)\) on \([0,3]\) (and 0 elsewhere) is a pdf when \(k\cdot\tfrac92 = 1\), i.e. \(k = \tfrac29\). Then \(P(1 \le X \le 2) = \tfrac29\int_1^2 x(3-x)\,dx = \tfrac{13}{27}\).

Note that \(P(X = a) = \int_a^a f = 0\) for a continuous variable, so \(\le\) and \(<\) give the same probabilities. Also, \(f(x)\) itself is not a probability; it can even exceed 1.

The cumulative distribution function (cdf) is \(F(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt\). It rises from 0 to 1, and by the Fundamental Theorem \(F'(x) = f(x)\). Then \(P(a \le X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)\).

Mean and median. The mean (expected value) is the balance point of the density:

\[\mu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,dx.\]

The median \(m\) splits the probability in half: \(\int_{-\infty}^{m} f(x)\,dx = \tfrac12\), i.e. \(F(m) = \tfrac12\).

Exponential distribution. Waiting times (between calls, until a component fails) are often modelled by

\[f(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & t < 0 \\ \frac{1}{\mu}e^{-t/\mu} & t \ge 0 \end{cases}\]

whose mean is \(\mu\). If the mean wait at a counter is 4 minutes, the chance of waiting more than 6 minutes is \(\int_6^\infty \tfrac14 e^{-t/4}\,dt = e^{-3/2} \approx 0.223\). In general, \(P(T > t) = e^{-t/\mu}\), and the median waiting time solves \(e^{-m/\mu} = \tfrac12\), giving \(m = \mu\ln 2 \approx 0.69\mu\). The median is less than the mean because a few very long waits pull the mean upward.

Normal distribution. Measurements such as heights or exam scores often follow the bell-shaped density

\[f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)},\]

with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). This integral has no elementary antiderivative, so probabilities are found numerically (a calculator, Simpson's Rule, or a table). About 68% of the probability lies within one standard deviation of the mean, and about 95% within two.

Common mistake Before using a function as a pdf, check that its total integral is 1. Forgetting the normalising constant \(k\) makes every probability wrong.
Key equations
\[ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1 \]
\[ F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt \]
\[ \mu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,dx \]
\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} \]

★Key takeaways

  • Arc length is \(L = \int ds\) with \(ds = \sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\,dx\) (or the \(dy\) version); exact answers usually need \(1 + (y')^2\) to be a perfect square.
  • The arc length function \(s(x) = \int_a^x \sqrt{1 + [f'(t)]^2}\,dt\) measures distance along the curve, and \(ds/dx = \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}\).
  • Surface area of revolution is \(S = \int 2\pi r\,ds\), where \(r\) is the distance to the axis; never replace \(ds\) by \(dx\).
  • A pdf is nonnegative with total area 1, and probabilities are areas: \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\).
  • The mean is \(\int x f(x)\,dx\); the median \(m\) satisfies \(F(m) = \tfrac12\).
  • Exponential waiting times satisfy \(P(T > t) = e^{-t/\mu}\); normal probabilities are computed numerically.

Example 10.1 Arc length with a perfect square

Find the exact length of the curve \(y = \dfrac{x^3}{6} + \dfrac{1}{2x}\) for \(1 \le x \le 2\).

(Similar to Stewart §8.1, Exercises 11-20)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Compute \(y'\), then \(1 + (y')^2\), and look for a perfect square. Curves of the form \(ax^p + bx^{-q}\) are often designed this way.

Simplify the integrand:

\(y' = \dfrac{x^2}{2} - \dfrac{1}{2x^2}\), so \((y')^2 = \dfrac{x^4}{4} - \dfrac12 + \dfrac{1}{4x^4}\).

Then \(1 + (y')^2 = \dfrac{x^4}{4} + \dfrac12 + \dfrac{1}{4x^4} = \Bigl(\dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{1}{2x^2}\Bigr)^2\). Only the sign of the middle term changed.

Solution:

\[L = \int_1^2 \Bigl(\frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{1}{2x^2}\Bigr)dx = \Bigl[\frac{x^3}{6} - \frac{1}{2x}\Bigr]_1^2 = \Bigl(\frac43 - \frac14\Bigr) - \Bigl(\frac16 - \frac12\Bigr) = \frac{17}{12}.\]

Check:

The endpoints are \((1, \tfrac23)\) and \((2, \tfrac{19}{12})\), so the straight-line distance is \(\sqrt{1 + (11/12)^2} \approx 1.357\). The curve must be at least that long, and \(17/12 \approx 1.417\) is slightly longer. Good.

Example 10.2 Surface area of a cubic horn

The curve \(y = x^3\), \(0 \le x \le 1\), is rotated about the \(x\)-axis. Find the area of the resulting surface.

(Similar to Stewart §8.2, Example 1)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use \(S = \int 2\pi y\,ds\) with \(y = x^3\) and \(ds = \sqrt{1 + 9x^4}\,dx\). The factor \(x^3\) is (up to a constant) the derivative of \(1 + 9x^4\), so substitute.

Set up:

\[S = \int_0^1 2\pi x^3\sqrt{1 + 9x^4}\,dx.\]

Let \(u = 1 + 9x^4\), \(du = 36x^3\,dx\); when \(x = 0, u = 1\), when \(x = 1, u = 10\).

Solution:

\[S = \frac{2\pi}{36}\int_1^{10} u^{1/2}\,du = \frac{\pi}{18}\cdot\frac23\bigl[u^{3/2}\bigr]_1^{10} = \frac{\pi}{27}\bigl(10\sqrt{10} - 1\bigr) \approx 3.563.\]

Check:

The surface is a horn ending in a circle of radius 1. A cone with the same end circle and slant height \(\sqrt2\) has lateral area \(\pi(1)\sqrt2 \approx 4.44\). Our curve hugs the axis for small \(x\), so it should have less area, and 3.56 < 4.44 fits.

Example 10.3 Normalising a density, then finding a probability and the mean

Let \(f(x) = kx^2(2 - x)\) for \(0 \le x \le 2\) and \(f(x) = 0\) otherwise. (a) Find \(k\) so that \(f\) is a pdf. (b) Find \(P(X \le 1)\). (c) Find the mean of \(X\).

(Similar to Stewart §8.5, Examples 1-2)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use the three integrals of §8.5: total area 1 fixes \(k\); the area from 0 to 1 is the probability; \(\int x f(x)\,dx\) is the mean. Note \(f \ge 0\) on \([0,2]\) once \(k > 0\).

(a) Normalise:

\(\int_0^2 x^2(2 - x)\,dx = \bigl[\tfrac{2x^3}{3} - \tfrac{x^4}{4}\bigr]_0^2 = \tfrac{16}{3} - 4 = \tfrac43\). So \(k\cdot\tfrac43 = 1\) and \(k = \tfrac34\).

(b) Probability:

\(P(X \le 1) = \tfrac34\int_0^1 (2x^2 - x^3)\,dx = \tfrac34\bigl(\tfrac23 - \tfrac14\bigr) = \tfrac34\cdot\tfrac{5}{12} = \tfrac{5}{16} = 0.3125\).

(c) Mean:

\(\mu = \tfrac34\int_0^2 x^3(2 - x)\,dx = \tfrac34\bigl[\tfrac{x^4}{2} - \tfrac{x^5}{5}\bigr]_0^2 = \tfrac34\bigl(8 - \tfrac{32}{5}\bigr) = \tfrac34\cdot\tfrac85 = \tfrac65\).

Check:

The density is lopsided toward the right (its peak is at \(x = 4/3\)), so the mean \(1.2\) lies to the right of the midpoint 1, and less than half the probability (\(5/16\)) lies to the left of 1. Both results agree with the picture.
