8.1Arc Length

We already know how to find the area under a curve. Now let's ask a different question: how long is the curve itself? You cannot lay a ruler along a curved path. So what do we do? The same thing we did with areas: approximate, then take a limit (Stewart §8.1).

Divide \([a,b]\) into \(n\) small pieces and join the points \(P_i = (x_i, f(x_i))\) on the graph by straight segments. The length of this broken-line path is easy to compute, and as \(n \to \infty\) it approaches the length of the curve. Notice that a tiny step \(dx\) across and \(dy\) up is the hypotenuse of a small right triangle, so its length is \(ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} = \sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\,dx\). Adding all these tiny lengths gives the formula.

Arc length To find the arc length of a curve defined by a function \(y = f(x)\) from \(x = a\) to \(x = b\) (with \(f'\) continuous), we use \[L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr)^2}\,dx. \qquad (1)\]

So the first step is always the same: differentiate your \(y\). Then substitute \(dy/dx\) into the formula and simplify before you integrate. For example, for \(y = \tfrac23 x^{3/2}\) on \([0,3]\) we get \(dy/dx = \sqrt{x}\), so \(L = \int_0^3 \sqrt{1 + x}\,dx = \tfrac23\bigl[(1+x)^{3/2}\bigr]_0^3 = \tfrac23(8 - 1) = \tfrac{14}{3}\).

Is that reasonable? Take note that a curve is never shorter than the straight line joining its endpoints. The endpoints \((0,0)\) and \((3, 2\sqrt3)\) are \(\sqrt{21} \approx 4.58\) apart, and \(14/3 \approx 4.67\) is a little more. Good.

Curves \(x = g(y)\). Sometimes it is easier to write the curve with \(x\) as a function of \(y\), \(c \le y \le d\). Then we just swap the roles: \(L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + (dx/dy)^2}\,dy\). Take the arc of the parabola \(y^2 = x\) from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\). Here \(x = y^2\), so \(dx/dy = 2y\) and \(L = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + 4y^2}\,dy \approx 1.479\) (this one needs a trigonometric substitution). Notice that the limits are \(y\)-values now.

Parametric curves. For a curve given by \(x = x(t)\), \(y = y(t)\), the arc length from \(t = a\) to \(t = b\) is \[L = \int_a^b \sqrt{\Bigl(\frac{dx}{dt}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{dy}{dt}\Bigr)^2}\,dt. \qquad (2)\] It is the same \(ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}\) idea, only now both \(dx\) and \(dy\) come from \(dt\). You will see this again with parametric curves in Stewart §10.2.

The arc length function. If we measure from a fixed starting point \(P_0(a, f(a))\), the distance along the curve to the point \((x, f(x))\) is \[s(x) = \int_a^x \sqrt{1 + \bigl[f'(t)\bigr]^2}\,dt,\] and by the Fundamental Theorem, \(ds/dx = \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}\). Let's try \(y = x^2 - \tfrac18\ln x\), starting at \((1,1)\). We get \(y' = 2x - \frac{1}{8x}\), and \(1 + (y')^2 = \bigl(2x + \frac{1}{8x}\bigr)^2\), a perfect square. So \(s(x) = \int_1^x \bigl(2t + \frac{1}{8t}\bigr)dt = x^2 + \tfrac18\ln x - 1\).

Why did the square root go away so nicely? Because the curve was built that way. Most arc length integrals, even for \(y = x^2\) or \(y = \sin x\), cannot be done exactly; then we use Simpson's Rule or a calculator.

Common mistake \(\sqrt{1 + (y')^2}\) is not \(1 + y'\). You may only drop the square root after you have shown that the expression inside is a perfect square, and then you take the positive square root.
Key equations
\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr)^2}\,dx \]
\[ L = \int_c^d \sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{dx}{dy}\Bigr)^2}\,dy \]
\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{\Bigl(\frac{dx}{dt}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{dy}{dt}\Bigr)^2}\,dt \]
\[ s(x) = \int_a^x \sqrt{1 + \bigl[f'(t)\bigr]^2}\,dt \]

8.2Area of a Surface of Revolution

Now let's rotate a curve (not a region) about an axis. What we get is a surface, like the outside of a vase or a lampshade. How do we find its area? Same strategy as arc length (Stewart §8.2): cut the curve into tiny pieces, find the area each piece sweeps out, and add.

A tiny piece of the curve of length \(ds\) at a distance \(r\) from the axis sweeps out a thin band. If we cut the band and flatten it, it is almost a strip of length \(2\pi r\) (the circumference) and width \(ds\). So its area is about \(2\pi r\,ds\), and the surface area is \(S = \int 2\pi r\,ds\).

Surface area To find the surface area of a solid formed by revolving a curve around an axis:
  • For rotation around the \(x\)-axis, curve \(y = f(x)\), \(a \le x \le b\): \[S = 2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr)^2}\,dx. \qquad (3)\]
  • For rotation around the \(y\)-axis, curve \(x = g(y)\), \(c \le y \le d\): \[S = 2\pi\int_c^d g(y)\sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{dx}{dy}\Bigr)^2}\,dy. \qquad (4)\]

Notice that both formulas are just "\(2\pi\) times radius times \(ds\)". About the \(x\)-axis the radius is \(y\); about the \(y\)-axis the radius is \(x\). If it is easier to keep the curve as \(y = f(x)\) while rotating about the \(y\)-axis, you can also use \(S = 2\pi\int_a^b x\sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\,dx\).

A quick example. Rotate \(y = \sqrt{x}\), \(0 \le x \le 2\), about the \(x\)-axis. First, differentiate: \(dy/dx = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\). Then \[\sqrt{x}\,\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{1}{4x}} = \sqrt{x + \tfrac14},\qquad S = 2\pi\int_0^2 \sqrt{x + \tfrac14}\,dx = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\Bigl[\bigl(\tfrac94\bigr)^{3/2} - \bigl(\tfrac14\bigr)^{3/2}\Bigr] = \tfrac{13\pi}{3}.\] Take note how \(f(x)\) combined with the square root. That happens often, so simplify before you integrate.

About the \(y\)-axis. Let's rotate the arc of the parabola \(y = x^2\) from \((1,1)\) to \((2,4)\) about the \(y\)-axis. The radius of each band is now \(x\), and \(dy/dx = 2x\), so \(S = 2\pi\int_1^2 x\sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\,dx\). With \(u = 1 + 4x^2\), \(du = 8x\,dx\), this becomes \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\int_5^{17} \sqrt{u}\,du = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\bigl(17\sqrt{17} - 5\sqrt5\bigr) \approx 30.85\).

Does it agree with geometry? Rotate the upper semicircle \(y = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\), \(-r \le x \le r\), about the \(x\)-axis. You get a sphere. Here \(f(x)\sqrt{1 + (f')^2}\) simplifies to just \(r\), so \(S = \int_{-r}^{r} 2\pi r\,dx = 4\pi r^2\). That is the formula you learned in high school.

Common mistake Using \(2\pi f(x)\,dx\) instead of \(2\pi f(x)\,ds\). For volumes we could use \(dx\), because a thin slab has thickness \(dx\). But a surface band is tilted, and its width is the slant length \(ds\). Dropping the square root gives too small an area.

Also, do not mix up the formulas. Volumes of revolution (Module 4) use \(\pi r^2\) or \(2\pi r h\) with \(dx\); surface areas use \(2\pi r\) with \(ds\).

Key equations
\[ S = 2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr)^2}\,dx \ (x\text{-axis}) \]
\[ S = 2\pi\int_c^d g(y)\sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{dx}{dy}\Bigr)^2}\,dy \ (y\text{-axis}) \]

8.5Probability

Some quantities can take any value in an interval: a waiting time, a height, the lifetime of a battery. What is the probability that such a quantity lands between two values? Let's use integrals (Stewart §8.5). A quantity like this is a continuous random variable \(X\).

Definition In continuous probability, the probability density function (PDF) \(f(x)\) satisfies
  • \(f(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x\), and
  • \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1\).
The probability that a random variable \(X\) lies between \(a\) and \(b\) is \[P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \qquad (5)\]

So, a probability is an area under the graph of the pdf, and the total area is 1 because something must happen. For example, let \(f(x) = 0.006x(10 - x)\) for \(0 \le x \le 10\) and \(f(x) = 0\) otherwise. Is it a pdf? It is never negative on \([0,10]\), and \(\int_0^{10} 0.006x(10 - x)\,dx = 0.006\cdot\tfrac{500}{3} = 1\). Yes. Then \(P(4 \le X \le 8) = 0.006\int_4^8 (10x - x^2)\,dx = 0.544\).

What if the total is not 1? Then we fix it with a constant. For \(f(x) = kx(3 - x)\) on \([0,3]\), \(\int_0^3 x(3-x)\,dx = \tfrac92\), so we need \(k = \tfrac29\). Then \(P(1 \le X \le 2) = \tfrac29\int_1^2 x(3-x)\,dx = \tfrac{13}{27}\).

Take note that \(P(X = a) = \int_a^a f(x)\,dx = 0\), so \(\le\) and \(<\) give the same probabilities. Also, \(f(x)\) itself is not a probability; it can even be bigger than 1.

The cumulative distribution function (cdf) is \(F(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt\). It rises from 0 to 1, and by the Fundamental Theorem \(F'(x) = f(x)\). Then \(P(a \le X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)\).

Mean and median. The expected value (mean) of \(X\) is \[\mu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,dx. \qquad (6)\] You can think of it as the balance point of the graph of \(f\). The median \(m\) splits the probability in half: \(\int_{-\infty}^{m} f(x)\,dx = \tfrac12\).

Exponential distribution. Waiting times (between calls, until a part fails) are often modelled by \[f(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & t < 0 \\ \frac{1}{\mu}e^{-t/\mu} & t \ge 0 \end{cases}\] whose mean is \(\mu\). If the mean wait at a counter is 4 minutes, the chance of waiting more than 6 minutes is \(\int_6^\infty \tfrac14 e^{-t/4}\,dt = e^{-3/2} \approx 0.223\). In general \(P(T > t) = e^{-t/\mu}\), and the median solves \(e^{-m/\mu} = \tfrac12\), so \(m = \mu\ln 2 \approx 0.69\mu\). Why is the median smaller than the mean? Because a few very long waits pull the mean up.

Normal distribution. Heights or exam scores often follow the bell-shaped density \[f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)},\] with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). This one has no elementary antiderivative, so we find its probabilities numerically (calculator, Simpson's Rule, or a table). About 68% of the probability lies within one standard deviation of the mean, and about 95% within two.

Common mistake Before you use a function as a pdf, check that its total integral is 1. If you forget the constant \(k\), every probability you compute will be wrong.
Key equations
\[ f(x) \ge 0, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1 \]
\[ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]
\[ \mu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,dx \]
\[ F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt \]
\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} \]

★Key takeaways

  • Arc length: \(L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\,dx\); for a parametric curve, \(L = \int_a^b \sqrt{(dx/dt)^2 + (dy/dt)^2}\,dt\). Always differentiate first.
  • The arc length function \(s(x) = \int_a^x \sqrt{1 + [f'(t)]^2}\,dt\) measures distance along the curve from the starting point, and \(ds/dx = \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2}\).
  • Surface area: \(S = 2\pi\int f(x)\sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\,dx\) about the \(x\)-axis and \(S = 2\pi\int g(y)\sqrt{1 + (dx/dy)^2}\,dy\) about the \(y\)-axis; never drop the square root.
  • A pdf satisfies \(f(x) \ge 0\) and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1\), and probabilities are areas: \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\).
  • The mean is \(\mu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,dx\); the median \(m\) satisfies \(\int_{-\infty}^{m} f(x)\,dx = \tfrac12\).
  • For an exponential waiting time with mean \(\mu\), \(P(T \gt t) = e^{-t/\mu}\); normal probabilities are found numerically.

Example 10.1 Arc length with a u-substitution

Find the length of the curve \(y = \tfrac13 x^{3/2}\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 4\).

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 4, Example 1; Stewart §8.1)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Curve: \(y = \tfrac13 x^{3/2}\)
  • Interval: \(0 \le x \le 4\)
  • Find: the arc length \(L\)
Step 1: Differentiate y

The formula needs \(dy/dx\), so let's differentiate first using the power rule.

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac13\cdot\frac32 x^{3/2 - 1}\]

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac12 x^{1/2}\]

Step 2: Substitute into the arc length formula

Put \(dy/dx\) into equation (1) and square it.

\[L = \int_0^4 \sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\tfrac12 x^{1/2}\Bigr)^2}\,dx\]

\[L = \int_0^4 \sqrt{1 + \tfrac14 x}\,dx\]

Step 3: Substitute u and change the limits

Let \(u = 1 + \tfrac14 x\). Then \(du = \tfrac14\,dx\), so \(dx = 4\,du\).

When \(x = 0\), \(u = 1\); when \(x = 4\), \(u = 1 + 1 = 2\).

\[L = 4\int_1^2 \sqrt{u}\,du\]

Step 4: Integrate and apply the limits

Use the power rule, \(\int u^{1/2}\,du = \tfrac23 u^{3/2}\).

\[L = 4\cdot\frac23\,u^{3/2}\Big]_1^2\]

\[L = \frac83\bigl(2^{3/2} - 1^{3/2}\bigr)\]

\[L = \frac83\bigl(2\sqrt2 - 1\bigr) \approx \frac83(1.8284) \approx 4.88\]

Answer

The length of the curve is \(L = \tfrac83\bigl(2\sqrt2 - 1\bigr) \approx 4.88\) units.

Check

The endpoints are \((0,0)\) and \((4, \tfrac83)\), which are \(\sqrt{16 + 64/9} \approx 4.81\) apart. The curve should be a little longer, and \(4.88 > 4.81\). Good.

Example 10.2 Arc length of y = ln(cos x)

Find the length of \(y = \ln(\cos x)\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\).

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 4, Example 2; Stewart §8.1)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Curve: \(y = \ln(\cos x)\)
  • Interval: \(0 \le x \le \tfrac{\pi}{4}\)
  • Find: the arc length \(L\)
Step 1: Differentiate y using the chain rule

Recall that \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln u = \frac{1}{u}\frac{du}{dx}\), with \(u = \cos x\).

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot(-\sin x)\]

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = -\tan x\]

Step 2: Substitute into the arc length formula

Square the derivative; the minus sign disappears.

\[L = \int_0^{\pi/4} \sqrt{1 + (-\tan x)^2}\,dx\]

\[L = \int_0^{\pi/4} \sqrt{1 + \tan^2 x}\,dx\]

Step 3: Use a Pythagorean identity

Recall \(1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x\). Since \(\sec x > 0\) on \([0, \pi/4]\), the square root is just \(\sec x\).

\[L = \int_0^{\pi/4} \sqrt{\sec^2 x}\,dx\]

\[L = \int_0^{\pi/4} \sec x\,dx\]

Step 4: Integrate and apply the limits

Use \(\int \sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + C\).

\[L = \ln|\sec x + \tan x|\,\Big]_0^{\pi/4}\]

\[L = \ln\Bigl(\sec\tfrac{\pi}{4} + \tan\tfrac{\pi}{4}\Bigr) - \ln(\sec 0 + \tan 0)\]

\[L = \ln\bigl(\sqrt2 + 1\bigr) - \ln(1 + 0)\]

Since \(\ln 1 = 0\), the second term is zero.

\[L = \ln\bigl(\sqrt2 + 1\bigr) \approx 0.88\]

Answer

The length of the curve is \(L = \ln\bigl(\sqrt2 + 1\bigr) \approx 0.88\) units.

Check

The endpoints are \((0, 0)\) and \((\tfrac{\pi}{4}, \ln\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}) \approx (0.785, -0.347)\), which are about 0.86 apart. The curve is slightly longer. Good.

Example 10.3 Arc length of a parametric curve

Find the length of the curve \(x = t^2\), \(y = t^3\), \(0 \le t \le 2\).

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 4, Example 3; see also Stewart §10.2)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Parametric curve: \(x = t^2\), \(y = t^3\)
  • Parameter interval: \(0 \le t \le 2\)
  • Find: the arc length \(L\)
Step 1: Differentiate x and y with respect to t

For a parametric curve we use equation (2), which needs both derivatives.

\[\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2\]

Step 2: Substitute into the parametric formula

\[L = \int_0^2 \sqrt{(2t)^2 + (3t^2)^2}\,dt\]

\[L = \int_0^2 \sqrt{4t^2 + 9t^4}\,dt\]

Step 3: Factor out t² from the root

Both terms contain \(t^2\), and \(\sqrt{t^2} = t\) because \(t \ge 0\) here.

\[\sqrt{4t^2 + 9t^4} = \sqrt{t^2(4 + 9t^2)} = t\sqrt{4 + 9t^2}\]

\[L = \int_0^2 t\sqrt{4 + 9t^2}\,dt\]

Step 4: Substitute u and change the limits

Let \(u = 4 + 9t^2\). Then \(du = 18t\,dt\), so \(t\,dt = \tfrac{1}{18}\,du\).

When \(t = 0\), \(u = 4\); when \(t = 2\), \(u = 4 + 36 = 40\).

\[L = \frac{1}{18}\int_4^{40} \sqrt{u}\,du\]

Step 5: Integrate and apply the limits

\[L = \frac{1}{18}\cdot\frac23\,u^{3/2}\Big]_4^{40}\]

\[L = \frac{1}{27}\bigl(40^{3/2} - 4^{3/2}\bigr)\]

\[L = \frac{1}{27}\bigl(80\sqrt{10} - 8\bigr) \approx \frac{1}{27}(252.98 - 8) \approx 9.07\]

Answer

The length of the curve is \(L = \tfrac{1}{27}\bigl(80\sqrt{10} - 8\bigr) \approx 9.07\) units.

Check

The curve runs from \((0,0)\) to \((4, 8)\), a straight distance of \(\sqrt{80} \approx 8.94\). The curve is slightly longer. Good.

Example 10.4 Surface area: rotating y = √x about the x-axis

Find the surface area of the solid obtained by rotating \(y = \sqrt{x}\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 4\) about the \(x\)-axis.

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 4, Example 4; Stewart §8.2)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Curve: \(y = \sqrt{x}\), \(0 \le x \le 4\)
  • Axis of rotation: the \(x\)-axis
  • Find: the surface area \(S\)
Step 1: Differentiate y

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\]

Step 2: Substitute into formula (3)

About the \(x\)-axis the radius is \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\).

\[S = 2\pi\int_0^4 \sqrt{x}\,\sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\Bigr)^2}\,dx\]

\[S = 2\pi\int_0^4 \sqrt{x}\,\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{4x}}\,dx\]

Step 3: Simplify the integrand

Combine the two square roots into one, then simplify.

\[\sqrt{x}\,\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{4x}} = \sqrt{x + \frac14} = \sqrt{\frac{4x + 1}{4}} = \frac12\sqrt{4x + 1}\]

\[S = 2\pi\cdot\frac12\int_0^4 \sqrt{4x + 1}\,dx = \pi\int_0^4 \sqrt{4x + 1}\,dx\]

Step 4: Substitute u and change the limits

Let \(u = 4x + 1\). Then \(du = 4\,dx\), so \(dx = \tfrac14\,du\).

When \(x = 0\), \(u = 1\); when \(x = 4\), \(u = 17\).

\[S = \frac{\pi}{4}\int_1^{17} \sqrt{u}\,du\]

Step 5: Integrate and apply the limits

\[S = \frac{\pi}{4}\cdot\frac23\,u^{3/2}\Big]_1^{17}\]

\[S = \frac{\pi}{6}\bigl(17^{3/2} - 1\bigr)\]

\[S = \frac{\pi}{6}\bigl(17\sqrt{17} - 1\bigr) \approx \frac{\pi}{6}(69.093) \approx 36.18\]

Answer

The surface area is \(S = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\bigl(17\sqrt{17} - 1\bigr) \approx 36.18\) square units.

Check

Do not forget the \(\pi\): without it you would get about 11.5, which is far too small. The surface ends in a circle of radius 2; a cone with the same end circle and slant height \(\sqrt{20}\) has lateral area \(\pi(2)\sqrt{20} \approx 28.1\). Our curve bulges outward compared with that cone, so a larger area, 36.18, makes sense.

Example 10.5 Probability and mean for f(x) = 2x

Let \(f(x) = 2x\) for \(0 \le x \le 1\) (and \(f(x) = 0\) otherwise). Find (a) \(P(0.25 \le X \le 0.75)\) and (b) the mean \(\mu\).

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 4, Example 5; Stewart §8.5)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • PDF: \(f(x) = 2x\) on \([0, 1]\), zero elsewhere
  • Find: (a) \(P(0.25 \le X \le 0.75)\); (b) \(\mu\)
Step 1: Make sure f is a pdf

\(2x \ge 0\) on \([0,1]\), and the total area is 1:

\[\int_0^1 2x\,dx = x^2\Big]_0^1 = 1\]

Step 2: (a) Substitute into equation (5)

\[P(0.25 \le X \le 0.75) = \int_{0.25}^{0.75} 2x\,dx\]

\[= x^2\Big]_{0.25}^{0.75}\]

\[= (0.75)^2 - (0.25)^2\]

\[= 0.5625 - 0.0625 = 0.5\]

Step 3: (b) Substitute into equation (6)

Outside \([0,1]\) the pdf is zero, so we only integrate from 0 to 1.

\[\mu = \int_0^1 x\cdot 2x\,dx\]

\[= \int_0^1 2x^2\,dx\]

\[= \frac23 x^3\Big]_0^1\]

\[= \frac23(1) - \frac23(0) = \frac23\]

Answer

(a) \(P(0.25 \le X \le 0.75) = 0.5\). (b) The mean is \(\mu = \tfrac23 \approx 0.667\).

Check

The density grows to the right, so larger values are more likely. That is why the mean \(2/3\) is to the right of the midpoint \(1/2\).

Example 10.6 Exponential pdf: a tail probability and the mean

Let \(f(x) = \tfrac15 e^{-x/5}\) for \(x \ge 0\) (and \(f(x) = 0\) for \(x < 0\)). Find (a) \(P(X > 3)\) and (b) the mean \(\mu\).

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 4, Example 6; Stewart §8.5)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • PDF: \(f(x) = \tfrac15 e^{-x/5}\), \(x \ge 0\)
  • Find: (a) \(P(X > 3)\); (b) \(\mu\)
Step 1: (a) Write the probability as an improper integral

The event \(X > 3\) means from 3 to \(\infty\), so we replace \(\infty\) by \(t\) and take a limit.

\[P(X > 3) = \int_3^{\infty} \frac15 e^{-x/5}\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_3^{t} \frac15 e^{-x/5}\,dx\]

Step 2: Integrate and take the limit

An antiderivative of \(\tfrac15 e^{-x/5}\) is \(-e^{-x/5}\) (check: its derivative is \(\tfrac15 e^{-x/5}\)).

\[= \lim_{t\to\infty}\Bigl[-e^{-x/5}\Bigr]_3^{t}\]

\[= \lim_{t\to\infty}\bigl(-e^{-t/5} + e^{-3/5}\bigr)\]

Since \(e^{-t/5} \to 0\) as \(t \to \infty\),

\[P(X > 3) = e^{-3/5} \approx 0.5488\]

Step 3: (b) Set up the mean

Substitute into equation (6); the pdf is zero for \(x < 0\).

\[\mu = \int_0^{\infty} x\cdot\frac15 e^{-x/5}\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_0^{t} \frac{x}{5}e^{-x/5}\,dx\]

Step 4: Integrate by parts

Let \(u = x\) and \(dv = \tfrac15 e^{-x/5}\,dx\). Then \(du = dx\) and \(v = -e^{-x/5}\).

\[\int \frac{x}{5}e^{-x/5}\,dx = -x e^{-x/5} + \int e^{-x/5}\,dx\]

\[= -x e^{-x/5} - 5e^{-x/5} + C\]

Step 5: Apply the limits and take the limit

\[\int_0^{t} \frac{x}{5}e^{-x/5}\,dx = \bigl(-t e^{-t/5} - 5e^{-t/5}\bigr) - (0 - 5)\]

\[= 5 - t e^{-t/5} - 5e^{-t/5}\]

As \(t \to \infty\), \(e^{-t/5} \to 0\) and \(t e^{-t/5} \to 0\) (by l'Hospital's Rule, \(t/e^{t/5} \to 0\)).

\[\mu = 5 - 0 - 0 = 5\]

Answer

(a) \(P(X > 3) = e^{-3/5} \approx 0.549\). (b) The mean is \(\mu = 5\).

Check

This is an exponential pdf \(\tfrac{1}{\mu}e^{-x/\mu}\) with \(\mu = 5\), so the mean 5 and \(P(X > 3) = e^{-3/5}\) match the general formula \(P(T > t) = e^{-t/\mu}\).
