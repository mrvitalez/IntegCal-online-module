8.1Arc Length

In our previous modules, we used integrals to find areas and volumes. Now let's ask a different question: how long is a curve? If you walk along the path \(y = f(x)\) from \(x = a\) to \(x = b\), how far did you actually walk? For a straight line, we just use the distance formula. For a curve, we do what we always do in calculus: approximate it with many small straight pieces, then take a limit.

Divide \([a, b]\) into \(n\) small pieces and join the points on the curve with straight segments (Figure 10.1). A segment over a piece of width \(\Delta x\) rises by \(\Delta y\), so its length is \(\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2} = \sqrt{1 + (\Delta y/\Delta x)^2}\,\Delta x\). As the pieces get smaller, \(\Delta y/\Delta x \to dy/dx\), and the sum of the segments becomes an integral.

Arc length To find the arc length of a curve defined by a function \(y = f(x)\) from \(x = a\) to \(x = b\) (with \(f'\) continuous), we use \[L = \int_a^b\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx \qquad (1)\] If the curve is given as \(x = g(y)\), \(c \le y \le d\), swap the roles: \(L = \int_c^d\sqrt{1 + (dx/dy)^2}\,dy\). For parametric curves \(x = x(t)\), \(y = y(t)\), the arc length from \(t = a\) to \(t = b\) is given by \[L = \int_a^b\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt \qquad (2)\]

The formula is easy to write down, but take note: the hard part is always the integral. The square root makes most arc length integrals impossible to do exactly, so textbook problems are chosen so that \(1 + (dy/dx)^2\) becomes a perfect square, or so that a substitution works. For example, with \(y = \ln(\cos x)\) we get \(1 + \tan^2x = \sec^2x\), and the root disappears (Example 10.2). When nothing works, as for one arch of \(y = \sin x\), we compute the integral numerically.

The arc length function. If we measure the length from a fixed starting point \((a, f(a))\) to a moving point \((x, f(x))\), we get a function of \(x\): \[s(x) = \int_a^x\sqrt{1 + [f^{\prime}(t)]^2}\,dt.\] By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, \(\frac{ds}{dx} = \sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\), which we often write in the memorable differential form \((ds)^2 = (dx)^2 + (dy)^2\). Think of \(ds\) as a tiny piece of the curve, the hypotenuse of a tiny right triangle with legs \(dx\) and \(dy\).

Common mistake Squaring wrongly inside the root: \(\sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\) is not \(1 + dy/dx\). Simplify \(1 + (dy/dx)^2\) completely before you integrate, and check whether it is a perfect square.
Key equations
\[ L = \int_a^b\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx,\qquad L = \int_a^b\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt \]
\[ s(x) = \int_a^x\sqrt{1 + [f^{\prime}(t)]^2}\,dt,\qquad ds = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx \]

8.2Area of a Surface of Revolution

Now, what if we take a curve and spin it around an axis? We get a surface, like a vase, a bowl or a satellite dish. The question for this section is: how much material do we need to cover that surface? That is its surface area.

The idea is the same as for arc length. A tiny piece of the curve with length \(ds\), at a distance \(r\) from the axis, sweeps out a thin band when it rotates (Figure 10.2). The band is like a very short slanted cylinder with circumference \(2\pi r\) and slant width \(ds\), so its area is about \(2\pi r\,ds\). Adding all the bands gives an integral.

Surface area of revolution To find the surface area of a solid formed by revolving a curve around an axis:
• For rotation around the \(x\)-axis, curve \(y = f(x)\), \(a \le x \le b\) (the radius is \(y\)): \[S = 2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx \qquad (3)\] • For rotation around the \(y\)-axis, curve \(x = g(y)\), \(c \le y \le d\) (the radius is \(x\)): \[S = 2\pi\int_c^d g(y)\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dx}{dy}\right)^2}\,dy \qquad (4)\] In short: \(S = \int 2\pi(\text{radius})\,ds\), where the radius is the distance to the axis of rotation and \(ds\) can be written in terms of \(dx\) or \(dy\), whichever is easier.
Example Let's check the formula on something we know. Rotating the upper semicircle \(y = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\), \(-r \le x \le r\), about the \(x\)-axis gives a sphere. Here \(1 + (y^{\prime})^2 = \frac{r^2}{r^2 - x^2}\), so \(y\sqrt{1 + (y^{\prime})^2} = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\cdot\frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}} = r\), and \(S = 2\pi\int_{-r}^{r}r\,dx = 4\pi r^2\). That is exactly the surface area of a sphere.
Common mistake Using \(dx\) instead of \(ds\), as in \(\int 2\pi y\,dx\). That is the area of stacked cylinders, not of the slanted bands, and it is always too small (for a cone it gives \(\pi rh\) instead of \(\pi r\sqrt{r^2 + h^2}\)). Also, the radius is the distance to the axis of rotation: about the \(y\)-axis it is \(x\), not \(y\).
Key equations
\[ S = 2\pi\int_a^b y\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx\ \ (x\text{-axis}),\qquad S = 2\pi\int_c^d x\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dx}{dy}\right)^2}\,dy\ \ (y\text{-axis}) \]

8.5Probability

For our last application, let's go from geometry to chance. In continuous probability, a quantity can take any value in an interval, such as the waiting time for a jeepney, the height of a student, or the lifetime of a battery. Such a quantity is a continuous random variable \(X\). How do we compute the chance that \(X\) falls in some interval? With an integral, of course.

Probability density function In continuous probability, the probability density function (PDF) \(f(x)\) satisfies \[f(x) \ge 0\ \text{for all } x\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx = 1.\] The probability that a random variable \(X\) lies between \(a\) and \(b\) is the area under \(f\) from \(a\) to \(b\): \[P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^bf(x)\,dx \qquad (5)\] The expected value (mean) of \(X\) is its balance point: \[\mu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,f(x)\,dx \qquad (6)\]

The second condition says that the total probability is 1 (something must happen). For example, \(f(x) = kx(3 - x)\) on \([0,3]\) (and 0 elsewhere) is a PDF only if \(k\cdot\frac92 = 1\), that is \(k = \frac29\). Then \(P(1 \le X \le 2) = \frac29\int_1^2x(3 - x)\,dx = \frac{13}{27}\) (Figure 10.3). Notice also that \(P(X = a) = \int_a^af = 0\), so for continuous variables \(\le\) and \(\lt\) give the same probability. And \(f(x)\) itself is not a probability; it can even be bigger than 1.

The cumulative distribution function and the median. The cdf is \(F(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt\). It rises from 0 to 1, and by the Fundamental Theorem \(F'(x) = f(x)\). The median \(m\) splits the probability in half: \(F(m) = \frac12\).

Exponential distribution. Waiting times are often modelled by \(f(t) = \frac1\mu e^{-t/\mu}\) for \(t \ge 0\) (and 0 for \(t \lt 0\)), whose mean is \(\mu\). Recall from Module 8 that the integral over \([0, \infty)\) is improper, so we compute it with a limit. Then \(P(T \gt t) = e^{-t/\mu}\), and the median is \(m = \mu\ln 2 \approx 0.69\mu\). The median is less than the mean because a few very long waits pull the mean upward.

Normal distribution. Measurements such as heights or exam scores often follow the bell-shaped density \[f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)},\] with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\) (Figure 10.4). This is the \(e^{-x^2}\) type of integral from Module 8 with no elementary antiderivative, so its probabilities are found numerically or with a table. About 68% of the probability lies within one standard deviation of the mean, and about 95% within two.

Common mistake Before using a function as a PDF, check that its total integral is 1. Forgetting the normalizing constant \(k\) makes every probability wrong. And in the normal density, the denominator of the exponent is \(2\sigma^2\), not \(\sigma^2\).
Key equations
\[ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^bf(x)\,dx,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx = 1,\qquad \mu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f(x)\,dx \]
\[ F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt,\qquad F(m) = \tfrac12 \]
\[ f(t) = \tfrac1\mu e^{-t/\mu}\ (t \ge 0),\qquad f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} \]

★Key takeaways

  • Arc length adds up tiny hypotenuses: \(L = \int_a^b\sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\,dx\), or \(\int\sqrt{(dx/dt)^2 + (dy/dt)^2}\,dt\) for parametric curves.
  • Simplify \(1 + (dy/dx)^2\) first; good problems make it a perfect square or ready for a substitution.
  • The arc length function \(s(x) = \int_a^x\sqrt{1 + [f^{\prime}(t)]^2}\,dt\) measures the length from a fixed starting point.
  • Surface area adds up thin bands: \(S = \int 2\pi(\text{radius})\,ds\), where the radius is the distance to the axis of rotation.
  • A PDF is never negative and has total area 1; probabilities are areas \(\int_a^bf(x)\,dx\), and the mean is \(\int xf(x)\,dx\).
  • Exponential waiting times: \(P(T \gt t) = e^{-t/\mu}\); normal probabilities are computed numerically.

Example 10.1 Arc length with a substitution

Find the length of the curve \(y = \tfrac13x^{3/2}\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 4\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(y = \tfrac13x^{3/2}\) on \([0, 4]\).

Plan: Differentiate, substitute into equation (1), and evaluate the integral with a \(u\)-substitution (remembering to change the limits).

Step 1. Differentiate \(y\) using the power rule.

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac13\cdot\frac32x^{3/2 - 1} = \frac12x^{1/2}\]

Step 2. Square it and substitute into equation (1).

\[L = \int_0^4\sqrt{1 + \left(\tfrac12x^{1/2}\right)^2}\,dx = \int_0^4\sqrt{1 + \tfrac14x}\,dx\]

Step 3. Let \(u = 1 + \frac14x\), so \(du = \frac14dx\), or \(dx = 4\,du\). Change the limits: when \(x = 0\), \(u = 1\); when \(x = 4\), \(u = 2\).

\[L = 4\int_1^2u^{1/2}\,du = 4\cdot\frac23\Big[u^{3/2}\Big]_1^2\]

Step 4. Evaluate with \(F(b) - F(a)\).

\[L = \frac83\left(2^{3/2} - 1^{3/2}\right) = \frac83\left(2\sqrt2 - 1\right) \approx 4.876\]

Answer: \(\boxed{L = \tfrac83\left(2\sqrt2 - 1\right) \approx 4.88\text{ units}}\). That is our answer.

Check: The straight line from \((0, 0)\) to \((4, \tfrac83)\) has length \(\sqrt{16 + 64/9} \approx 4.807\). The curve must be a little longer than the straight line, and \(4.876 \gt 4.807\). ✓

Example 10.2 When the root becomes a secant

Find the length of \(y = \ln(\cos x)\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = \frac\pi4\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(y = \ln(\cos x)\) on \([0, \frac\pi4]\).

Plan: Differentiate with the chain rule; then the identity \(1 + \tan^2x = \sec^2x\) removes the square root.

Step 1. Recall the chain rule: \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln(u) = \frac{u^{\prime}}{u}\).

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-\sin x}{\cos x} = -\tan x\]

Step 2. Substitute into equation (1) and simplify. On \([0, \frac\pi4]\), \(\sec x \gt 0\), so \(\sqrt{\sec^2x} = \sec x\).

\[L = \int_0^{\pi/4}\sqrt{1 + \tan^2x}\,dx = \int_0^{\pi/4}\sqrt{\sec^2x}\,dx = \int_0^{\pi/4}\sec x\,dx\]

Step 3. Use \(\int\sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x|\) (Module 6) and apply the limits.

\[L = \Big[\ln|\sec x + \tan x|\Big]_0^{\pi/4} = \ln\left(\sqrt2 + 1\right) - \ln(1 + 0)\]

Step 4. Since \(\ln 1 = 0\), the second term is zero.

\[L = \ln\left(\sqrt2 + 1\right) \approx 0.881\]

Answer: \(\boxed{L = \ln\left(1 + \sqrt2\right) \approx 0.88\text{ units}}\).

Check: The endpoints are \((0, 0)\) and \(\left(\frac\pi4, \ln\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right) \approx (0.785, -0.347)\); the straight-line distance is \(\approx 0.858\), slightly less than 0.881. ✓

Example 10.3 Length of a parametric curve

Find the length of the curve \(x = t^2\), \(y = t^3\), \(0 \le t \le 2\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: A parametric curve with \(x = t^2\), \(y = t^3\) on \([0, 2]\).

Plan: Use equation (2). With the same steps as before, differentiate first, then factor out \(t^2\) from the root and use a \(u\)-substitution.

Step 1. Differentiate each coordinate.

\[\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t,\qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2\]

Step 2. Substitute into equation (2).

\[L = \int_0^2\sqrt{(2t)^2 + (3t^2)^2}\,dt = \int_0^2\sqrt{4t^2 + 9t^4}\,dt\]

Step 3. Factor \(t^2\) out of the root. Since \(t \ge 0\), \(\sqrt{t^2} = t\).

\[L = \int_0^2t\sqrt{4 + 9t^2}\,dt\]

Step 4. Let \(u = 4 + 9t^2\), \(du = 18t\,dt\), so \(t\,dt = \frac1{18}du\). When \(t = 0\), \(u = 4\); when \(t = 2\), \(u = 40\).

\[L = \frac1{18}\int_4^{40}u^{1/2}\,du = \frac1{18}\cdot\frac23\Big[u^{3/2}\Big]_4^{40} = \frac1{27}\left(40^{3/2} - 4^{3/2}\right)\]

Step 5. Compute: \(4^{3/2} = 8\) and \(40^{3/2} = 40\sqrt{40} \approx 252.98\).

\[L = \frac{40\sqrt{40} - 8}{27} \approx 9.073\]

Answer: \(\boxed{L = \tfrac1{27}\left(80\sqrt{10} - 8\right) \approx 9.07\text{ units}}\).

Check: The chord from \((0, 0)\) to \((4, 8)\) has length \(\sqrt{80} \approx 8.94\), just below 9.07. ✓

Example 10.4 Surface area about the \(x\)-axis

Find the surface area of the solid obtained by rotating \(y = \sqrt x\), \(0 \le x \le 4\), about the \(x\)-axis.

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(y = \sqrt x\) on \([0, 4]\), rotated about the \(x\)-axis, so the radius is \(y = \sqrt x\).

Plan: Use equation (3). The \(\sqrt x\) outside the root will cancel with a \(\sqrt x\) in the denominator inside, then a \(u\)-substitution finishes it.

Step 1. Differentiate and simplify \(1 + (dy/dx)^2\).

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt x},\qquad 1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2 = 1 + \frac{1}{4x} = \frac{4x + 1}{4x}\]

Step 2. Substitute into equation (3). Since \(\sqrt{\frac{4x+1}{4x}} = \frac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt x}\), the \(\sqrt x\) cancels.

\[S = 2\pi\int_0^4\sqrt x\cdot\frac{\sqrt{4x + 1}}{2\sqrt x}\,dx = \pi\int_0^4\sqrt{4x + 1}\,dx\]

Step 3. Let \(u = 4x + 1\), \(du = 4\,dx\), so \(dx = \frac14du\). When \(x = 0\), \(u = 1\); when \(x = 4\), \(u = 17\).

\[S = \frac\pi4\int_1^{17}u^{1/2}\,du = \frac\pi4\cdot\frac23\Big[u^{3/2}\Big]_1^{17} = \frac\pi6\left(17^{3/2} - 1\right)\]

Step 4. Compute: \(17^{3/2} = 17\sqrt{17} \approx 70.09\).

\[S = \frac\pi6\left(17\sqrt{17} - 1\right) \approx \frac\pi6(69.09) \approx 36.18\]

Answer: \(\boxed{S = \tfrac\pi6\left(17\sqrt{17} - 1\right) \approx 36.18\text{ square units}}\). Take note: do not forget the factor \(\pi\) at the end.

Check: The surface is wider than a cone with the same ends (radius 2 at \(x = 4\)), whose lateral area is \(\pi(2)\sqrt{4 + 16} \approx 28.1\). A bulging surface should have more area: \(36.18 \gt 28.1\). ✓

Example 10.5 A probability density on [0, 1]

Let \(f(x) = 2x\) for \(0 \le x \le 1\) (and 0 elsewhere). Show that \(f\) is a PDF, then find (a) \(P(0.25 \le X \le 0.75)\) and (b) the mean \(\mu\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = 2x\) on \([0, 1]\), zero outside.

Plan: Check the two PDF conditions, then use equations (5) and (6).

Step 1. Check the PDF conditions: \(2x \ge 0\) on \([0, 1]\), and the total area is 1.

\[\int_0^1 2x\,dx = \Big[x^2\Big]_0^1 = 1\]

Step 2. (a) Substitute into equation (5).

\[P(0.25 \le X \le 0.75) = \int_{0.25}^{0.75}2x\,dx = \Big[x^2\Big]_{0.25}^{0.75} = 0.5625 - 0.0625 = 0.5\]

Step 3. (b) Substitute into equation (6).

\[\mu = \int_0^1x\cdot2x\,dx = \int_0^12x^2\,dx = \frac23\Big[x^3\Big]_0^1 = \frac23\]

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{0.5}\) and (b) \(\boxed{\mu = \tfrac23}\).

Check: The density grows with \(x\), so larger values are more likely and the mean should be above the midpoint \(\frac12\): \(\frac23 \gt \frac12\). ✓

Example 10.6 An exponential waiting time

Let \(f(x) = \frac15e^{-x/5}\) for \(x \ge 0\) (and 0 for \(x \lt 0\)). Find (a) \(P(X \gt 3)\) and (b) the mean \(\mu\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: An exponential density with parameter 5.

Plan: Both parts are improper integrals (Module 8), so write them as limits. Part (b) also needs integration by parts.

Step 1. (a) Write \(P(X \gt 3)\) as an improper integral.

\[P(X \gt 3) = \int_3^\infty\tfrac15e^{-x/5}\,dx = \lim_{b\to\infty}\Big[-e^{-x/5}\Big]_3^b\]

Step 2. Evaluate and take the limit; \(e^{-b/5}\to0\).

\[= \lim_{b\to\infty}\left(-e^{-b/5} + e^{-3/5}\right) = e^{-3/5} \approx 0.549\]

Step 3. (b) Set up the mean and integrate by parts with \(u = x\), \(dv = \frac15e^{-x/5}dx\), so \(du = dx\), \(v = -e^{-x/5}\).

\[\int_0^bx\cdot\tfrac15e^{-x/5}\,dx = \Big[-xe^{-x/5}\Big]_0^b + \int_0^be^{-x/5}\,dx = -be^{-b/5} + 5\left(1 - e^{-b/5}\right)\]

Step 4. Take the limit. An exponential beats any power, so \(be^{-b/5}\to0\).

\[\mu = \lim_{b\to\infty}\left[-be^{-b/5} + 5 - 5e^{-b/5}\right] = 5\]

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{P(X \gt 3) = e^{-3/5} \approx 0.549}\) and (b) \(\boxed{\mu = 5}\).

Check: For an exponential density \(\frac1\mu e^{-t/\mu}\), we expect \(P(T \gt t) = e^{-t/\mu}\) and mean \(\mu\); here \(\mu = 5\) and \(t = 3\). ✓
