5.1Areas Between Curves

In Chapter 4 the definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) measured the area under one curve. Now you will measure the area of a region trapped between two curves. The idea is the same as before: slice the region into thin vertical rectangles, add up their areas, and take a limit (Stewart §5.1).

Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous and \(f(x) \ge g(x)\) on \([a,b]\). A thin vertical rectangle at position \(x\) has height \(f(x) - g(x)\) (top minus bottom) and width \(\Delta x\). Adding these and letting \(n \to \infty\) gives

\[A = \int_a^b \bigl[f(x) - g(x)\bigr]\,dx.\]

Notice that this works even when part of the region is below the \(x\)-axis: the height of each strip is always top minus bottom, and that difference is positive wherever \(f \ge g\).

Finding the limits. Often the problem does not give \(a\) and \(b\); the region is the one enclosed by the curves. Then you set the two formulas equal and solve. For \(y = 2x + 3\) and \(y = x^2\), solving \(x^2 = 2x + 3\) gives \(x = -1\) and \(x = 3\). Testing \(x = 0\) shows the line is on top (\(3 \gt 0\)), so

\[A = \int_{-1}^{3} \bigl(2x + 3 - x^2\bigr)\,dx = \Bigl[x^2 + 3x - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\Bigr]_{-1}^{3} = 9 - \Bigl(-\tfrac{5}{3}\Bigr) = \tfrac{32}{3}.\]

When the curves cross. If the curves swap places inside \([a,b]\), the area is \(\int_a^b |f(x) - g(x)|\,dx\). In practice you find the crossing points, split the interval there, and on each piece integrate (top − bottom). For \(y = \sin x\) and \(y = \cos x\) on \([0, \pi]\), they cross at \(x = \pi/4\), so the area is \(\int_0^{\pi/4} (\cos x - \sin x)\,dx + \int_{\pi/4}^{\pi} (\sin x - \cos x)\,dx = (\sqrt2 - 1) + (\sqrt2 + 1) = 2\sqrt2\).

Common mistake Writing \(\int_0^{\pi}(\sin x - \cos x)\,dx\) without splitting gives \(2\), not \(2\sqrt2\): the negative part cancels some of the positive part. Area is never negative, so always check which curve is on top on each piece.

Integrating with respect to \(y\). Some regions are bounded by curves of the form \(x = f(y)\), or have a boundary that changes formula partway along \(x\). Then it is easier to use horizontal strips. A horizontal strip has length \(x_R - x_L\) (right minus left) and thickness \(\Delta y\), so

\[A = \int_c^d \bigl[x_R(y) - x_L(y)\bigr]\,dy.\]

For the region between \(x = y^2\) and \(x = y + 2\), solve \(y^2 = y + 2\) to get \(y = -1\) and \(y = 2\). The line is on the right, so \(A = \int_{-1}^{2} (y + 2 - y^2)\,dy = \tfrac{9}{2}\). With vertical strips you would need two integrals, because the lower boundary changes from the parabola's lower half to the line at \(x = 1\).

A good habit for every area problem: sketch first, decide whether vertical or horizontal strips give a single formula, then find the limits.

Key equations
\[ A = \int_a^b \bigl[f(x) - g(x)\bigr]\,dx \quad (f \ge g) \]
\[ A = \int_a^b \lvert f(x) - g(x)\rvert\,dx \]
\[ A = \int_c^d \bigl[x_R(y) - x_L(y)\bigr]\,dy \]

5.2Volumes

The same slicing idea measures volume (Stewart §5.2). Cut a solid by planes perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis. If the slice at position \(x\) has cross-sectional area \(A(x)\), then a thin slab has volume about \(A(x)\,\Delta x\). Adding the slabs and taking the limit gives

\[V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx.\]

This works for any solid whose cross-sections you can describe. For example, if the base of a solid is the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\) and every cross-section perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis is a square, the side of the square at \(x\) is the chord length \(2\sqrt{4 - x^2}\), so \(A(x) = 4(4 - x^2)\) and \(V = \int_{-2}^{2} 4(4 - x^2)\,dx = \tfrac{128}{3}\).

Solids of revolution: disks. When a region is rotated about a line, every cross-section perpendicular to that line is a circle. If the region under \(y = f(x)\) on \([a,b]\) is rotated about the \(x\)-axis, the slice at \(x\) is a disk of radius \(f(x)\), so \(A(x) = \pi [f(x)]^2\) and

\[V = \int_a^b \pi [f(x)]^2\,dx.\]

Rotating the region under \(y = \sqrt{x}\), \(0 \le x \le 4\), about the \(x\)-axis gives \(V = \int_0^4 \pi x\,dx = 8\pi\).

Washers. If the region lies between two curves and does not touch the axis, each slice is a disk with a hole: a washer. With outer radius \(R\) and inner radius \(r\), its area is \(\pi R^2 - \pi r^2\). For the region between \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\) (\(0 \le x \le 1\)) rotated about the \(x\)-axis, the line is farther from the axis, so \(R = x\), \(r = x^2\), and \(V = \pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^4)\,dx = \tfrac{2\pi}{15}\).

Common mistake The washer area is \(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\), not \(\pi(R - r)^2\). Square each radius first, then subtract.

Other axes. A radius is always a distance from the axis of rotation. If the same region is rotated about the line \(y = -1\), every distance grows by 1: \(R = x + 1\) and \(r = x^2 + 1\). Then \(V = \pi\int_0^1 \bigl[(x+1)^2 - (x^2+1)^2\bigr]\,dx = \tfrac{7\pi}{15}\). For a horizontal axis \(y = k\), a radius is \(|y - k|\); for a vertical axis \(x = h\), slice perpendicular to it (strips of thickness \(dy\)) and use radii \(|x - h|\), written in terms of \(y\).

Note A reliable recipe: (1) sketch the region and the axis; (2) draw one strip perpendicular to the axis; (3) rotate it in your mind and decide disk or washer; (4) write \(R\) and \(r\) as distances to the axis; (5) integrate in the variable along the axis.
Key equations
\[ V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx \]
\[ V = \int_a^b \pi [f(x)]^2\,dx \quad \text{(disks)} \]
\[ V = \int_a^b \pi\bigl(R^2 - r^2\bigr)\,dx \quad \text{(washers)} \]

★Key takeaways

  • Area between curves is \(\int (\text{top} - \text{bottom})\,dx\) or \(\int (\text{right} - \text{left})\,dy\); the integrand is always a positive length.
  • Set the curves equal to find where they meet; those intersections are usually your limits of integration.
  • If the curves cross inside the interval, split there so that you always subtract the lower curve from the upper one.
  • Volume by slicing is \(V = \int A(x)\,dx\), where \(A(x)\) is the area of the cross-section at \(x\).
  • Disks use \(\pi R^2\) and washers use \(\pi(R^2 - r^2)\); every radius is a distance from the axis of rotation.
  • For a vertical axis of rotation, slice horizontally and integrate in \(y\).

Example 4.1 Area enclosed by a parabola and a line

Find the area of the region enclosed by \(y = x^2 - 4x\) and \(y = 2x\).

(Similar to Stewart §5.1, Example 2)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

The region is enclosed, so the limits are the intersection points. Find them, decide which curve is on top, then integrate (top − bottom) with respect to \(x\).

Find the intersections:

\(x^2 - 4x = 2x \Rightarrow x^2 - 6x = 0 \Rightarrow x(x - 6) = 0\), so \(x = 0\) and \(x = 6\). At \(x = 1\): the line gives \(2\), the parabola gives \(-3\). The line is on top.

Solution:

\[A = \int_0^6 \bigl[2x - (x^2 - 4x)\bigr]\,dx = \int_0^6 (6x - x^2)\,dx = \Bigl[3x^2 - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\Bigr]_0^6 = 108 - 72 = 36.\]

Check:

The answer is positive, as an area must be. Part of the region lies below the \(x\)-axis (the parabola dips to \(-4\) at \(x = 2\)), but (top − bottom) handles that automatically. A rough check: the region is about 6 wide and at most 9 tall (at \(x = 3\)), and a parabolic segment fills \(2/3\) of its bounding box: \(\tfrac23 \cdot 6 \cdot 9 = 36\).

Example 4.2 Choosing horizontal strips

Find the area of the region in the first quadrant bounded by \(y = \sqrt{x}\), \(y = x - 6\) and the \(x\)-axis.

(Similar to Stewart §5.1, Example 6)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Sketch it. With vertical strips the bottom boundary is the \(x\)-axis for \(0 \le x \le 6\) but the line for \(6 \le x \le 9\), so you would need two integrals. With horizontal strips the left boundary is always the curve and the right boundary is always the line, so one integral suffices.

Rewrite the curves in terms of y:

\(y = \sqrt{x} \Rightarrow x = y^2\) (left), and \(y = x - 6 \Rightarrow x = y + 6\) (right). They meet where \(y^2 = y + 6\), i.e. \((y - 3)(y + 2) = 0\); in the first quadrant \(y = 3\). The bottom of the region is \(y = 0\).

Solution:

\[A = \int_0^3 \bigl[(y + 6) - y^2\bigr]\,dy = \Bigl[\tfrac{y^2}{2} + 6y - \tfrac{y^3}{3}\Bigr]_0^3 = \tfrac92 + 18 - 9 = \tfrac{27}{2}.\]

Check:

Doing it the long way with vertical strips: \(\int_0^6 \sqrt{x}\,dx + \int_6^9 \bigl[\sqrt{x} - (x - 6)\bigr]\,dx\) also equals \(13.5\). Same answer, twice the work.

Example 4.3 Washers about a vertical line

The region bounded by \(y = x^2\) and \(y = 2x\) is rotated about the line \(x = -1\). Find the volume of the solid.

(Similar to Stewart §5.2, Example 6)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

The axis is vertical, so slice perpendicular to it: horizontal strips of thickness \(dy\). Each strip sweeps out a washer. Write both curves as \(x\) in terms of \(y\) and measure the radii from \(x = -1\).

Set up:

The curves meet at \((0,0)\) and \((2,4)\), so \(0 \le y \le 4\). The line is \(x = y/2\) and the parabola is \(x = \sqrt{y}\). For \(0 < y < 4\), \(\sqrt{y} > y/2\), so the parabola is farther from the axis.

Outer radius \(R = \sqrt{y} - (-1) = \sqrt{y} + 1\); inner radius \(r = \tfrac{y}{2} + 1\).

Solution:

\[V = \pi\int_0^4 \Bigl[(\sqrt{y} + 1)^2 - \bigl(\tfrac{y}{2} + 1\bigr)^2\Bigr]dy = \pi\int_0^4 \Bigl(2\sqrt{y} - \tfrac{y^2}{4}\Bigr)dy.\]

(Expand: \(y + 2\sqrt y + 1 - \tfrac{y^2}{4} - y - 1\).) Then \(V = \pi\Bigl[\tfrac43 y^{3/2} - \tfrac{y^3}{12}\Bigr]_0^4 = \pi\Bigl(\tfrac{32}{3} - \tfrac{16}{3}\Bigr) = \tfrac{16\pi}{3}\).

Check:

In Module 5 you will meet cylindrical shells, which give \(2\pi\int_0^2 (x + 1)(2x - x^2)\,dx = \tfrac{16\pi}{3}\) too. The common error here is using \(R = \sqrt{y}\) and \(r = y/2\), which measures from the \(y\)-axis instead of from \(x = -1\).
