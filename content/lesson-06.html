7.1Integration by Parts

Recall the Product Rule: \((fg)' = f'g + fg'\). Every differentiation rule gives us an integration rule. The Chain Rule gave us substitution, and the Product Rule gives us integration by parts. If we integrate both sides and rearrange, the formula for integration by parts is given by

\[\int f(x)g'(x)\,dx = f(x)g(x) - \int g(x)f'(x)\,dx. \qquad (1)\]

Now let \(u = f(x)\) and \(v = g(x)\). Their differentials are \(du = f'(x)\,dx\) and \(dv = g'(x)\,dx\). Therefore, equation (1) becomes the short form that we will use all the time (Stewart §7.1):

\[\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du. \qquad (2)\]

So, what does the formula really do? It trades the integral \(\int u\,dv\) for a new integral \(\int v\,du\). The trade is worth it only if the new integral is simpler. Let's try \(\int x e^{2x}\,dx\) with \(u = x\) and \(dv = e^{2x}\,dx\). Then \(du = dx\) and \(v = \tfrac12 e^{2x}\). Using equation (2), our integral becomes

\[\int x e^{2x}\,dx = \tfrac12 x e^{2x} - \int \tfrac12 e^{2x}\,dx = \tfrac12 x e^{2x} - \tfrac14 e^{2x} + C.\]

Notice that the \(x\) is gone from the new integral. That is exactly what we wanted.

LIATE In choosing your \(u\), you need to remember the LIATE acronym: Logarithmic function, Inverse (trigonometric) function, Algebraic function, Trigonometric function, Exponential function. Whichever factor comes first in the list becomes \(u\); everything else is \(dv\).

In \(\int xe^{2x}\,dx\) we have an \(x\), which is an algebraic function, and \(e^{2x}\), which is exponential. A comes before E, hence we let \(u = x\). Why does LIATE work? Functions near the top get simpler when we differentiate them (\(\ln x\) becomes \(\tfrac1x\)), while functions near the bottom are easy to integrate. Take note that LIATE is a guide, not a law. In \(\int \ln x\,dx\) there is only one factor, so we let \(u = \ln x\) and \(dv = dx\), and we get \(x\ln x - x + C\). Figure 6.2 shows that \(\int_1^e \ln x\,dx = 1\).

Repeated parts and the tabular method. For \(\int x^2 e^{x}\,dx\), one round of parts leaves \(\int 2xe^x\,dx\), so we use parts again. A neat way to keep track is the tabular method: list \(u\) and its derivatives (\(x^2,\ 2x,\ 2,\ 0\)) in one column, \(dv\) and its antiderivatives (\(e^x,\ e^x,\ e^x,\ e^x\)) in another, then multiply along the diagonals with the signs \(+,-,+\). We get \(\int x^2e^x\,dx = e^x(x^2 - 2x + 2) + C\). It works whenever \(u\) is a polynomial that eventually becomes 0.

When the integral comes back. For \(\int e^x\sin x\,dx\), two rounds of parts give back the original integral \(I\): \(I = e^x\sin x - e^x\cos x - I\). Does that mean we are going in circles? No. We simply solve for \(I\): \(I = \tfrac12 e^x(\sin x - \cos x) + C\).

Definite integrals and reduction formulas. In a definite integral, evaluate the \(uv\) part between the limits too: \(\int_a^b u\,dv = \big[uv\big]_a^b - \int_a^b v\,du\). For example, \(\int_0^2 xe^{-x}\,dx = \big[-xe^{-x}\big]_0^2 + \int_0^2 e^{-x}\,dx = 1 - 3e^{-2}\) (Figure 6.1). Sometimes parts lowers a power by one and gives a formula we can use again and again. With \(u = (\ln x)^n\) and \(dv = dx\), \(\int(\ln x)^n\,dx = x(\ln x)^n - n\int(\ln x)^{n-1}\,dx\). In the same way, \(\int\sin^n x\,dx = -\tfrac1n\cos x\sin^{n-1}x + \tfrac{n-1}{n}\int\sin^{n-2}x\,dx\), which we use in one of the worked examples below.

Common mistake Forgetting the minus sign in \(uv - \int v\,du\), or forgetting to evaluate \(uv\) at the limits in a definite integral.
Key equations
\[ \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du \]
\[ \int_a^b f(x)g'(x)\,dx = \Big[f(x)g(x)\Big]_a^b - \int_a^b g(x)f'(x)\,dx \]
\[ \int (\ln x)^n\,dx = x(\ln x)^n - n\int (\ln x)^{n-1}\,dx \]
\[ \int \sin^n x\,dx = -\frac{1}{n}\cos x\sin^{n-1}x + \frac{n-1}{n}\int \sin^{n-2}x\,dx \]

7.2Trigonometric Integrals

In this section (Stewart §7.2) we integrate products of powers of trigonometric functions. The plan is always the same: use an identity so that the integrand becomes a polynomial in one function times that function's derivative, and then substitute. Let's start with the strategy for evaluating \(\int\sin^m x\cos^n x\,dx\).

(a) If the power of cosine is odd \((n = 2k+1)\), save one cosine factor and use \(\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x\) to express the remaining factors in terms of sine:

\[\int\sin^m x\cos^{2k+1}x\,dx = \int\sin^m x\,(1-\sin^2 x)^k\cos x\,dx. \qquad (3)\]

Then substitute \(u = \sin x\). The worked example on \(\int\cos^5 x\sin^2 x\,dx\) below shows every step.

(b) If the power of sine is odd \((m = 2k+1)\), save one sine factor and use \(\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x\) to express the remaining factors in terms of cosine:

\[\int\sin^{2k+1}x\cos^n x\,dx = \int(1-\cos^2 x)^k\cos^n x\,\sin x\,dx. \qquad (4)\]

Then substitute \(u = \cos x\). Take note that \(du = -\sin x\,dx\), so a minus sign appears. For example, \(\int\sin^5 x\cos^2 x\,dx\) is almost the same as the cosine example, but the exponents swap: \(\int(1-\cos^2 x)^2\cos^2 x\,\sin x\,dx = -\int(1-u^2)^2u^2\,du = -\tfrac13\cos^3 x + \tfrac25\cos^5 x - \tfrac17\cos^7 x + C\). In the same way, \(\int_0^{\pi}\sin^3 x\,dx = \tfrac43\) (Figure 6.3). If the powers of both sine and cosine are odd, either (a) or (b) can be used.

(c) If the powers of both sine and cosine are even, there is no spare factor, so we use the half-angle identities

\[\sin^2 x = \tfrac12(1-\cos 2x), \qquad \cos^2 x = \tfrac12(1+\cos 2x). \qquad (5)\]

It is sometimes helpful to use the identity \(\sin x\cos x = \tfrac12\sin 2x\) (6). For \(\int\sin^2 x\,dx\), rewriting it as \(\int(1-\cos^2 x)\,dx\) will not help us at all. However, if we use the half-angle identity, then \(\int\sin^2 x\,dx = \tfrac12\int(1-\cos 2x)\,dx = \tfrac12x - \tfrac14\sin 2x + C\). Figure 6.4 shows that \(\sin^2 x\) and \(\tfrac12(1-\cos 2x)\) really are the same curve.

Now, the strategy for evaluating \(\int\tan^m x\sec^n x\,dx\). Here we use \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^2 x\), \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\sec x = \sec x\tan x\) and \(\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1\).

(a) If the power of secant is even \((n = 2k,\ k \ge 2)\), save a factor of \(\sec^2 x\) and use \(\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x\) for the rest: \(\int\tan^m x\sec^{2k}x\,dx = \int\tan^m x\,(1+\tan^2 x)^{k-1}\sec^2 x\,dx\). Then substitute \(u = \tan x\).

(b) If the power of tangent is odd \((m = 2k+1)\), save a factor of \(\sec x\tan x\) and use \(\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1\):

\[\int\tan^{2k+1}x\sec^n x\,dx = \int(\sec^2 x - 1)^k\sec^{n-1}x\,\sec x\tan x\,dx. \qquad (7)\]

Then substitute \(u = \sec x\), \(du = \sec x\tan x\,dx\). For example, \(\int\tan^3 x\sec x\,dx = \int(\sec^2 x - 1)\sec x\tan x\,dx = \int(u^2-1)\,du = \tfrac13\sec^3 x - \sec x + C\).

Some important notes:

  • \(\int\tan x\,dx = \ln|\sec x| + C\) and \(\int\sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + C\).
  • If neither pattern fits, use \(\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1\) to peel off pieces: \(\int\tan^2 x\,dx = \tan x - x + C\).
  • For different angles, use the product-to-sum identities \(\sin A\cos B = \tfrac12[\sin(A-B)+\sin(A+B)]\), \(\sin A\sin B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B)]\) and \(\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B)]\). So \(\int\sin 5x\cos 2x\,dx = \tfrac12\int(\sin 3x + \sin 7x)\,dx = -\tfrac16\cos 3x - \tfrac1{14}\cos 7x + C\).
Common mistake Writing \(\int\sin^2 x\,dx = \tfrac13\sin^3 x + C\). That would need a \(\cos x\) factor that is not there. Use the half-angle identity instead.
Key equations
\[ \sin^2 x = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2x), \qquad \cos^2 x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x) \]
\[ \sin x\cos x = \tfrac12\sin 2x \]
\[ \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1 \]
\[ \int \tan x\,dx = \ln|\sec x| + C, \qquad \int \sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + C \]
\[ \sin A\cos B = \tfrac12[\sin(A-B) + \sin(A+B)] \]

★Key takeaways

  • Integration by parts, ∫u dv = uv − ∫v du, is the Product Rule in reverse; it is worth using only when the new integral ∫v du is simpler.
  • To choose u, remember LIATE: Logarithmic, Inverse, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential. The first one on the list is u.
  • If parts must be repeated, the tabular method keeps the work organised; if the original integral comes back, solve for it.
  • For ∫sin^m x cos^n x dx, save one factor of the odd power and substitute; if both powers are even, use the half-angle identities.
  • For ∫tan^m x sec^n x dx, save sec²x when the power of secant is even, or save sec x tan x when the power of tangent is odd.
  • Take note of ∫tan x dx = ln|sec x| + C and ∫sec x dx = ln|sec x + tan x| + C, and use product-to-sum identities for different angles.

Example 6.1 An algebraic times a trigonometric function

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int x\cos 5x\,dx\).

(Instr. Vitalez's notes, Integration by Parts, Example 2)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • The integrand \(x\cos 5x\) is a product: \(x\) is algebraic, \(\cos 5x\) is trigonometric.
  • Find: \(\int x\cos 5x\,dx\).
Step 1: Choose u and dv with LIATE

A comes before T, so we let \(u\) be the algebraic part and \(dv\) the rest.

\[u = x \quad\Rightarrow\quad du = dx\]

\[dv = \cos 5x\,dx\]

Step 2: Find v

To integrate \(\cos 5x\), let \(t = 5x\), so \(dt = 5\,dx\) and \(dx = \tfrac15\,dt\).

\[v = \int\cos 5x\,dx = \frac15\int\cos t\,dt\]

\[v = \frac15\sin t = \frac15\sin 5x\]

Step 3: Substitute into equation (2)

Use \(\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du\).

\[\int x\cos 5x\,dx = x\cdot\frac15\sin 5x - \int\frac15\sin 5x\,dx\]

\[= \frac{x}{5}\sin 5x - \frac15\int\sin 5x\,dx\]

Step 4: Integrate the new integral

Following the same process as in Step 2, \(\int\sin 5x\,dx = -\tfrac15\cos 5x\).

\[-\frac15\int\sin 5x\,dx = -\frac15\left(-\frac15\cos 5x\right)\]

\[= \frac1{25}\cos 5x\]

Answer

\[\int x\cos 5x\,dx = \frac{x}{5}\sin 5x + \frac1{25}\cos 5x + C\]

Check

Differentiate: \(\tfrac15\sin 5x + x\cos 5x - \tfrac{5}{25}\sin 5x = x\cos 5x\). That is our integrand.

Example 6.2 A logarithm times a power

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int t^4\ln t\,dt\).

(Instr. Vitalez's notes, Integration by Parts, Example 3)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • The integrand is \(t^4\ln t\): \(\ln t\) is logarithmic, \(t^4\) is algebraic.
  • Find: \(\int t^4\ln t\,dt\).
Step 1: Choose u and dv with LIATE

L comes before A, so the logarithm is \(u\). (We could not choose \(dv = \ln t\,dt\) anyway, because we do not yet know its antiderivative.)

\[u = \ln t \quad\Rightarrow\quad du = \frac1t\,dt\]

\[dv = t^4\,dt \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \frac15 t^5\]

Step 2: Substitute into equation (2)

\[\int t^4\ln t\,dt = \frac15 t^5\ln t - \int\frac15 t^5\cdot\frac1t\,dt\]

Step 3: Simplify the new integrand

Since \(t^5\cdot\frac1t = t^4\):

\[= \frac15 t^5\ln t - \frac15\int t^4\,dt\]

Step 4: Integrate

\[\frac15\int t^4\,dt = \frac15\cdot\frac{t^5}{5} = \frac{t^5}{25}\]

Answer

So, the answer would be

\[\int t^4\ln t\,dt = \frac15 t^5\ln t - \frac1{25}t^5 + C\]

Check

Differentiate: \(t^4\ln t + \frac15 t^5\cdot\frac1t - \frac{5t^4}{25} = t^4\ln t + \frac{t^4}{5} - \frac{t^4}{5} = t^4\ln t\).

Example 6.3 Parts, then long division

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int x\ln(1+x)\,dx\) (for \(x \gt -1\)).

(Instr. Vitalez's notes, Integration by Parts, Problem Set No. 1)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • The integrand is \(x\ln(1+x)\): a logarithm times an algebraic function.
  • \(x \gt -1\), so \(\ln(1+x)\) is defined and needs no absolute value.
  • Find: \(\int x\ln(1+x)\,dx\).
Step 1: Choose u and dv with LIATE

L comes before A, so \(u = \ln(1+x)\).

\[u = \ln(1+x) \quad\Rightarrow\quad du = \frac{1}{1+x}\,dx\]

\[dv = x\,dx \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \frac{x^2}{2}\]

Step 2: Substitute into equation (2)

\[\int x\ln(1+x)\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2}\ln(1+x) - \int\frac{x^2}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{1+x}\,dx\]

\[= \frac{x^2}{2}\ln(1+x) - \frac12\int\frac{x^2}{1+x}\,dx\]

Step 3: Divide, because the new fraction is improper

The degree of the numerator is bigger than the degree of the denominator, so we divide first. Since \(x^2 = (x-1)(x+1) + 1\):

\[\frac{x^2}{1+x} = x - 1 + \frac{1}{1+x}\]

Step 4: Integrate the pieces

\[\int\left(x - 1 + \frac{1}{1+x}\right)dx = \frac{x^2}{2} - x + \ln(1+x)\]

Step 5: Put everything together

Multiply the result of Step 4 by \(-\tfrac12\) and add it to the first term.

\[\frac{x^2}{2}\ln(1+x) - \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{x}{2} - \frac12\ln(1+x) + C\]

Combine the two logarithm terms:

\[= \frac12(x^2-1)\ln(1+x) - \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{x}{2} + C\]

Answer

\[\int x\ln(1+x)\,dx = \frac12(x^2-1)\ln(1+x) - \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{x}{2} + C\]

Check

Differentiate: \(x\ln(1+x) + \frac{x^2-1}{2(1+x)} - \frac{x}{2} + \frac12 = x\ln(1+x) + \frac{x-1}{2} - \frac{x}{2} + \frac12 = x\ln(1+x)\).

Example 6.4 Using the reduction formula for sine

Using \(\displaystyle\int\sin^n x\,dx = -\frac1n\cos x\sin^{n-1}x + \frac{n-1}{n}\int\sin^{n-2}x\,dx\), show that \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,dx = \frac{n-1}{n}\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{n-2}x\,dx\) for integers \(n \ge 2\). Then evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^5 x\,dx\).

(Instr. Vitalez's notes, Integration by Parts, Problem Set Nos. 2 and 3)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • The reduction formula for \(\int\sin^n x\,dx\) (it comes from parts with \(u = \sin^{n-1}x\), \(dv = \sin x\,dx\)).
  • Show: the definite version on \([0, \pi/2]\).
  • Find: \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^5 x\,dx\).
Step 1: Evaluate the boundary term

Put the limits on the first term of the formula. At \(x = \pi/2\), \(\cos x = 0\). At \(x = 0\), \(\sin x = 0\) (and \(n - 1 \ge 1\)).

\[\Big[-\frac1n\cos x\sin^{n-1}x\Big]_0^{\pi/2} = 0 - 0 = 0\]

So only the integral part survives:

\[\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,dx = \frac{n-1}{n}\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{n-2}x\,dx\]

Step 2: Use the formula with n = 5

\[\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^5 x\,dx = \frac45\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^3 x\,dx\]

Step 3: Use the formula again with n = 3

\[\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^3 x\,dx = \frac23\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\,dx\]

Step 4: Evaluate the last integral

\[\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\,dx = \Big[-\cos x\Big]_0^{\pi/2} = 0 - (-1) = 1\]

So \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^3 x\,dx = \tfrac23\).

Step 5: Multiply the factors

\[\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^5 x\,dx = \frac45\cdot\frac23\cdot 1 = \frac{8}{15}\]

Answer

\(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^5 x\,dx = \frac{8}{15} \approx 0.533\).

Check

The answer follows the pattern for odd powers, \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2n+1}x\,dx = \frac{2\cdot4\cdot6\cdots(2n)}{3\cdot5\cdot7\cdots(2n+1)}\): with \(n = 2\) this is \(\frac{2\cdot4}{3\cdot5} = \frac{8}{15}\). It is also less than \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^3x\,dx = \tfrac23\), as it should be, since \(\sin^5 x \le \sin^3 x\) on \([0, \pi/2]\).

Example 6.5 An odd power of cosine

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int\cos^5 x\sin^2 x\,dx\).

(Instr. Vitalez's notes, Trigonometric Integrals, Example 4)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • The power of cosine is 5 (odd); the power of sine is 2.
  • Find: \(\int\cos^5 x\sin^2 x\,dx\).
Step 1: Save one cosine factor

Using strategy (a), we keep one \(\cos x\) for \(du\) and write the rest with \(\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x\).

\[\int\cos^5 x\sin^2 x\,dx = \int\cos^4 x\,\cos x\,\sin^2 x\,dx\]

\[= \int(\cos^2 x)^2\cos x\,\sin^2 x\,dx\]

\[= \int(1 - \sin^2 x)^2\cos x\,\sin^2 x\,dx\]

Step 2: Substitute u = sin x

Let \(u = \sin x\), so \(du = \cos x\,dx\).

\[= \int(1 - u^2)^2u^2\,du\]

Step 3: Expand the integrand

Expand \((1-u^2)^2 = 1 - 2u^2 + u^4\), then multiply by \(u^2\).

\[= \int(u^2 - 2u^4 + u^6)\,du\]

Step 4: Integrate term by term

\[= \frac{u^3}{3} - \frac{2u^5}{5} + \frac{u^7}{7} + C\]

Step 5: Substitute back u = sin x

\[= \frac{\sin^3 x}{3} - \frac{2\sin^5 x}{5} + \frac{\sin^7 x}{7} + C\]

Answer

\[\int\cos^5 x\sin^2 x\,dx = \frac{\sin^3 x}{3} - \frac{2\sin^5 x}{5} + \frac{\sin^7 x}{7} + C\]

Check

Differentiate: \((\sin^2 x - 2\sin^4 x + \sin^6 x)\cos x = \sin^2 x(1-\sin^2 x)^2\cos x = \sin^2 x\cos^4 x\cos x\). That is our integrand.

Example 6.6 An even power of secant

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int\tan^6 x\sec^4 x\,dx\).

(Instr. Vitalez's notes, Trigonometric Integrals, Example 7)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • The power of secant is 4 (even); the power of tangent is 6.
  • Find: \(\int\tan^6 x\sec^4 x\,dx\).
Step 1: Save a factor of sec² x

Using strategy (a) for tangent and secant, we keep \(\sec^2 x\) for \(du\) and write the other \(\sec^2 x\) as \(1 + \tan^2 x\).

\[\int\tan^6 x\sec^4 x\,dx = \int\tan^6 x\,\sec^2 x\,\sec^2 x\,dx\]

\[= \int\tan^6 x\,(1 + \tan^2 x)\sec^2 x\,dx\]

Step 2: Substitute u = tan x

Let \(u = \tan x\), so \(du = \sec^2 x\,dx\).

\[= \int u^6(1 + u^2)\,du\]

Step 3: Distribute the u⁶

\[= \int(u^6 + u^8)\,du\]

Step 4: Integrate term by term

\[= \frac{u^7}{7} + \frac{u^9}{9} + C\]

Step 5: Substitute back u = tan x

\[= \frac{\tan^7 x}{7} + \frac{\tan^9 x}{9} + C\]

Answer

\[\int\tan^6 x\sec^4 x\,dx = \frac{\tan^7 x}{7} + \frac{\tan^9 x}{9} + C\]

Check

Differentiate: \((\tan^6 x + \tan^8 x)\sec^2 x = \tan^6 x(1 + \tan^2 x)\sec^2 x = \tan^6 x\sec^4 x\).
