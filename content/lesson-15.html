12.2Vectors

If we say a car moves at 60 km/h, is that enough to know where it ends up? No. We also need to know which way it goes. Some quantities need only a size: mass, temperature, area. Others need a size and a direction: a displacement, a velocity, a force. Stewart §12.2 calls these vectors and draws them as arrows. Two arrows with the same length and direction represent the same vector, no matter where they start.

Components. Place the tail of a vector at the origin; the coordinates of its tip are its components, written \(\mathbf{a}=\langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle\) (or \(\langle a_1,a_2\rangle\) in the plane). The vector from \(A(x_1,y_1,z_1)\) to \(B(x_2,y_2,z_2)\) is \(\overrightarrow{AB}=\langle x_2-x_1,\,y_2-y_1,\,z_2-z_1\rangle\): always tip minus tail. For example, from \(A(1,4,0)\) to \(B(3,1,2)\) the vector is \(\langle2,-3,2\rangle\).

Length. By the Pythagorean theorem (used twice in space), the magnitude of \(\mathbf{a}\) is \(|\mathbf{a}|=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}\). The only vector of length 0 is the zero vector \(\mathbf{0}\).

Adding and scaling. To add vectors, we add components: \(\langle1,2\rangle+\langle3,-1\rangle=\langle4,1\rangle\). Geometrically, put the tail of \(\mathbf{b}\) at the tip of \(\mathbf{a}\); then \(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\) runs from the tail of \(\mathbf{a}\) to the tip of \(\mathbf{b}\) (the triangle law). Equivalently, \(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\) is the diagonal of the parallelogram with sides \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) (Figure 15.1). A scalar multiple \(c\mathbf{a}=\langle ca_1,ca_2,ca_3\rangle\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{a}\), \(|c|\) times as long, and points the opposite way when \(c\lt 0\). Subtraction is \(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}+(-1)\mathbf{b}\). These operations obey the usual algebra rules: commutative, associative, distributive.

Standard basis and unit vectors. The vectors \(\mathbf{i}=\langle1,0,0\rangle\), \(\mathbf{j}=\langle0,1,0\rangle\), \(\mathbf{k}=\langle0,0,1\rangle\) let us write any vector as \(\mathbf{a}=a_1\mathbf{i}+a_2\mathbf{j}+a_3\mathbf{k}\). A unit vector has length 1. To get the unit vector in the direction of a nonzero \(\mathbf{a}\), we divide by its length: \(\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{a}/|\mathbf{a}|\). For \(\mathbf{a}=\langle2,-1,2\rangle\), \(|\mathbf{a}|=3\), so \(\mathbf{u}=\langle\frac23,-\frac13,\frac23\rangle\).

Why this matters. Forces acting on one object add as vectors. If two ropes pull on a box with forces \(\mathbf{F}_1\) and \(\mathbf{F}_2\), the box feels the single resultant force \(\mathbf{F}_1+\mathbf{F}_2\), and the box stays put only when the forces sum to \(\mathbf{0}\).

Common mistake Take note that magnitudes do not add: \(|\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}|\) is usually less than \(|\mathbf{a}|+|\mathbf{b}|\) (the triangle inequality). Also, \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) is tip minus tail, not tail minus tip.
Key equations
\[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\langle x_2-x_1,\,y_2-y_1,\,z_2-z_1\rangle \]
\[ |\mathbf{a}|=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2} \]
\[ \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}=\langle a_1+b_1,\,a_2+b_2,\,a_3+b_3\rangle,\qquad c\mathbf{a}=\langle ca_1,\,ca_2,\,ca_3\rangle \]
\[ \mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{a}}{|\mathbf{a}|} \]

12.3The Dot Product

Can two vectors be multiplied? Stewart §12.3 gives the first answer: the dot product, which takes two vectors and returns a number.

Definition \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3\). Multiply matching components and add.

For instance, \(\langle1,-2,4\rangle\cdot\langle3,1,2\rangle=3-2+8=9\). The dot product is commutative, distributes over addition, and satisfies \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}=|\mathbf{a}|^2\), which is a handy way to compute lengths.

The geometric meaning. If \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) (with \(0\le\theta\le\pi\)), then \[\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=|\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta.\] This comes from the Law of Cosines applied to the triangle with sides \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{b}\) and \(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\). Solving for \(\cos\theta\) lets us find the angle between two vectors from their components alone. The sign tells us a lot: positive means an acute angle, negative means obtuse, and zero means the vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular). So \(\langle2,1,-1\rangle\) and \(\langle1,1,3\rangle\) are orthogonal because \(2+1-3=0\).

Direction angles. The angles \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) that \(\mathbf{a}\) makes with the positive axes satisfy \(\cos\alpha=a_1/|\mathbf{a}|\), \(\cos\beta=a_2/|\mathbf{a}|\), \(\cos\gamma=a_3/|\mathbf{a}|\). These are just the components of the unit vector \(\mathbf{a}/|\mathbf{a}|\).

Projections. How much of \(\mathbf{b}\) points along \(\mathbf{a}\)? Let's drop a perpendicular from the tip of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto the line of \(\mathbf{a}\) (Figure 15.2). The signed length of the shadow is the scalar projection \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|}\), and the shadow itself is the vector projection \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|^2}\,\mathbf{a}\). For \(\mathbf{a}=\langle4,1\rangle\), \(\mathbf{b}=\langle2,3\rangle\): \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=11\), so \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\frac{11}{17}\langle4,1\rangle\).

Work. In physics, a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\) moving an object through displacement \(\mathbf{D}\) does work \(W=\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{D}\). Only the part of the force along the motion counts, which is exactly what the dot product measures. This extends the work integrals we met earlier in the course.

A quick way to remember it. The dot product answers the question "how much do these two vectors agree in direction?" It is largest when they point the same way, zero when they are at right angles, and most negative when they point in opposite directions. Whenever a problem asks about an angle, a perpendicular direction or the part of one vector along another, the dot product is the tool to reach for first.

Common mistake Take note that the dot product is a scalar, so \((\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathbf{c}\) makes no sense. Also, \(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}\) divides by \(|\mathbf{a}|\), the vector we project onto, not by \(|\mathbf{b}|\).
Key equations
\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3 \]
\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=|\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta \]
\[ \operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|},\qquad \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|^2}\,\mathbf{a} \]
\[ W=\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{D} \]

12.4The Cross Product

The dot product gives a number. Is there a product of two vectors that gives a vector? Yes. Stewart §12.4 introduces a second product, defined only for vectors in three dimensions. The cross product \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\) is a vector, and it is built to be perpendicular to both \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\).

Definition \[\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\a_1&a_2&a_3\\b_1&b_2&b_3\end{vmatrix}=\langle a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\;a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\;a_1b_2-a_2b_1\rangle.\]

The determinant is a memory aid: we expand along the first row and remember the minus sign on the \(\mathbf{j}\) term. For example, \(\langle1,2,0\rangle\times\langle0,1,1\rangle=\langle2\cdot1-0\cdot1,\;0\cdot0-1\cdot1,\;1\cdot1-2\cdot0\rangle=\langle2,-1,1\rangle\). Let's always check the answer: its dot product with each factor should be 0, and here \(2-2+0=0\) and \(0-1+1=0\).

Direction and length. The direction of \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\) is given by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand from \(\mathbf{a}\) toward \(\mathbf{b}\), and your thumb points along \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\). Its length is \[|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|=|\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta,\] which is the area of the parallelogram with sides \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) (base \(|\mathbf{a}|\), height \(|\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta\)), as in Figure 15.3. Half of it is the area of the triangle with those sides. Two nonzero vectors are parallel exactly when \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}\).

Algebra rules, with surprises. The basis vectors satisfy \(\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k}\), \(\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{j}\). The cross product distributes over addition, but it is not commutative: \(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a}=-(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\). It is not associative either; for instance \((\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{i})\times\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{0}\) but \(\mathbf{i}\times(\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j})=-\mathbf{j}\).

Triple product and torque. The number \(\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})\) is the scalar triple product; its absolute value is the volume of the box (parallelepiped) spanned by the three vectors, and it is 0 exactly when they lie in one plane. In mechanics, a force \(\mathbf{F}\) applied at position \(\mathbf{r}\) relative to a pivot produces torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\). A longer wrench or a force closer to perpendicular gives more turning effect, just as \(|\mathbf{r}||\mathbf{F}|\sin\theta\) predicts.

Dot versus cross. Let's compare the two products side by side. The dot product uses \(\cos\theta\) and measures how much two vectors line up; the cross product uses \(\sin\theta\) and measures how much they spread apart. That is why parallel vectors have the largest dot product but a zero cross product, while perpendicular vectors have a zero dot product and the largest possible cross product for their lengths.

Common mistake Take note of two slips: forgetting the minus sign in the middle component, or swapping the order of the factors. Order matters: \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a}\) point in opposite directions.
Key equations
\[ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=\langle a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\;a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\;a_1b_2-a_2b_1\rangle \]
\[ |\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|=|\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta \]
\[ \mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a}=-(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}) \]
\[ V=|\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})| \]

13.1Vector Functions and Space Curves

So far each vector has been a fixed arrow. But what if the arrow moves, like the position of a moving particle? In Stewart §13.1 the vector is allowed to change: a vector function assigns a vector to each number \(t\) in its domain, \[\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle f(t),g(t),h(t)\rangle=f(t)\,\mathbf{i}+g(t)\,\mathbf{j}+h(t)\,\mathbf{k}.\] The real-valued functions \(f,g,h\) are its component functions. Think of \(t\) as time and \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) as the position vector of a moving particle. The domain of \(\mathbf{r}\) is the set of \(t\) where all three components are defined; for \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle t^2,\ln t,\sqrt{4-t}\rangle\) it is \(0<t\le4\).

Limits and continuity work component by component. \[\lim_{t\to a}\mathbf{r}(t)=\Big\langle\lim_{t\to a}f(t),\,\lim_{t\to a}g(t),\,\lim_{t\to a}h(t)\Big\rangle,\] provided all three limits exist. So \(\mathbf{r}\) is continuous at \(a\) exactly when each component is. Nothing new is needed here: we apply what we already know from single-variable calculus three times.

Space curves. As \(t\) varies, the tip of \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) traces a space curve \(C\) with parametric equations \(x=f(t)\), \(y=g(t)\), \(z=h(t)\). The parameter also gives the curve a direction, the way \(t\) increases. Two classic examples:

  • \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle1+2t,\,3-t,\,4t\rangle\) is a line through \((1,3,0)\) in the direction \(\langle2,-1,4\rangle\).
  • \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle\cos t,\,\sin t,\,t\rangle\) is a helix. Its \(x\) and \(y\) coordinates go around the unit circle while \(z\) climbs steadily, like a spring or a spiral staircase.

A plane curve is the special case with only two components, as in Figure 15.4. There \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle\cos t,\sin2t\rangle\) traces a figure-eight: the \(y\) coordinate oscillates twice as fast as the \(x\) coordinate, so the curve crosses itself at the origin.

Sketching a curve. How do we picture a space curve? A useful trick is to look for an equation linking the components. For the helix, \(x^2+y^2=1\), so the curve lies on a circular cylinder. A curve can also arise as the intersection of two surfaces; parametrizing it then means finding \(x,y,z\) in terms of one variable that satisfy both equations. Computers are very good at drawing complicated space curves from their parametrizations, and we should feel free to use graphing software to explore.

Common mistake Take note that a curve and its parametrization are different things. \(\langle\cos t,\sin t\rangle\) and \(\langle\cos2t,\sin2t\rangle\) trace the same circle, but the second one goes around twice as fast.
Key equations
\[ \mathbf{r}(t)=\langle f(t),g(t),h(t)\rangle=f(t)\,\mathbf{i}+g(t)\,\mathbf{j}+h(t)\,\mathbf{k} \]
\[ \lim_{t\to a}\mathbf{r}(t)=\Big\langle\lim_{t\to a}f(t),\,\lim_{t\to a}g(t),\,\lim_{t\to a}h(t)\Big\rangle \]
\[ \text{line: }\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{r}_0+t\,\mathbf{v},\qquad \text{helix: }\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle\cos t,\sin t,t\rangle \]

13.2Derivatives and Integrals of Vector Functions

Now let's bring calculus to vector functions (Stewart §13.2). The derivative is defined by the familiar limit, \[\mathbf{r}'(t)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\mathbf{r}(t+h)-\mathbf{r}(t)}{h},\] and, because limits work componentwise, it is computed componentwise: \[\mathbf{r}'(t)=\langle f'(t),\,g'(t),\,h'(t)\rangle.\] For \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle t^2,\,e^{3t},\,\sin t\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}'(t)=\langle2t,\,3e^{3t},\,\cos t\rangle\).

Tangent vectors. The difference quotient is a vector along a secant of the curve; as \(h\to0\) it turns into a tangent vector \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\), pointing in the direction of motion (Figure 15.5). The unit tangent vector is \(\mathbf{T}(t)=\mathbf{r}'(t)/|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\), and the tangent line at \(\mathbf{r}(t_0)\) passes through that point in the direction \(\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\).

Rules. The usual rules carry over, with products interpreted carefully: \((\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})'=\mathbf{u}'+\mathbf{v}'\), \((c\mathbf{u})'=c\mathbf{u}'\), \((f\mathbf{u})'=f'\mathbf{u}+f\mathbf{u}'\), \((\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})'=\mathbf{u}'\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}'\), \((\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v})'=\mathbf{u}'\times\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}'\) (keep the order!), and the chain rule \(\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{u}(f(t))=f'(t)\mathbf{u}'(f(t))\). Notice a nice consequence: if \(|\mathbf{r}(t)|\) is constant, then differentiating \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}=c^2\) gives \(2\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}'=0\). So a particle moving on a sphere centered at the origin always moves perpendicular to its position vector.

Integrals are also componentwise: \(\int_a^b\mathbf{r}(t)\,dt=\big\langle\int_a^bf,\int_a^bg,\int_a^bh\big\rangle\), and the Fundamental Theorem still holds, \(\int_a^b\mathbf{r}(t)\,dt=\mathbf{R}(b)-\mathbf{R}(a)\) when \(\mathbf{R}'=\mathbf{r}\). An indefinite integral gets a vector constant \(\mathbf{C}\), fixed by an initial condition such as \(\mathbf{r}(0)\).

A look ahead (§13.3 and §13.4). The length of a curve traced once for \(a\le t\le b\) is \(L=\int_a^b|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt\), the vector version of the arc length formula we learned for parametric curves. For a moving particle, the velocity is \(\mathbf{v}(t)=\mathbf{r}'(t)\), the speed is \(|\mathbf{v}(t)|\), and the acceleration is \(\mathbf{a}(t)=\mathbf{r}''(t)\). Running this backwards, integrating \(\mathbf{a}\) twice with the initial velocity and position recovers the motion, which is how projectile paths are computed.

The big picture. Almost nothing in this section is truly new. Each derivative, integral or limit of a vector function is three ordinary single-variable problems done side by side, so every technique from this course (substitution, integration by parts, partial fractions) still applies, one component at a time. What is new is the geometric meaning: the derivative is a direction of motion, and its length is a speed.

Common mistake Take note that \(|\mathbf{r}(t)|'\) is not \(|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\). The speed is the length of the derivative, not the derivative of the length.
Key equations
\[ \mathbf{r}'(t)=\langle f'(t),\,g'(t),\,h'(t)\rangle \]
\[ \mathbf{T}(t)=\frac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{|\mathbf{r}'(t)|} \]
\[ \frac{d}{dt}[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}]=\mathbf{u}'\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}',\qquad \frac{d}{dt}[\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}]=\mathbf{u}'\times\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}' \]
\[ \int_a^b\mathbf{r}(t)\,dt=\mathbf{R}(b)-\mathbf{R}(a) \]
\[ L=\int_a^b|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt,\qquad \mathbf{v}=\mathbf{r}',\ \ \mathbf{a}=\mathbf{r}'' \]

★Key takeaways

  • A vector has a length and a direction; in components we add, scale and measure it with \(|\mathbf{a}|=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}\).
  • The dot product \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta\) is a number that gives us angles, tests orthogonality and produces projections and work.
  • The cross product \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\) is a vector perpendicular to both factors whose length is the area of the parallelogram they span; take note that order matters.
  • A vector function \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) traces a curve, and we compute its limits, derivatives and integrals component by component.
  • \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) is a tangent vector (the velocity), and integrating \(\mathbf{r}'\) needs a vector constant fixed by an initial condition.

Example 15.1 The angle between two vectors and a projection

Let \(\mathbf{a}=\langle2,-1,2\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{b}=\langle1,3,-2\rangle\). Find the angle between them (to the nearest tenth of a degree) and the vector projection of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto \(\mathbf{a}\).

(Similar to Stewart §12.3, Examples 3 and 5)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\mathbf{a}=\langle2,-1,2\rangle\), \(\mathbf{b}=\langle1,3,-2\rangle\).
  • Asked: the angle \(\theta\) between them, and \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}\).
Step 1: Compute the dot product

We multiply matching components and add.

\[\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=(2)(1)+(-1)(3)+(2)(-2)\]

\[=2-3-4=-5\]

Step 2: Compute the lengths

We use \(|\mathbf{a}|=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}\) for each vector.

\[|\mathbf{a}|=\sqrt{2^2+(-1)^2+2^2}=\sqrt{4+1+4}=\sqrt9=3\]

\[|\mathbf{b}|=\sqrt{1^2+3^2+(-2)^2}=\sqrt{1+9+4}=\sqrt{14}\]

Step 3: Find the angle

We solve \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta\) for \(\cos\theta\).

\[\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|}=\frac{-5}{3\sqrt{14}}\]

\[\approx\frac{-5}{11.225}\approx-0.4454\]

\[\theta=\cos^{-1}(-0.4454)\approx116.5^\circ\]

Step 4: Find the vector projection

We use \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\dfrac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|^2}\,\mathbf{a}\), with \(|\mathbf{a}|^2=9\).

\[\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\frac{-5}{9}\langle2,-1,2\rangle\]

\[=\left\langle-\tfrac{10}{9},\tfrac59,-\tfrac{10}{9}\right\rangle\]

Answer

So the angle between the vectors is about \(116.5^\circ\), and \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\left\langle-\tfrac{10}{9},\tfrac59,-\tfrac{10}{9}\right\rangle\).

Check

The negative dot product predicted an obtuse angle, and \(116.5^\circ\) is obtuse ✓. The projection points opposite to \(\mathbf{a}\), as it should when \(\theta\gt 90^\circ\). Also, \(\mathbf{b}-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\langle\frac{19}{9},\frac{22}{9},-\frac{8}{9}\rangle\) has dot product \(\frac{38-22-16}{9}=0\) with \(\mathbf{a}\) ✓.

Example 15.2 The area of a triangle in space

Find a vector perpendicular to the plane through \(P(1,0,2)\), \(Q(3,1,1)\) and \(R(0,2,3)\), and find the area of triangle \(PQR\).

(Similar to Stewart §12.4, Example 4)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Points \(P(1,0,2)\), \(Q(3,1,1)\), \(R(0,2,3)\).
  • Asked: a vector perpendicular to the plane \(PQR\), and the area of triangle \(PQR\).
Step 1: Find two vectors in the plane

We take tip minus tail for \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) and \(\overrightarrow{PR}\).

\[\overrightarrow{PQ}=\langle3-1,\,1-0,\,1-2\rangle=\langle2,1,-1\rangle\]

\[\overrightarrow{PR}=\langle0-1,\,2-0,\,3-2\rangle=\langle-1,2,1\rangle\]

Step 2: Compute the cross product, one component at a time

The cross product is perpendicular to both vectors, so it is perpendicular to the plane. We use \(\langle a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\;a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\;a_1b_2-a_2b_1\rangle\).

\[\text{first: }(1)(1)-(-1)(2)=1+2=3\]

\[\text{second: }(-1)(-1)-(2)(1)=1-2=-1\]

\[\text{third: }(2)(2)-(1)(-1)=4+1=5\]

\[\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR}=\langle3,-1,5\rangle\]

Step 3: Find the length of the cross product

This length is the area of the parallelogram with sides \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) and \(\overrightarrow{PR}\).

\[|\langle3,-1,5\rangle|=\sqrt{3^2+(-1)^2+5^2}=\sqrt{9+1+25}=\sqrt{35}\]

Step 4: Take half for the triangle

The triangle is half of the parallelogram.

\[\text{Area}=\frac{\sqrt{35}}{2}\approx2.958\]

Answer

So \(\langle3,-1,5\rangle\) is perpendicular to the plane, and the area of triangle \(PQR\) is \(\dfrac{\sqrt{35}}{2}\approx2.958\) square units.

Check

\(\langle3,-1,5\rangle\cdot\langle2,1,-1\rangle=6-1-5=0\) and \(\langle3,-1,5\rangle\cdot\langle-1,2,1\rangle=-3-2+5=0\), so the cross product really is perpendicular to both sides ✓.

Example 15.3 Recovering a curve from its tangent vector

A curve satisfies \(\mathbf{r}'(t)=\langle\cos t,\,2t,\,e^t\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r}(0)=\langle1,0,2\rangle\). Find \(\mathbf{r}(t)\), the unit tangent vector at \(t=0\), and the tangent line at \(t=0\).

(Similar to Stewart §13.2, Example 5)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\mathbf{r}'(t)=\langle\cos t,\,2t,\,e^t\rangle\)
  • \(\mathbf{r}(0)=\langle1,0,2\rangle\)
  • Asked: \(\mathbf{r}(t)\), \(\mathbf{T}(0)\), and the tangent line at \(t=0\).
Step 1: Integrate each component

Integrals of vector functions are done component by component, and we add a vector constant \(\mathbf{C}\).

\[\int\cos t\,dt=\sin t,\qquad\int2t\,dt=t^2,\qquad\int e^t\,dt=e^t\]

\[\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle\sin t,\,t^2,\,e^t\rangle+\mathbf{C}\]

Step 2: Use r(0) to find C

We substitute \(t=0\) and compare with the given \(\mathbf{r}(0)\).

\[\mathbf{r}(0)=\langle\sin0,\,0^2,\,e^0\rangle+\mathbf{C}=\langle0,0,1\rangle+\mathbf{C}\]

\[\langle0,0,1\rangle+\mathbf{C}=\langle1,0,2\rangle\]

\[\mathbf{C}=\langle1,0,1\rangle\]

\[\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle1+\sin t,\;t^2,\;e^t+1\rangle\]

Step 3: Find the unit tangent vector at t = 0

We evaluate \(\mathbf{r}'(0)\) and divide by its length.

\[\mathbf{r}'(0)=\langle\cos0,\,2(0),\,e^0\rangle=\langle1,0,1\rangle\]

\[|\mathbf{r}'(0)|=\sqrt{1^2+0^2+1^2}=\sqrt2\]

\[\mathbf{T}(0)=\frac{\langle1,0,1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=\left\langle\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},0,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right\rangle\]

Step 4: Write the tangent line

The tangent line passes through \(\mathbf{r}(0)=(1,0,2)\) in the direction \(\mathbf{r}'(0)=\langle1,0,1\rangle\). We use a parameter \(u\) for the line.

\[\langle x,y,z\rangle=\langle1,0,2\rangle+u\langle1,0,1\rangle\]

\[x=1+u,\qquad y=0,\qquad z=2+u\]

Answer

So \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle1+\sin t,\;t^2,\;e^t+1\rangle\), \(\mathbf{T}(0)=\left\langle\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},0,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right\rangle\), and the tangent line is \(x=1+u\), \(y=0\), \(z=2+u\).

Check

Differentiating \(\langle1+\sin t,t^2,e^t+1\rangle\) gives back \(\langle\cos t,2t,e^t\rangle\), and \(\mathbf{r}(0)=\langle1,0,2\rangle\) ✓. Take note of a common slip: putting a scalar \(+C\) in only one component. Each component needs its own constant.
