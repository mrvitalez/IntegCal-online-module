4.3The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

In Module 1 you computed \(\int_0^3 x^2\,dx = 9\) with a page of summation formulas. Stewart §4.3 shows a far shorter route, and in doing so reveals that differentiation and integration are inverse processes. This link is called the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC).

The area function. Fix a continuous \(f\) on \([a,b]\) and define \[g(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt, \qquad a \le x \le b.\] For each \(x\), \(g(x)\) is a number: the net area under \(f\) from \(a\) up to \(x\). As \(x\) moves right, \(g(x)\) changes. How fast? If you push \(x\) a tiny step \(h\) to the right, you add a thin strip of width \(h\) and height about \(f(x)\), so \(g(x+h) - g(x) \approx f(x)\,h\). Dividing by \(h\) and letting \(h \to 0\) suggests \(g'(x) = f(x)\).

FTC, Part 1 If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then \(g(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and \[\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x).\]

So \(\frac{d}{dx}\int_2^x \sqrt{1 + t^4}\,dt = \sqrt{1 + x^4}\), even though no simple formula for the integral exists. Just replace \(t\) by \(x\).

With the Chain Rule. If the upper limit is a function \(u = h(x)\) instead of \(x\), write \(\int_a^{h(x)} f(t)\,dt = g(u)\) and differentiate: \[\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{h(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(h(x)\big)\,h'(x).\] If \(x\) appears in the lower limit, first flip the limits: \(\int_x^a f(t)\,dt = -\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\).

If both limits depend on \(x\), split at any convenient constant \(a\): \(\int_{x}^{x^2} f(t)\,dt = \int_a^{x^2} f(t)\,dt - \int_a^{x} f(t)\,dt\), then differentiate each piece. For instance, \(\frac{d}{dx}\int_{x}^{x^2} \sqrt{t}\,dt = \sqrt{x^2}\cdot 2x - \sqrt{x} = 2x^2 - \sqrt{x}\) for \(x > 0\). Notice that you never needed to find an antiderivative of \(\sqrt{t}\): FTC1 works directly with the integrand.

FTC, Part 2 If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\) (that is, \(F' = f\)), then \[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a).\]

We write \(F(b) - F(a) = \big[F(x)\big]_a^b\). Now \(\int_0^3 x^2\,dx = \big[\tfrac{x^3}{3}\big]_0^3 = 9 - 0 = 9\) in one line. Similarly \(\int_0^{\pi} \sin x\,dx = \big[-\cos x\big]_0^{\pi} = 1 - (-1) = 2\): one arch of the sine curve has area exactly 2. Any antiderivative works, because an added constant \(C\) cancels in \(F(b) - F(a)\).

Put together, the two parts say: integrate \(f\) and then differentiate, and you get \(f\) back (Part 1); differentiate \(F\) and then integrate, and you get the change \(F(b) - F(a)\) (Part 2).

Common mistake FTC2 needs \(f\) to be continuous on all of \([a,b]\). Blindly writing \(\int_{-1}^{1} x^{-2}\,dx = \big[-x^{-1}\big]_{-1}^{1} = -2\) gives a negative answer for a positive integrand. The function blows up at 0, so the theorem does not apply.
Key equations
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x) \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{h(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(h(x)\big)\,h'(x) \]
\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a), \quad F' = f \]

4.4Indefinite Integrals and the Net Change Theorem

Because FTC2 turns definite integrals into antiderivative problems, it pays to have a good notation and a table of antiderivatives. Stewart §4.4 introduces the indefinite integral \[\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C \quad\text{means}\quad F'(x) = f(x).\] Be careful to tell the two kinds apart: a definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) is a number, while an indefinite integral \(\int f(x)\,dx\) is a family of functions, one for each value of \(C\).

A short table. Each formula is checked by differentiating the right side.

IntegralResult
\(\int c f(x)\,dx\)\(c\int f(x)\,dx\)
\(\int [f(x) + g(x)]\,dx\)\(\int f(x)\,dx + \int g(x)\,dx\)
\(\int k\,dx\)\(kx + C\)
\(\int x^n\,dx\) \((n \ne -1)\)\(\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C\)
\(\int \sin x\,dx\)\(-\cos x + C\)
\(\int \cos x\,dx\)\(\sin x + C\)
\(\int \sec^2 x\,dx\)\(\tan x + C\)
\(\int \csc^2 x\,dx\)\(-\cot x + C\)
\(\int \sec x\tan x\,dx\)\(\sec x + C\)
\(\int \csc x\cot x\,dx\)\(-\csc x + C\)

Before integrating, rewrite roots and quotients as powers: \(\int \frac{x + 2}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx = \int (x^{1/2} + 2x^{-1/2})\,dx = \tfrac23 x^{3/2} + 4x^{1/2} + C\). There is no "quotient rule" for integrals, so you must simplify first.

Trigonometric identities can do the same job. For example, \(\int \frac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x}\,dx\) is not in the table as written, but \(\frac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \sec x\tan x\), so the answer is \(\sec x + C\). Always finish by differentiating your answer: if you get the integrand back, you are right. This check takes seconds and catches most sign errors (the minus signs in \(-\cos x\), \(-\cot x\) and \(-\csc x\) are the usual culprits).

Common mistake The integral of a product is not the product of the integrals: \(\int x\cdot x\,dx = \tfrac{x^3}{3} + C\), not \(\tfrac{x^2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{x^2}{2}\). Multiply out first.

The Net Change Theorem. FTC2 applied to a rate of change \(F'\) reads \[\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a):\] the integral of a rate of change is the net change. If \(V'(t)\) is the rate (L/min) at which water enters a tank, \(\int_{t_1}^{t_2} V'(t)\,dt\) is the change in volume. If \(C'(x)\) is marginal cost, \(\int_{100}^{200} C'(x)\,dx\) is the extra cost of raising production from 100 to 200 units. The units of the answer are always (units of the rate) × (units of the variable), for example (L/min) × min = L.

Displacement versus distance. For a particle with velocity \(v(t)\):

  • displacement \(= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,dt\) (net change in position; backward motion cancels forward motion);
  • total distance \(= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v(t)|\,dt\). To compute it, find where \(v(t) = 0\), split the interval there, and add the absolute values of the pieces.

For \(v(t) = t^2 - 4t + 3 = (t-1)(t-3)\) on \([0,4]\), the three pieces are \(\tfrac43, -\tfrac43, \tfrac43\). The displacement is \(\tfrac43\) but the distance is \(4\).

Key equations
\[ \int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C \iff F'(x) = f(x) \]
\[ \int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \quad (n \ne -1) \]
\[ \int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \]
\[ \text{distance} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v(t)|\,dt \]

★Key takeaways

  • FTC Part 1: the derivative of an area function is the integrand, d/dx ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt = f(x); with a variable limit h(x), multiply by h′(x).
  • FTC Part 2: if F′ = f and f is continuous on [a, b], then ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a).
  • An indefinite integral is a family of antiderivatives F(x) + C; a definite integral is a single number.
  • Rewrite roots and quotients as powers before integrating, and check every antiderivative by differentiating.
  • The integral of a rate of change is the net change; for velocity, ∫v dt is displacement and ∫|v| dt is total distance.

Example 2.1 Derivatives of integrals with variable limits

Find the derivative: (a) \(\displaystyle y = \int_1^{x^3} \sqrt{1 + t^2}\,dt\)   (b) \(\displaystyle y = \int_x^{2} \cos(t^2)\,dt\).

(Similar to Stewart §4.3, Examples 3 and 4)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use FTC1. In (a) the upper limit is \(u = x^3\), so the Chain Rule adds a factor \(du/dx\). In (b) the variable is in the lower limit, so flip the limits first.

Solution:

(a) Let \(u = x^3\). Then \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{d}{du}\left[\int_1^{u} \sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt\right]\dfrac{du}{dx} = \sqrt{1+u^2}\cdot 3x^2 = 3x^2\sqrt{1 + x^6}\).

(b) \(y = -\int_2^{x} \cos(t^2)\,dt\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\cos(x^2)\).

Check:

A frequent error in (a) is to write \(\sqrt{1 + x^6}\) and forget the factor \(3x^2\). In (b), think about the sign: increasing \(x\) shrinks the interval \([x, 2]\), so near \(x = 0\) (where \(\cos(t^2) > 0\)) \(y\) should decrease, and indeed \(y' = -\cos(x^2) < 0\) there.

Example 2.2 Evaluating with FTC Part 2

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^4 \left(3\sqrt{x} - \frac{2}{x^2}\right)dx\).

(Similar to Stewart §4.3, Example 7)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Rewrite as powers, find an antiderivative with the power rule, then substitute the limits.

Solution:

\(3\sqrt{x} - 2x^{-2}\) has antiderivative \(3\cdot\tfrac23 x^{3/2} - 2\cdot\dfrac{x^{-1}}{-1} = 2x^{3/2} + \dfrac{2}{x}\). So \[\int_1^4 \left(3\sqrt{x} - \frac{2}{x^2}\right)dx = \left[2x^{3/2} + \frac{2}{x}\right]_1^4 = \left(16 + \tfrac12\right) - (2 + 2) = \frac{25}{2}.\]

Check:

Differentiate \(2x^{3/2} + 2x^{-1}\): you get \(3x^{1/2} - 2x^{-2}\). ✔ The integrand is continuous on \([1,4]\) (no division by zero there), so FTC2 applies.

Example 2.3 Displacement and distance

A particle moves along a line with velocity \(v(t) = t^2 - 2t - 3\) m/s for \(0 \le t \le 5\). Find (a) its displacement and (b) the total distance it travels.

(Similar to Stewart §4.4, Example 6)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Displacement is \(\int_0^5 v\,dt\). For distance, find where \(v\) changes sign, split there, and add absolute values.

Solution:

An antiderivative is \(F(t) = \tfrac{t^3}{3} - t^2 - 3t\).

(a) Displacement \(= F(5) - F(0) = \tfrac{125}{3} - 25 - 15 = \tfrac{5}{3}\) m.

(b) \(v(t) = (t - 3)(t + 1)\) is negative on \([0, 3)\) and positive on \((3, 5]\). \[\int_0^3 v\,dt = F(3) - F(0) = 9 - 9 - 9 = -9, \qquad \int_3^5 v\,dt = \tfrac53 - (-9) = \tfrac{32}{3}.\] Distance \(= 9 + \tfrac{32}{3} = \tfrac{59}{3} \approx 19.7\) m.

Check:

The two pieces add back to the displacement: \(-9 + \tfrac{32}{3} = \tfrac53\). ✔ The particle goes 9 m backward, then \(32/3\) m forward, ending \(5/3\) m ahead of its start.
