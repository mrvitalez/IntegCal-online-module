4.3The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

In Module 1 we evaluated \(\int_0^3 (x^3 - 6x)\,dx = -6.75\) straight from the definition. That took us a whole page of sums and limits. So, is there a faster way? Yes. That long solution can be done in a few lines by following the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC). It is called fundamental because it connects the two halves of calculus: differentiation (Calculus 1) and integration (this course). They turn out to be inverse processes.

The area function. Let's start with a continuous \(f\) on \([a,b]\) and define \[g(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt, \qquad a \le x \le b.\] Notice that the upper limit is now a variable. If \(f \ge 0\), \(g(x)\) is the area under \(f\) from \(a\) up to \(x\). What happens when we move \(x\) a little to the right, by \(h\)? We add a thin strip of width \(h\) and height about \(f(x)\), so \(g(x+h) - g(x) \approx f(x)\,h\). Dividing by \(h\) and letting \(h \to 0\), we get \(g'(x) = f(x)\). This is the first part of the theorem.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\).
  1. (FTC1) If \(g(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt\), then \(g'(x) = f(x)\). In words: the derivative of the area function is the original function.
  2. (FTC2) \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)\), where \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\), that is, any function with \(F' = f\).

Why FTC2 works. By FTC1, \(g(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) is an antiderivative of \(f\). Any other antiderivative differs from it by a constant: \(F(x) = g(x) + C\). Then \(F(b) - F(a) = [g(b) + C] - [g(a) + C] = g(b) - g(a) = \int_a^b f(t)\,dt - 0\). The constant cancels, which is why we can use any antiderivative.

How to use FTC2. If we are given an integral of a power, \(\int x^n\,dx\), the antiderivative is found by adding one to the power and dividing by the new power: \[\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \qquad (n \ne -1).\] Now, if there is an interval, say from \(a\) to \(b\), we write \[\int_a^b x^n\,dx = \left.\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\right|_a^b = \frac{b^{n+1}}{n+1} - \frac{a^{n+1}}{n+1}.\] That is, we substitute the upper limit, then the lower limit, and take the difference. We often use the notation \(F(x)\big|_a^b\) or \(\big[F(x)\big]_a^b\) for \(F(b) - F(a)\).

FTC1 with the Chain Rule. If the upper limit is a function \(u(x)\) instead of \(x\), then by the Chain Rule \[\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x).\] Take note that we do not need to evaluate the integral at all to find this derivative.

Common mistake In FTC2 the order is upper minus lower: \(F(b) - F(a)\). Also, when substituting a negative limit into \(F\), use parentheses: \(\left[\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_{-1}^{2} = \frac{16}{4} - \frac{(-1)^4}{4}\).
Key equations
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x) \]
\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) = \Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b, \quad F' = f \]
\[ \int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \quad (n \ne -1) \]

4.4Indefinite Integrals and the Net Change Theorem

Because FTC2 turns every definite integral into a search for an antiderivative, we need a convenient notation for antiderivatives. We write \[\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C \quad \text{means} \quad F'(x) = f(x),\] and call it an indefinite integral. Notice the difference: a definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) is a number, while an indefinite integral \(\int f(x)\,dx\) is a family of functions. The \(+C\) is there because adding any constant to \(F\) does not change its derivative.

Table of indefinite integrals. Every derivative formula we learned in Calculus 1, read backwards, gives an integral formula:

IntegralAnswerIntegralAnswer
\(\int k\,dx\)\(kx + C\)\(\int \sin x\,dx\)\(-\cos x + C\)
\(\int x^n\,dx\ (n \ne -1)\)\(\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C\)\(\int \cos x\,dx\)\(\sin x + C\)
\(\int \frac1x\,dx\)\(\ln|x| + C\)\(\int \sec^2 x\,dx\)\(\tan x + C\)
\(\int e^x\,dx\)\(e^x + C\)\(\int \sec x\tan x\,dx\)\(\sec x + C\)
\(\int b^x\,dx\)\(\frac{b^x}{\ln b} + C\)\(\int \frac{1}{1+x^2}\,dx\)\(\tan^{-1}x + C\)

Also, constants come out and sums split: \(\int [c f(x) \pm g(x)]\,dx = c\int f(x)\,dx \pm \int g(x)\,dx\). Before integrating, always rewrite roots and fractions as powers: \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), \(\frac{1}{x^2} = x^{-2}\). And we can always check an indefinite integral by differentiating the answer.

The Net Change Theorem. FTC2 says something very physical if we read it the other way around. Since \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(F'\), \[\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a).\] In words: the integral of a rate of change is the net change. For example, if \(V'(t)\) is the rate water flows into a tank, \(\int_{t_1}^{t_2} V'(t)\,dt\) is the change in volume. In motion, recall that velocity is the derivative of position, \(v(t) = s'(t)\). So:

  • Displacement \(= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,dt = s(t_2) - s(t_1)\): areas below the axis (moving backward) count as negative.
  • Total distance travelled \(= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v(t)|\,dt\): every piece counts as positive. To compute it, find where \(v(t) = 0\), split the interval there, and add the absolute values of the pieces.
Common mistake Forgetting \(+C\) on an indefinite integral, or adding \(+C\) to a definite integral. A definite integral is just a number: \(\int_0^2 y^3\,dy = 4\), not \(4 + C\).
Key equations
\[ \int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C \iff F'(x) = f(x) \]
\[ \int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \]
\[ \text{displacement} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,dt, \qquad \text{distance} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v(t)|\,dt \]

★Key takeaways

  • Differentiation and integration are inverse processes: that is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
  • FTC1: the derivative of g(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt is f(x); with a variable upper limit u(x), multiply by u′(x).
  • FTC2: ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a) for any antiderivative F, so we no longer need limits of sums.
  • Power rule for integrals: add one to the power and divide by the new power (n ≠ −1).
  • An indefinite integral is a family of functions (+C); a definite integral is a number.
  • The integral of a rate of change is the net change; displacement uses v, total distance uses |v|.

Example 2.1 The long integral from Module 1, now with the FTC

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 (x^3 - 6x)\,dx\) using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = x^3 - 6x\) on \([0, 3]\). Find: the definite integral.

Plan: Split the integral into two terms, integrate each with the power rule, then substitute the upper limit minus the lower limit.

Step 1. Split the integral using the sum rule, and take the constant 6 out.

\[\int_0^3 (x^3 - 6x)\,dx = \int_0^3 x^3\,dx - 6\int_0^3 x\,dx\]

Step 2. Apply the power rule: add one to the power and divide by the new power.

\[= \left.\frac{x^4}{4}\right|_0^3 - 6\left.\frac{x^2}{2}\right|_0^3 = \left.\frac{x^4}{4}\right|_0^3 - \left.3x^2\right|_0^3\]

Step 3. Substitute the limits: upper minus lower.

\[= \left[\frac{(3)^4}{4} - \frac{(0)^4}{4}\right] - \left[3(3)^2 - 3(0)^2\right] = \frac{81}{4} - 27\]

Step 4. Simplify.

\[= 20.25 - 27 = -6.75\]

Answer: \(\boxed{\int_0^3 (x^3 - 6x)\,dx = -\tfrac{27}{4} = -6.75}\). That is our answer, and it is exactly what we got from the limit of Riemann sums in Example 1.4, in a fraction of the work.

Check: Differentiate the antiderivative: \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x^4}{4} - 3x^2\right) = x^3 - 6x\), the integrand. The sign is negative because most of the region on \([0,3]\) lies below the \(x\)-axis (the curve crosses the axis at \(x = \sqrt6 \approx 2.45\)).

Example 2.2 Power-rule integrals

Evaluate: (a) \(\displaystyle\int x^3\,dx\)   (b) \(\displaystyle\int \tfrac16 g^2\,dg\)   (c) \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 y^3\,dy\)   (d) \(\displaystyle\int_1^6 (s^2 + 4s^3)\,ds\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: four power-rule integrals, two indefinite and two definite. Find: each one.

Plan: Use \(\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C\). Indefinite integrals get \(+C\); definite integrals get upper minus lower and no \(+C\). Notice that the variable can be any letter.

Step 1. (a) Add one to the power: \(3 + 1 = 4\), then divide by 4.

\[\int x^3\,dx = \frac{x^4}{4} + C\]

Step 2. (b) Take the constant \(\tfrac16\) out, then integrate \(g^2\).

\[\int \tfrac16 g^2\,dg = \frac16\cdot\frac{g^3}{3} + C = \frac{g^3}{18} + C\]

Step 3. (c) Antiderivative, then upper minus lower.

\[\int_0^2 y^3\,dy = \left.\frac{y^4}{4}\right|_0^2 = \frac{2^4}{4} - \frac{0^4}{4} = \frac{16}{4} = 4\]

Step 4. (d) Integrate term by term: \(\int s^2\,ds = \frac{s^3}{3}\) and \(\int 4s^3\,ds = 4\cdot\frac{s^4}{4} = s^4\).

\[\int_1^6 (s^2 + 4s^3)\,ds = \left[\frac{s^3}{3} + s^4\right]_1^6\]

Step 5. Substitute \(s = 6\) and \(s = 1\), and subtract.

\[= \left(\frac{216}{3} + 1296\right) - \left(\frac13 + 1\right) = 72 + 1296 - \frac43 = 1368 - \frac43 = \frac{4100}{3}\]

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{\frac{x^4}{4} + C}\) (b) \(\boxed{\frac{g^3}{18} + C}\) (c) \(\boxed{4}\) (d) \(\boxed{\frac{4100}{3} \approx 1366.67}\).

Check: Differentiate (a) and (b): \(\frac{d}{dx}\frac{x^4}{4} = x^3\) and \(\frac{d}{dg}\frac{g^3}{18} = \frac{3g^2}{18} = \frac{g^2}{6}\). For (d), the integrand grows from 5 to 900 on an interval of length 5, so an answer in the low thousands is reasonable.

Example 2.3 FTC1 with the Chain Rule

Find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_1^{x^2} \sqrt{1 + t^3}\,dt\).

(Similar to Stewart §4.3, Example 5)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: an integral with a variable upper limit \(u(x) = x^2\). Find: its derivative.

Plan: We cannot find an antiderivative of \(\sqrt{1+t^3}\) with our table, and we do not need one. Use FTC1 together with the Chain Rule.

Step 1. Let \(u = x^2\), so the function is \(g(u) = \int_1^{u}\sqrt{1+t^3}\,dt\) with \(u\) depending on \(x\).

Step 2. By FTC1, the derivative with respect to \(u\) is the integrand evaluated at \(u\).

\[\frac{dg}{du} = \sqrt{1 + u^3}\]

Step 3. By the Chain Rule, multiply by \(\frac{du}{dx} = 2x\).

\[\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^2}\sqrt{1+t^3}\,dt = \sqrt{1 + u^3}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} = \sqrt{1 + (x^2)^3}\cdot 2x\]

Step 4. Simplify \((x^2)^3 = x^6\).

\[= 2x\sqrt{1 + x^6}\]

Answer: \(\boxed{2x\sqrt{1 + x^6}}\). That is our answer.

Check: At \(x = 1\) the upper limit equals the lower limit, so the integral is 0; our formula says it is increasing there at rate \(2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83\), which is positive as expected since the integrand is positive. The lower limit 1 does not appear in the answer at all.

Example 2.4 Displacement versus total distance

A particle moves along a line with velocity \(v(t) = t^2 - 4t + 3\) m/s. Find (a) the displacement and (b) the total distance travelled during \(0 \le t \le 4\).

(Similar to Stewart §4.4, Example 6)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(v(t) = t^2 - 4t + 3\), \(0 \le t \le 4\). Find: (a) \(\int_0^4 v\,dt\); (b) \(\int_0^4 |v|\,dt\).

Plan: Displacement is the integral of \(v\). For distance, find where \(v = 0\), split the interval there, and add the absolute values of the pieces.

Step 1. Find an antiderivative of \(v\).

\[F(t) = \frac{t^3}{3} - 2t^2 + 3t\]

Step 2. (a) Displacement: upper minus lower.

\[\int_0^4 v(t)\,dt = F(4) - F(0) = \left(\frac{64}{3} - 32 + 12\right) - 0 = \frac{64}{3} - 20 = \frac43\]

Step 3. (b) Find where the particle stops: \(t^2 - 4t + 3 = (t-1)(t-3) = 0\), so \(t = 1\) and \(t = 3\). Test a point in each piece: \(v(0) = 3 \gt 0\), \(v(2) = -1 \lt 0\), \(v(4) = 3 \gt 0\).

Step 4. Integrate each piece with the same \(F\). Note \(F(1) = \frac13 - 2 + 3 = \frac43\) and \(F(3) = 9 - 18 + 9 = 0\).

\[\int_0^1 v\,dt = F(1) - F(0) = \tfrac43, \qquad \int_1^3 v\,dt = F(3) - F(1) = -\tfrac43, \qquad \int_3^4 v\,dt = F(4) - F(3) = \tfrac43\]

Step 5. Add the absolute values.

\[\text{distance} = \tfrac43 + \left|-\tfrac43\right| + \tfrac43 = 4\]

Answer: (a) displacement \(\boxed{\tfrac43 \text{ m}}\) (it ends \(\tfrac43\) m to the right of where it started); (b) total distance \(\boxed{4 \text{ m}}\).

Check: The three pieces in Figure 2.3 have the same size, \(\tfrac43\), with the middle one below the axis: \(\tfrac43 - \tfrac43 + \tfrac43 = \tfrac43\) for displacement, and \(3 \times \tfrac43 = 4\) for distance. Distance can never be smaller than the size of the displacement.

Example 2.5 Rewrite as powers first

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^4 \left(\sqrt{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}\right)dx\).

(Similar to Stewart §4.4, Example 4)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: an integrand with a root and a fraction. Find: the definite integral.

Plan: Rewrite \(\sqrt x\) and \(\frac{1}{x^2}\) as powers of \(x\), apply the power rule to each, then evaluate.

Step 1. Rewrite as powers.

\[\sqrt{x} + \frac{1}{x^2} = x^{1/2} + x^{-2}\]

Step 2. Power rule: \(\frac12 + 1 = \frac32\) and \(-2 + 1 = -1\).

\[\int (x^{1/2} + x^{-2})\,dx = \frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2} + \frac{x^{-1}}{-1} = \frac23 x^{3/2} - \frac1x\]

Step 3. Substitute the limits. Note \(4^{3/2} = (\sqrt4)^3 = 8\).

\[\left[\frac23 x^{3/2} - \frac1x\right]_1^4 = \left(\frac{16}{3} - \frac14\right) - \left(\frac23 - 1\right)\]

Step 4. Simplify with a common denominator of 12.

\[= \frac{64}{12} - \frac{3}{12} - \frac{8}{12} + \frac{12}{12} = \frac{65}{12}\]

Answer: \(\boxed{\dfrac{65}{12} \approx 5.42}\).

Check: On \([1,4]\) the integrand goes from \(1 + 1 = 2\) down to about \(1.5\) and back up to \(2 + \tfrac1{16} \approx 2.06\); a height around 1.8 over a width of 3 gives about 5.4, which matches.
