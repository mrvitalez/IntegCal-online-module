AThe Laplace Transform

For our special topics, let's start with a question: what if we could turn a differential equation into an algebra problem? That is exactly what a transform does. It is an integral that turns a function of time \(t\) into a function of a new variable \(s\). The payoff is that derivatives turn into multiplication by \(s\). And here is the good news: the transform rests entirely on an idea we already know, the improper integral from Module 8. (A standard reference is Kreyszig, Advanced Engineering Mathematics, Chapter 6.)

Definition For a function \(f(t)\) defined for \(t \ge 0\), the Laplace transform of \(f\) is \[F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s)=\int_0^{\infty} e^{-st} f(t)\,dt=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_0^{b} e^{-st} f(t)\,dt\] for every \(s\) for which this limit exists.

Recall from Module 8 that an improper integral may converge or diverge. So when does this limit exist? If \(f\) grows no faster than an exponential, say \(|f(t)|\le Me^{at}\), then the integrand is at most \(Me^{-(s-a)t}\), which has a finite integral when \(s\gt a\). By the Comparison Theorem of §7.8 the transform converges for all \(s\gt a\). So every transform comes with a region of convergence \(s\gt a\); outside it the formula means nothing.

Basic transforms from the definition. For \(f(t)=e^{at}\): \(\int_0^b e^{-(s-a)t}\,dt=\frac{1-e^{-(s-a)b}}{s-a}\to\frac{1}{s-a}\) as \(b\to\infty\), provided \(s\gt a\) (for \(s\le a\) the integral diverges). Taking \(a=0\) gives \(\mathcal{L}\{1\}=1/s\) for \(s\gt 0\). For \(f(t)=t\), integrate by parts (Stewart §7.1) with \(u=t\), \(dv=e^{-st}\,dt\): \[\int_0^\infty te^{-st}\,dt=\Big[-\tfrac{t}{s}e^{-st}\Big]_0^\infty+\frac1s\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\,dt=0+\frac{1}{s^2}.\] The boundary term vanishes because an exponential beats any power (l'Hospital's Rule). Repeating the argument gives \(\mathcal{L}\{t^n\}=n!/s^{n+1}\). For \(\cos bt\) and \(\sin bt\), integrating by parts twice brings the original integral back, and you solve for it, just like \(\int e^x\sin x\,dx\) in §7.1. The results are in the table below.

\(f(t)\)\(F(s)\)valid for
\(1\)\(\frac1s\)\(s\gt 0\)
\(t^n\)\(\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}}\)\(s\gt 0\)
\(e^{at}\)\(\frac{1}{s-a}\)\(s\gt a\)
\(\cos bt\)\(\frac{s}{s^2+b^2}\)\(s\gt 0\)
\(\sin bt\)\(\frac{b}{s^2+b^2}\)\(s\gt 0\)

Rules. Integrals are linear, so \(\mathcal{L}\{\alpha f+\beta g\}=\alpha F+\beta G\). Multiplying by \(e^{at}\) just shifts the transform (the first shifting theorem): \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}f(t)\}=F(s-a)\), because \(e^{-st}e^{at}=e^{-(s-a)t}\). One integration by parts gives the rule that makes the whole method work: \(\mathcal{L}\{f'\}=sF(s)-f(0)\).

Inverse transforms and an initial-value problem. Solve \(y'+y=1\), \(y(0)=0\). Transform both sides: \(sY-0+Y=1/s\), so \(Y=\frac{1}{s(s+1)}\). Partial fractions (Stewart §7.4) give \(Y=\frac1s-\frac1{s+1}\), and reading the table backwards, \(y=1-e^{-t}\). You can check that this satisfies both the equation and the initial condition.

Common mistake Forgetting the \(-f(0)\) in \(\mathcal{L}\{f'\}\), or shifting the wrong way. Multiplying by \(e^{at}\) replaces \(s\) by \(s-a\), so \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{-2t}\cos t\}=\frac{s+2}{(s+2)^2+1}\).
Key equations
\[ \mathcal{L}\{f\}(s)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-st}f(t)\,dt \]
\[ \mathcal{L}\{t^n\}=\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}},\qquad \mathcal{L}\{e^{at}\}=\frac{1}{s-a}\ (s\gt a) \]
\[ \mathcal{L}\{\cos bt\}=\frac{s}{s^2+b^2},\qquad \mathcal{L}\{\sin bt\}=\frac{b}{s^2+b^2} \]
\[ \mathcal{L}\{e^{at}f(t)\}=F(s-a) \]
\[ \mathcal{L}\{f'\}=sF(s)-f(0) \]

BThe Fourier Transform

Now, the Laplace transform is great for signals that start at \(t = 0\). But what if we want to know which frequencies are inside a signal, like the light from a star or the sound of a guitar string? Recall that a Fourier series (Kreyszig, Chapter 11) writes a periodic signal of period \(T\) as a sum of waves \(e^{i\omega_n t}\) with frequencies \(\omega_n=2\pi n/T\). Each coefficient is an integral over one period, \(c_n=\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} f(t)e^{-i\omega_n t}\,dt\). But what about a signal that is not periodic, such as a single pulse? Let \(T\to\infty\). The allowed frequencies crowd together (their spacing \(2\pi/T\) shrinks to 0), the sum becomes an integral, and the list of coefficients becomes a function of a continuous frequency \(\omega\).

Definition The Fourier transform of \(f\) is \[F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt,\] and \(f\) is recovered by the inverse transform \(f(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega\).

This is an improper integral over the whole line (Stewart §7.8), so both \(\int_{-\infty}^0\) and \(\int_0^\infty\) must converge. Since \(|e^{-i\omega t}|=1\), it is enough that \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(t)|\,dt\) is finite. Other books put \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\) in front or measure frequency in cycles instead of radians, so always check the convention.

Even functions give real transforms. By Euler's formula, \(e^{-i\omega t}=\cos\omega t-i\sin\omega t\). If \(f\) is even, then \(f(t)\sin\omega t\) is odd and integrates to 0, while \(f(t)\cos\omega t\) is even. Hence \[F(\omega)=2\int_0^\infty f(t)\cos\omega t\,dt,\] a real, even function of \(\omega\). Every example in this module uses an even \(f\).

Rectangular pulse. Let \(f(t)=1\) for \(|t|\lt a\) and \(0\) otherwise. Then \(F(\omega)=2\int_0^a\cos\omega t\,dt=\frac{2\sin a\omega}{\omega}\) for \(\omega\ne0\), and \(F(0)=2a\), the area under the pulse. This \(\sin x/x\) shape is called a sinc function (Figures 9.3 and 9.4).

Two-sided exponential. For \(f(t)=e^{-a|t|}\) with \(a\gt 0\), the formula above gives \(F(\omega)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\cos\omega t\,dt\). That integral is exactly the Laplace transform of \(\cos\omega t\) evaluated at \(s=a\), so \(F(\omega)=\frac{2a}{a^2+\omega^2}\).

Gaussian (stated). For \(a\gt 0\), \(e^{-at^2}\) has transform \(\sqrt{\pi/a}\,e^{-\omega^2/(4a)}\): a Gaussian transforms into another Gaussian. At \(\omega=0\) this is the famous integral \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-at^2}\,dt=\sqrt{\pi/a}\).

Properties. The transform is linear. A time shift only changes the phase: \(f(t-t_0)\) has transform \(e^{-i\omega t_0}F(\omega)\), with the same size \(|F(\omega)|\). Scaling: \(f(ct)\) has transform \(\frac{1}{|c|}F(\omega/c)\).

What \(F(\omega)\) means. \(|F(\omega)|\) tells you how strongly the frequency \(\omega\) is present in \(f\). The scaling rule says that squeezing a signal in time stretches its spectrum: a short pulse has its first sinc zero at \(\omega=\pi/a\), far from 0. Short, sharp events contain many frequencies, while a long steady tone has a narrow spectrum.

Common mistake Dropping the factor 2 when you fold the integral onto \([0,\infty)\), or dividing by \(\omega\) at \(\omega=0\). At \(\omega=0\) use \(F(0)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t)\,dt\).
Key equations
\[ F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt \]
\[ f \text{ even}:\quad F(\omega)=2\int_0^{\infty}f(t)\cos\omega t\,dt \]
\[ f(t)=\begin{cases}1,&|t|\lt a\\0,&|t|\gt a\end{cases}\ \Longrightarrow\ F(\omega)=\frac{2\sin a\omega}{\omega} \]
\[ e^{-a|t|}\ \longmapsto\ \frac{2a}{a^2+\omega^2},\qquad e^{-at^2}\ \longmapsto\ \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}\,e^{-\omega^2/(4a)} \]
\[ f(t-t_0)\ \longmapsto\ e^{-i\omega t_0}F(\omega),\qquad f(ct)\ \longmapsto\ \frac{1}{|c|}F\!\left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right) \]

★Key takeaways

  • The Laplace transform \(\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s) = \int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt\) is an improper integral, so every transform comes with a region of convergence such as \(s \gt a\).
  • Basic transforms: \(\mathcal{L}\{1\} = \frac1s\), \(\mathcal{L}\{t^n\} = \frac{n!}{s^{n+1}}\), \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}\} = \frac1{s-a}\), \(\mathcal{L}\{\cos bt\} = \frac{s}{s^2+b^2}\), \(\mathcal{L}\{\sin bt\} = \frac{b}{s^2+b^2}\).
  • Multiplying by \(e^{at}\) shifts the transform: \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}f(t)\} = F(s-a)\).
  • \(\mathcal{L}\{f^{\prime}\} = sF(s) - f(0)\) turns a differential equation into algebra; partial fractions bring us back.
  • The Fourier transform \(F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt\) measures how much of each frequency is in \(f\); for even \(f\) it equals \(2\int_0^\infty f(t)\cos\omega t\,dt\).
  • Squeezing a signal in time spreads its spectrum: \(f(ct)\) has transform \(\frac{1}{|c|}F(\omega/c)\).

Example 9.1 A transform straight from the definition

Use the definition to find \(\mathcal{L}\{te^{2t}\}\) and state where it converges. Then check your answer with the first shifting theorem.

Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(t) = te^{2t}\) and \(\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s) = \int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt\).

Plan: Combine the exponentials, integrate by parts (\(u = t\)), and take the limit as the upper bound goes to infinity.

Step 1. Combine \(e^{-st}e^{2t} = e^{-(s-2)t}\) and write the improper integral as a limit.

\[\mathcal{L}\{te^{2t}\} = \int_0^\infty te^{-(s-2)t}\,dt = \lim_{b\to\infty}\int_0^b te^{-(s-2)t}\,dt\]

Step 2. Integrate by parts with \(u = t\), \(du = dt\), \(dv = e^{-(s-2)t}dt\), \(v = -\frac{1}{s-2}e^{-(s-2)t}\) (here \(s \ne 2\)).

\[\int_0^b te^{-(s-2)t}\,dt = \left[-\frac{t}{s-2}e^{-(s-2)t}\right]_0^b + \frac{1}{s-2}\int_0^b e^{-(s-2)t}\,dt = -\frac{b}{s-2}e^{-(s-2)b} + \frac{1 - e^{-(s-2)b}}{(s-2)^2}\]

Step 3. Take the limit. If \(s \gt 2\), then \(e^{-(s-2)b}\to0\) and \(be^{-(s-2)b}\to0\) (an exponential beats any power). If \(s \le 2\), the terms do not go to 0 and the integral diverges.

\[\lim_{b\to\infty}\left[-\frac{b}{s-2}e^{-(s-2)b} + \frac{1 - e^{-(s-2)b}}{(s-2)^2}\right] = 0 + \frac{1}{(s-2)^2}\]

Answer: \(\boxed{\mathcal{L}\{te^{2t}\} = \dfrac{1}{(s-2)^2},\ s \gt 2}\).

Check: From the table, \(\mathcal{L}\{t\} = \frac1{s^2}\). Multiplying by \(e^{2t}\) replaces \(s\) by \(s - 2\): \(\frac{1}{(s-2)^2}\). ✓

Example 9.2 Solving an initial-value problem

Use the Laplace transform to solve \(y^{\prime} + 3y = 2e^{-t}\), \(y(0) = 4\).

Show solution (try it first)

Given: A first-order linear differential equation with \(y(0) = 4\).

Plan: Transform both sides (using \(\mathcal{L}\{y^{\prime}\} = sY - y(0)\)), solve for \(Y(s)\), split with partial fractions, then read the inverse transforms from the table.

Step 1. Transform both sides. \(\mathcal{L}\{y^{\prime}\} = sY - 4\) and \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{-t}\} = \frac{1}{s+1}\).

\[sY - 4 + 3Y = \frac{2}{s + 1}\]

Step 2. Solve for \(Y\).

\[(s + 3)Y = 4 + \frac{2}{s+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Y = \frac{4}{s + 3} + \frac{2}{(s + 1)(s + 3)}\]

Step 3. Partial fractions for the second term: \(\frac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)} = \frac{A}{s+1} + \frac{B}{s+3}\), so \(2 = A(s+3) + B(s+1)\). At \(s = -1\): \(A = 1\). At \(s = -3\): \(B = -1\).

\[Y = \frac{4}{s+3} + \frac{1}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+3} = \frac{1}{s+1} + \frac{3}{s+3}\]

Step 4. Invert using \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac1{s-a}\right\} = e^{at}\).

\[y(t) = e^{-t} + 3e^{-3t}\]

Answer: \(\boxed{y(t) = e^{-t} + 3e^{-3t}}\).

Check: \(y(0) = 1 + 3 = 4\) ✓. \(y^{\prime} = -e^{-t} - 9e^{-3t}\), so \(y^{\prime} + 3y = -e^{-t} - 9e^{-3t} + 3e^{-t} + 9e^{-3t} = 2e^{-t}\) ✓.

Example 9.3 The Fourier transform of a two-sided exponential

Find the Fourier transform of \(f(t) = e^{-3|t|}\).

Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(t) = e^{-3|t|}\), an even function that decays on both sides.

Plan: Because \(f\) is even, \(F(\omega) = 2\int_0^\infty f(t)\cos\omega t\,dt\). This integral is a Laplace transform we already know.

Step 1. Use the formula for even functions. For \(t \ge 0\), \(|t| = t\).

\[F(\omega) = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-3t}\cos\omega t\,dt\]

Step 2. Notice that \(\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\cos\omega t\,dt = \mathcal{L}\{\cos\omega t\} = \frac{s}{s^2+\omega^2}\). Here \(s = 3\).

\[\int_0^\infty e^{-3t}\cos\omega t\,dt = \frac{3}{9 + \omega^2}\]

Step 3. Multiply by 2.

\[F(\omega) = \frac{6}{9 + \omega^2}\]

Answer: \(\boxed{F(\omega) = \dfrac{6}{9 + \omega^2}}\), which matches the general rule \(e^{-a|t|}\mapsto\frac{2a}{a^2+\omega^2}\) with \(a = 3\).

Check: \(F(0)\) should be the total area under \(f\): \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-3|t|}\,dt = 2\cdot\frac13 = \frac23\), and \(\frac{6}{9} = \frac23\). ✓

Example 9.4 The Fourier transform of a triangular pulse

Find the Fourier transform of the triangular pulse \(f(t) = 1 - |t|\) for \(|t| \le 1\) and \(f(t) = 0\) for \(|t| \gt 1\). What is \(F(0)\)?

Show solution (try it first)

Given: An even function that is zero outside \([-1, 1]\).

Plan: Use \(F(\omega) = 2\int_0^1(1 - t)\cos\omega t\,dt\) and integrate by parts with \(u = 1 - t\).

Step 1. For \(0 \le t \le 1\), \(f(t) = 1 - t\), and \(f = 0\) beyond 1.

\[F(\omega) = 2\int_0^1(1 - t)\cos\omega t\,dt\]

Step 2. Integrate by parts: \(u = 1 - t\), \(du = -dt\), \(dv = \cos\omega t\,dt\), \(v = \frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega}\) (for \(\omega \ne 0\)). The boundary term is 0 at both ends (at \(t = 1\) because \(1 - t = 0\), at \(t = 0\) because \(\sin 0 = 0\)).

\[\int_0^1(1-t)\cos\omega t\,dt = \Big[(1-t)\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega}\Big]_0^1 + \frac1\omega\int_0^1\sin\omega t\,dt = \frac1\omega\left[-\frac{\cos\omega t}{\omega}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1 - \cos\omega}{\omega^2}\]

Step 3. Multiply by 2 and use \(1 - \cos\omega = 2\sin^2(\omega/2)\).

\[F(\omega) = \frac{2(1 - \cos\omega)}{\omega^2} = \frac{4\sin^2(\omega/2)}{\omega^2}\]

Step 4. At \(\omega = 0\) we cannot divide by \(\omega\), so use \(F(0) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t)\,dt\), the area of the triangle with base 2 and height 1.

\[F(0) = \tfrac12(2)(1) = 1\]

Answer: \(\boxed{F(\omega) = \dfrac{4\sin^2(\omega/2)}{\omega^2}\ (\omega \ne 0),\quad F(0) = 1}\).

Check: As \(\omega\to0\), \(\sin(\omega/2) \approx \omega/2\), so \(F(\omega) \approx \frac{4(\omega/2)^2}{\omega^2} = 1\), matching \(F(0)\). ✓
