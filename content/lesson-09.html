AThe Laplace Transform

Many problems in physics and engineering become easier after a transform: an integral that turns a function of time \(t\) into a function of a new variable \(s\). The payoff is that derivatives turn into multiplication by \(s\), so a differential equation turns into an algebra problem. This topic is not in Stewart, but it rests entirely on an idea you already know: the improper integral of Stewart §7.8. (A standard reference is Kreyszig, Advanced Engineering Mathematics, Chapter 6.)

Definition For a function \(f(t)\) defined for \(t \ge 0\), the Laplace transform of \(f\) is \[F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s)=\int_0^{\infty} e^{-st} f(t)\,dt=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_0^{b} e^{-st} f(t)\,dt\] for every \(s\) for which this limit exists.

When does the limit exist? If \(f\) grows no faster than an exponential, say \(|f(t)|\le Me^{at}\), then the integrand is at most \(Me^{-(s-a)t}\), which has a finite integral when \(s\gt a\). By the Comparison Theorem of §7.8 the transform converges for all \(s\gt a\). So every transform comes with a region of convergence \(s\gt a\); outside it the formula means nothing.

Basic transforms from the definition. For \(f(t)=e^{at}\): \(\int_0^b e^{-(s-a)t}\,dt=\frac{1-e^{-(s-a)b}}{s-a}\to\frac{1}{s-a}\) as \(b\to\infty\), provided \(s\gt a\) (for \(s\le a\) the integral diverges). Taking \(a=0\) gives \(\mathcal{L}\{1\}=1/s\) for \(s\gt 0\). For \(f(t)=t\), integrate by parts (Stewart §7.1) with \(u=t\), \(dv=e^{-st}\,dt\): \[\int_0^\infty te^{-st}\,dt=\Big[-\tfrac{t}{s}e^{-st}\Big]_0^\infty+\frac1s\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\,dt=0+\frac{1}{s^2}.\] The boundary term vanishes because an exponential beats any power (l'Hospital's Rule). Repeating the argument gives \(\mathcal{L}\{t^n\}=n!/s^{n+1}\). For \(\cos bt\) and \(\sin bt\), integrating by parts twice brings the original integral back, and you solve for it, just like \(\int e^x\sin x\,dx\) in §7.1. The results are in the table below.

\(f(t)\)\(F(s)\)valid for
\(1\)\(\frac1s\)\(s\gt 0\)
\(t^n\)\(\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}}\)\(s\gt 0\)
\(e^{at}\)\(\frac{1}{s-a}\)\(s\gt a\)
\(\cos bt\)\(\frac{s}{s^2+b^2}\)\(s\gt 0\)
\(\sin bt\)\(\frac{b}{s^2+b^2}\)\(s\gt 0\)

Rules. Integrals are linear, so \(\mathcal{L}\{\alpha f+\beta g\}=\alpha F+\beta G\). Multiplying by \(e^{at}\) just shifts the transform (the first shifting theorem): \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}f(t)\}=F(s-a)\), because \(e^{-st}e^{at}=e^{-(s-a)t}\). One integration by parts gives the rule that makes the whole method work: \(\mathcal{L}\{f'\}=sF(s)-f(0)\).

Inverse transforms and an initial-value problem. Solve \(y'+y=1\), \(y(0)=0\). Transform both sides: \(sY-0+Y=1/s\), so \(Y=\frac{1}{s(s+1)}\). Partial fractions (Stewart §7.4) give \(Y=\frac1s-\frac1{s+1}\), and reading the table backwards, \(y=1-e^{-t}\). You can check that this satisfies both the equation and the initial condition.

Common mistake Forgetting the \(-f(0)\) in \(\mathcal{L}\{f'\}\), or shifting the wrong way. Multiplying by \(e^{at}\) replaces \(s\) by \(s-a\), so \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{-2t}\cos t\}=\frac{s+2}{(s+2)^2+1}\).
Key equations
\[ \mathcal{L}\{f\}(s)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-st}f(t)\,dt \]
\[ \mathcal{L}\{t^n\}=\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}},\qquad \mathcal{L}\{e^{at}\}=\frac{1}{s-a}\ (s\gt a) \]
\[ \mathcal{L}\{\cos bt\}=\frac{s}{s^2+b^2},\qquad \mathcal{L}\{\sin bt\}=\frac{b}{s^2+b^2} \]
\[ \mathcal{L}\{e^{at}f(t)\}=F(s-a) \]
\[ \mathcal{L}\{f'\}=sF(s)-f(0) \]

BThe Fourier Transform

A Fourier series (Kreyszig, Chapter 11) writes a periodic signal of period \(T\) as a sum of waves \(e^{i\omega_n t}\) with frequencies \(\omega_n=2\pi n/T\). Each coefficient is an integral over one period, \(c_n=\frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} f(t)e^{-i\omega_n t}\,dt\). But what about a signal that is not periodic, such as a single pulse? Let \(T\to\infty\). The allowed frequencies crowd together (their spacing \(2\pi/T\) shrinks to 0), the sum becomes an integral, and the list of coefficients becomes a function of a continuous frequency \(\omega\).

Definition The Fourier transform of \(f\) is \[F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt,\] and \(f\) is recovered by the inverse transform \(f(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega\).

This is an improper integral over the whole line (Stewart §7.8), so both \(\int_{-\infty}^0\) and \(\int_0^\infty\) must converge. Since \(|e^{-i\omega t}|=1\), it is enough that \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(t)|\,dt\) is finite. Other books put \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\) in front or measure frequency in cycles instead of radians, so always check the convention.

Even functions give real transforms. By Euler's formula, \(e^{-i\omega t}=\cos\omega t-i\sin\omega t\). If \(f\) is even, then \(f(t)\sin\omega t\) is odd and integrates to 0, while \(f(t)\cos\omega t\) is even. Hence \[F(\omega)=2\int_0^\infty f(t)\cos\omega t\,dt,\] a real, even function of \(\omega\). Every example in this module uses an even \(f\).

Rectangular pulse. Let \(f(t)=1\) for \(|t|\lt a\) and \(0\) otherwise. Then \(F(\omega)=2\int_0^a\cos\omega t\,dt=\frac{2\sin a\omega}{\omega}\) for \(\omega\ne0\), and \(F(0)=2a\), the area under the pulse. This \(\sin x/x\) shape is called a sinc function (Figures 9.3 and 9.4).

Two-sided exponential. For \(f(t)=e^{-a|t|}\) with \(a\gt 0\), the formula above gives \(F(\omega)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\cos\omega t\,dt\). That integral is exactly the Laplace transform of \(\cos\omega t\) evaluated at \(s=a\), so \(F(\omega)=\frac{2a}{a^2+\omega^2}\).

Gaussian (stated). For \(a\gt 0\), \(e^{-at^2}\) has transform \(\sqrt{\pi/a}\,e^{-\omega^2/(4a)}\): a Gaussian transforms into another Gaussian. At \(\omega=0\) this is the famous integral \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-at^2}\,dt=\sqrt{\pi/a}\).

Properties. The transform is linear. A time shift only changes the phase: \(f(t-t_0)\) has transform \(e^{-i\omega t_0}F(\omega)\), with the same size \(|F(\omega)|\). Scaling: \(f(ct)\) has transform \(\frac{1}{|c|}F(\omega/c)\).

What \(F(\omega)\) means. \(|F(\omega)|\) tells you how strongly the frequency \(\omega\) is present in \(f\). The scaling rule says that squeezing a signal in time stretches its spectrum: a short pulse has its first sinc zero at \(\omega=\pi/a\), far from 0. Short, sharp events contain many frequencies, while a long steady tone has a narrow spectrum.

Common mistake Dropping the factor 2 when you fold the integral onto \([0,\infty)\), or dividing by \(\omega\) at \(\omega=0\). At \(\omega=0\) use \(F(0)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t)\,dt\).
Key equations
\[ F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt \]
\[ f \text{ even}:\quad F(\omega)=2\int_0^{\infty}f(t)\cos\omega t\,dt \]
\[ f(t)=\begin{cases}1,&|t|\lt a\\0,&|t|\gt a\end{cases}\ \Longrightarrow\ F(\omega)=\frac{2\sin a\omega}{\omega} \]
\[ e^{-a|t|}\ \longmapsto\ \frac{2a}{a^2+\omega^2},\qquad e^{-at^2}\ \longmapsto\ \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}\,e^{-\omega^2/(4a)} \]
\[ f(t-t_0)\ \longmapsto\ e^{-i\omega t_0}F(\omega),\qquad f(ct)\ \longmapsto\ \frac{1}{|c|}F\!\left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right) \]

★Key takeaways

  • The Laplace transform \(F(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt\) is an improper integral; it exists for \(s\gt a\) when \(f\) grows no faster than \(e^{at}\).
  • The basic table (\(1/s\), \(n!/s^{n+1}\), \(1/(s-a)\), \(s/(s^2+b^2)\), \(b/(s^2+b^2)\)) comes from integration by parts, and linearity plus shifting extend it.
  • Because \(\mathcal{L}\{y'\}=sY-y(0)\), an initial-value problem becomes an algebra problem; partial fractions and the table bring you back to \(y(t)\).
  • The Fourier transform \(F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt\) is the limit of a Fourier series as the period grows without bound.
  • For an even \(f\), \(F(\omega)=2\int_0^\infty f(t)\cos\omega t\,dt\) is real; a pulse gives a sinc and \(e^{-a|t|}\) gives \(2a/(a^2+\omega^2)\).
  • Squeezing a signal in time spreads its spectrum in frequency, and a time shift changes only the phase.

Example 9.1 A transform straight from the definition

Use the definition to find \(\mathcal{L}\{te^{2t}\}\) and state where it converges. Then check your answer with the first shifting theorem.

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Combine the exponentials into \(e^{-(s-2)t}\) and integrate by parts with \(u=t\). Work on \([0,b]\) and let \(b\to\infty\).

Solution:

Write \(k=s-2\). Then \(\mathcal{L}\{te^{2t}\}=\int_0^\infty te^{-kt}\,dt\). With \(u=t\), \(dv=e^{-kt}\,dt\) (so \(v=-e^{-kt}/k\)):

\[\int_0^b te^{-kt}\,dt=\Big[-\frac{t}{k}e^{-kt}\Big]_0^b+\frac1k\int_0^b e^{-kt}\,dt=-\frac{b}{k}e^{-kb}+\frac{1-e^{-kb}}{k^2}.\]

If \(k\gt 0\), both \(be^{-kb}\) and \(e^{-kb}\) tend to 0 as \(b\to\infty\), so the limit is \(1/k^2\). If \(k\le0\), the integrand does not decay and the integral diverges. Hence \[\mathcal{L}\{te^{2t}\}=\frac{1}{(s-2)^2},\qquad s\gt 2.\]

Check:

\(\mathcal{L}\{t\}=1/s^2\). Multiplying by \(e^{2t}\) replaces \(s\) by \(s-2\), giving \(1/(s-2)^2\) for \(s\gt 2\). The two methods agree.

Example 9.2 Solving an initial-value problem

Use the Laplace transform to solve \(y'+3y=2e^{-t}\), \(y(0)=4\).

Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Transform the equation, using \(\mathcal{L}\{y'\}=sY-y(0)\). Solve for \(Y(s)\), split it with partial fractions, and invert with the table.

Solution:

Transforming: \(sY-4+3Y=\frac{2}{s+1}\), so \[Y=\frac{4}{s+3}+\frac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)}.\] Partial fractions: \(\frac{2}{(s+1)(s+3)}=\frac{A}{s+1}+\frac{B}{s+3}\) with \(2=A(s+3)+B(s+1)\). Setting \(s=-1\) gives \(A=1\); setting \(s=-3\) gives \(B=-1\). So \[Y=\frac{1}{s+1}+\frac{3}{s+3}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y(t)=e^{-t}+3e^{-3t}.\]

Check:

\(y(0)=1+3=4\). Also \(y'=-e^{-t}-9e^{-3t}\), so \(y'+3y=-e^{-t}-9e^{-3t}+3e^{-t}+9e^{-3t}=2e^{-t}\). ✓

Example 9.3 The Fourier transform of a triangular pulse

Find the Fourier transform of the triangular pulse \(f(t)=1-|t|\) for \(|t|\le1\) and \(f(t)=0\) for \(|t|\gt 1\). What is \(F(0)\)?

(Kreyszig, Advanced Engineering Mathematics, §11.9 (similar idea))
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

\(f\) is even, so \(F(\omega)=2\int_0^1(1-t)\cos\omega t\,dt\). Integrate by parts with \(u=1-t\).

Solution:

With \(u=1-t\), \(dv=\cos\omega t\,dt\), \(v=\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega}\):

\[\int_0^1(1-t)\cos\omega t\,dt=\Big[(1-t)\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega}\Big]_0^1+\frac1\omega\int_0^1\sin\omega t\,dt=0+\frac{1-\cos\omega}{\omega^2}.\]

So \[F(\omega)=\frac{2(1-\cos\omega)}{\omega^2}=\frac{4\sin^2(\omega/2)}{\omega^2}\qquad(\omega\ne0),\] using \(1-\cos\omega=2\sin^2(\omega/2)\).

Check:

As \(\omega\to0\), \(\sin(\omega/2)\approx\omega/2\), so \(F(\omega)\to4\cdot\frac{\omega^2/4}{\omega^2}=1\). That matches \(F(0)=\int_{-1}^1(1-|t|)\,dt=1\), the area of the triangle. Notice that \(F(\omega)\ge0\) and decays like \(1/\omega^2\), faster than the sinc of a rectangular pulse, because the triangle has no jumps.
