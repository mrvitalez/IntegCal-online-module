10.1Curves Defined by Parametric Equations

So far, every curve we have studied was the graph of a function \(y = f(x)\). But what about a circle, or a loop, or the path of a point on a rolling wheel? These fail the vertical line test. So, how do we describe them? We let both coordinates depend on a third variable (Stewart §10.1).

A parametric equation represents a curve by expressing both \(x\) and \(y\) as functions of a third variable, usually \(t\), called the parameter:

\[ x = f(t), \qquad y = g(t). \qquad (1) \]

The set of points \((x, y)\) that we get as \(t\) varies forms a parametric curve. Parametric equations are useful for representing curves that cannot be expressed as a single function \(y = f(x)\).

Sketching with a table. Take \(x = t^2 - 2t,\ y = t + 1\). First, we assume that \(t\) can be any real number, and we make a table: \(t = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2\) give the points \((8, -1), (3, 0), (0, 1), (-1, 2), (0, 3)\). Then let's get rid of \(t\). From \(y = t + 1\) we get \(t = y - 1\). If we substitute it into \(x\), we get \(x = (y-1)^2 - 2(y-1)\). So our curve is a parabola that opens to the right (Figure 11.1).

A definite interval. Sometimes \(t\) is restricted to a finite interval. In general, the curve

\[ x = f(t), \qquad y = g(t), \qquad a \le t \le b \qquad (2) \]

has initial point \((f(a), g(a))\) and terminal point \((f(b), g(b))\). Take note that the equations tell us not only where the curve is, but also the direction in which it is traced as \(t\) increases.

Circles. If \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t,\ 0 \le t \le 2\pi\), the points start at \((1, 0)\), go to \((0, 1)\), \((-1, 0)\), \((0, -1)\) and come back. We can prove it is the unit circle: \(x^2 + y^2 = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\). Now, what if we want a circle with center \((h, k)\) and radius \(r\)? Multiplying by \(r\) makes the circle bigger or smaller, and adding \(h\) and \(k\) moves it, just like in algebra:

\[ x = h + r\cos t, \qquad y = k + r\sin t, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi. \]

For example, \(x = 3 + 2\cos t,\ y = 4 + 2\sin t\) is the circle with center \((3, 4)\) and radius 2.

Common mistake The Cartesian equation alone loses information. \(x = \sin 2t,\ y = \cos 2t\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\), also gives \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\), but it starts at \((0, 1)\) and goes around twice, clockwise (Example 2). Also watch for restricted ranges: \(x = \sqrt{t}\) forces \(x \ge 0\), so only half of a parabola may be traced.

The cycloid. Roll a circle of radius \(r\) along the \(x\)-axis and follow one point on its rim. If \(\theta\) is the angle through which the circle has turned, the circle has rolled a distance \(r\theta\), and the point is at

\[ x = r(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad y = r(1 - \cos\theta). \]

One arch corresponds to \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\) (Figure 11.2). Notice that writing \(y\) as a function of \(x\) here is hopeless. That is exactly why parametric equations are so useful.

Try it in Desmos Open the free graphing calculator at https://www.desmos.com/calculator and type (t^2 - 2t, t + 1) as an ordered pair; Desmos treats it as a parametric curve. Then type (a^2 - 2a, a + 1) and accept the slider Desmos offers for \(a\); the slider plays the role of \(t\). Drag it and watch the point travel along the curve. You can also change the \(t\)-range under the curve to see only part of it. Repeat with the cycloid (t - sin t, 1 - cos t) and with the two circle parametrizations above.
Key equations
\[ x = f(t),\quad y = g(t),\quad a \le t \le b \]
\[ x = h + r\cos t,\quad y = k + r\sin t,\quad 0 \le t \le 2\pi \]
\[ x = r(\theta - \sin\theta),\quad y = r(1 - \cos\theta) \]

10.2Calculus with Parametric Curves

When we describe a curve using parametric equations, both \(x\) and \(y\) are expressed in terms of a parameter \(t\). Here \(t\) can represent time, an angle, or another quantity. The advantage of this form is that it can represent curves that are not functions in the usual sense, such as circles, ellipses or loops. Can we still do calculus on them without eliminating \(t\)? Yes. Everything comes from the Chain Rule (Stewart §10.2).

Differentiation. To find the slope of the curve, we use the chain rule. Since \(y = g(t)\) and \(x = f(t)\),

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}, \qquad \text{provided that } \frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0. \qquad (3) \]

So, what does equation (3) mean? \(dx/dt\) measures how fast the \(x\)-coordinate changes with respect to \(t\), \(dy/dt\) measures how fast the \(y\)-coordinate changes, and their ratio measures how steeply \(y\) changes with \(x\): the slope of the curve at that instant. This tells us where the tangents are special:

  • Horizontal tangent where \(dy/dt = 0\) and \(dx/dt \ne 0\).
  • Vertical tangent where \(dx/dt = 0\) and \(dy/dt \ne 0\).
  • If both are 0, the formula says nothing; we have to look more carefully (a cusp is possible, as on the cycloid).

Tangent line. At the point \(P(x(t_0), y(t_0))\), the tangent line is

\[ y - y(t_0) = \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{t = t_0}\big(x - x(t_0)\big). \qquad (4) \]

Second derivative. To determine concavity, we need \(d^2y/dx^2\), the derivative of \(dy/dx\) with respect to x. So let's apply equation (3) again, with \(dy/dx\) playing the role of \(y\):

\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac{\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt}. \qquad (5) \]

Common mistake \(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\) is not \(\frac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}\). Differentiate \(dy/dx\) with respect to \(t\), then divide by \(dx/dt\) one more time.

Area. If the curve is traced once from \(t = \alpha\) to \(t = \beta\), the area under it is \(A = \int y\,dx\). We substitute \(y = g(t)\) and \(dx = f'(t)\,dt\):

\[ A = \int y\,dx = \int_\alpha^\beta g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt. \]

Choose the limits so that \(x\) runs from left to right. For one arch of the cycloid this gives \(3\pi r^2\) (Example 5).

Arc length. A tiny piece of the curve has horizontal change \(dx\) and vertical change \(dy\), so by the Pythagorean theorem its length is \(\sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2}\). That gives

\[ L = \int_\alpha^\beta \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt. \]

Let's test it on the unit circle \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t,\ 0 \le t \le 2\pi\): the integrand is \(\sqrt{\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t} = 1\), so \(L = 2\pi\), the circumference. Take note that the curve must be traced exactly once: \(x = \sin 2t,\ y = \cos 2t\) over \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\) gives \(4\pi\), twice the true length. For the arc \(x = 3t^2,\ y = 2t^3,\ 0 \le t \le 1\) (Figure 11.4), the integrand is \(6t\sqrt{1 + t^2}\) and \(L = 4\sqrt2 - 2\).

Surface area. If the curve is rotated, each tiny piece sweeps a thin band whose area is about (circumference) \(\times\) (length) \(= 2\pi(\text{radius})\,ds\), where \(ds = \sqrt{(dx/dt)^2 + (dy/dt)^2}\,dt\). So

\[ S = \int 2\pi y\,ds \ \text{(about the } x\text{-axis)}, \qquad S = \int 2\pi x\,ds \ \text{(about the } y\text{-axis)}. \]

The radius is the distance from the axis of rotation, so we pick \(y\) or \(x\) accordingly. In Example 6 we use this to show that a sphere of radius \(r\) has area \(4\pi r^2\).

Key equations
\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} \]
\[ y - y(t_0) = \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{t=t_0}\big(x - x(t_0)\big) \]
\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt} \]
\[ A = \int_\alpha^\beta g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt \]
\[ L = \int_\alpha^\beta \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt \]
\[ S = \int_\alpha^\beta 2\pi y \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt \]

★Key takeaways

  • A parametric curve x = f(t), y = g(t) records both the path and the direction in which it is traced, from the initial point (f(a), g(a)) to the terminal point (f(b), g(b)).
  • Eliminating the parameter identifies the curve, but we must still check which part is traced and how many times.
  • x = h + r cos t, y = k + r sin t is the circle with center (h, k) and radius r.
  • The slope is dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt): horizontal tangents where dy/dt = 0, vertical where dx/dt = 0.
  • For d²y/dx², differentiate dy/dx with respect to t and divide by dx/dt again.
  • Area is ∫ y dx = ∫ g(t) f′(t) dt, arc length is ∫ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt, and surface area is ∫ 2π(radius) ds.

Example 11.1 Sketching a parametric curve on an interval

Sketch and identify the curve \(x = t^2 - 2t,\ y = t + 1\) for \(0 \le t \le 4\). Give its initial and terminal points and its direction.

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 5, Example 1 (Stewart §10.1))

Given
  • \(x = t^2 - 2t\), \(y = t + 1\)
  • Interval: \(0 \le t \le 4\)
  • Asked: the shape of the curve, the initial and terminal points, the direction.
Step 1: Make a table of values

Let's substitute \(t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4\) into both equations.

Step 2: Eliminate the parameter

Solve the simpler equation for \(t\), then substitute it into the other one.

\[ y = t + 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; t = y - 1 \]

\[ x = (y-1)^2 - 2(y-1) \]

\[ x = y^2 - 2y + 1 - 2y + 2 = y^2 - 4y + 3 \]

This is a parabola that opens to the right. Its vertex is where \(y = 2\): \(x = 4 - 8 + 3 = -1\), the point \((-1, 2)\).

Step 3: Read off the endpoints and the direction

The initial point comes from \(t = 0\) and the terminal point from \(t = 4\).

\[ t = 0:\ (0, 1), \qquad t = 4:\ (8, 5) \]

Since \(y = t + 1\) increases with \(t\), the point moves upward along the parabola.

Answer

The curve is the part of the parabola \(x = y^2 - 4y + 3\) from the initial point \((0, 1)\) to the terminal point \((8, 5)\), traced upward, passing through the vertex \((-1, 2)\) at \(t = 1\) (compare Figure 11.1).

Check

Try \(t = 3\) in the Cartesian equation: \(y = 4\) gives \(x = 16 - 16 + 3 = 3\), which matches the table.

Example 11.2 Same circle, different curve

What curve is represented by \(x = \sin 2t,\ y = \cos 2t\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\)? How is it different from \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\)?

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 5, Examples 2–3 (Stewart §10.1))
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(x = \sin 2t\), \(y = \cos 2t\), with \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\) (radians)
  • Asked: the curve, and how it compares with \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\).
Step 1: Eliminate the parameter with an identity

Let's square both equations and add, because \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\).

\[ x^2 + y^2 = \sin^2 2t + \cos^2 2t = 1 \]

So every point lies on the unit circle, just like in \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\).

Step 2: Make a table to see the motion
\(t\)\(x = \sin 2t\)\(y = \cos 2t\)
001
\(\pi/4\)10
\(\pi/2\)0\(-1\)
\(3\pi/4\)\(-1\)0
\(\pi\)01

The point starts at the top, \((0, 1)\), moves to the right side, then the bottom: that is clockwise. At \(t = \pi\) it is already back at \((0, 1)\).

Step 3: Count the trips around

One trip takes \(\pi\) units of \(t\), and our interval has length \(2\pi\).

\[ \frac{2\pi}{\pi} = 2 \text{ trips} \]

For \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\), the point starts at \((1, 0)\), moves to \((0, 1)\) (counterclockwise) and goes around once.

Answer

Both describe the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\). But \(x = \sin 2t,\ y = \cos 2t\) starts at \((0, 1)\) and goes around twice, clockwise, while \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\) starts at \((1, 0)\) and goes around once, counterclockwise.

Example 11.3 Two tangents at one point, and concavity

A curve \(C\) is defined by \(x = t^2,\ y = t^3 - 3t\). (a) Show that \(C\) has two tangents at the point \((3, 0)\) and find their equations. (b) Find the points where the tangent is horizontal or vertical. (c) Determine where the curve is concave upward or downward.

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 5, Example 5 (Stewart §10.2))
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(x = t^2\), \(y = t^3 - 3t\)
  • Asked: the tangents at \((3, 0)\); the horizontal and vertical tangents; the concavity.
Step 1: Find the values of t at (3, 0)

Substitute \(x = 3\) into \(x = t^2\).

\[ t^2 = 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; t = \sqrt3 \ \text{or}\ t = -\sqrt3 \]

Check \(y\) for both values:

\[ y = (\pm\sqrt3)^3 - 3(\pm\sqrt3) = \pm 3\sqrt3 \mp 3\sqrt3 = 0 \]

Two different values of \(t\) give the same point, so the curve passes through \((3, 0)\) twice. That is why there can be two tangents.

Step 2: Find dy/dx

Get the two rates separately, then divide (equation 3).

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = 2t, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2 - 3 \]

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3t^2 - 3}{2t} = \frac32\left(t - \frac1t\right) \]

Step 3: Get the two slopes and the tangent lines

Substitute each value of \(t\).

\[ t = \sqrt3:\quad m = \frac{3(3) - 3}{2\sqrt3} = \frac{6}{2\sqrt3} = \sqrt3 \]

\[ t = -\sqrt3:\quad m = \frac{6}{-2\sqrt3} = -\sqrt3 \]

Using equation (4) with the point \((3, 0)\):

\[ y = \sqrt3\,(x - 3) \quad\text{and}\quad y = -\sqrt3\,(x - 3) \]

Step 4: Horizontal and vertical tangents

Horizontal: set \(dy/dt = 0\) and check \(dx/dt \ne 0\).

\[ 3t^2 - 3 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; t = \pm 1, \qquad dx/dt = \pm 2 \ne 0 \]

\[ t = 1:\ (1, 1 - 3) = (1, -2), \qquad t = -1:\ (1, -1 + 3) = (1, 2) \]

Vertical: set \(dx/dt = 2t = 0\), so \(t = 0\). There \(dy/dt = -3 \ne 0\), and the point is \((0, 0)\).

Step 5: Concavity with equation (5)

Differentiate \(dy/dx\) with respect to \(t\), then divide by \(dx/dt\).

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac32\left(1 + \frac{1}{t^2}\right) = \frac{3(t^2 + 1)}{2t^2} \]

\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{3(t^2+1)/(2t^2)}{2t} = \frac{3(t^2 + 1)}{4t^3} \]

The numerator is always positive, so the sign is the sign of \(t^3\).

Answer

(a) The tangents at \((3, 0)\) are \(y = \sqrt3(x - 3)\) and \(y = -\sqrt3(x - 3)\). (b) Horizontal tangents at \((1, -2)\) and \((1, 2)\); a vertical tangent at \((0, 0)\). (c) Concave upward for \(t \gt 0\) and concave downward for \(t \lt 0\) (Figure 11.3).

Example 11.4 Tangent line to a cycloid

(a) Find the tangent to the cycloid \(x = r(\theta - \sin\theta),\ y = r(1 - \cos\theta)\) at the point where \(\theta = \pi/3\). (b) At what points is the tangent horizontal? When is it vertical?

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 5, Example 6 (Stewart §10.2))
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(x = r(\theta - \sin\theta)\), \(y = r(1 - \cos\theta)\), with \(r \gt 0\) constant
  • Asked: the tangent at \(\theta = \pi/3\); where the tangent is horizontal or vertical.
Step 1: Find dy/dx

Here the parameter is \(\theta\), so we differentiate with respect to \(\theta\).

\[ \frac{dx}{d\theta} = r(1 - \cos\theta), \qquad \frac{dy}{d\theta} = r\sin\theta \]

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{r\sin\theta}{r(1 - \cos\theta)} = \frac{\sin\theta}{1 - \cos\theta} \]

Step 2: Evaluate the slope at θ = π/3

Recall \(\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and \(\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12\).

\[ m = \frac{\sqrt3/2}{1 - 1/2} = \frac{\sqrt3/2}{1/2} = \sqrt3 \]

Step 3: Find the point

\[ x = r\left(\frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right), \qquad y = r\left(1 - \frac12\right) = \frac{r}{2} \]

Step 4: Write the tangent line with equation (4)

\[ y - \frac{r}{2} = \sqrt3\left(x - \frac{r\pi}{3} + \frac{r\sqrt3}{2}\right) \]

\[ y - \frac{r}{2} = \sqrt3\,x - \frac{r\pi}{\sqrt3} + \frac{3r}{2} \]

\[ y = \sqrt3\,x + r\left(2 - \frac{\pi}{\sqrt3}\right) \]

Step 5: Horizontal and vertical tangents

Horizontal: \(dy/d\theta = r\sin\theta = 0\) and \(dx/d\theta \ne 0\). This happens at \(\theta = \pi, 3\pi, 5\pi, \ldots\), that is \(\theta = (2n-1)\pi\), where \(\cos\theta = -1\):

\[ (x, y) = \big((2n-1)\pi r,\ 2r\big) \]

These are the tops of the arches. At \(\theta = 2n\pi\), both \(dx/d\theta\) and \(dy/d\theta\) are 0, so the formula says nothing. But \(\frac{\sin\theta}{1 - \cos\theta} \to \infty\) as \(\theta \to 2n\pi^+\), so the tangent is vertical there.

Answer

(a) The tangent at \(\theta = \pi/3\) is \(y = \sqrt3\,x + r\left(2 - \frac{\pi}{\sqrt3}\right)\), or equivalently \(\sqrt3\,x - y = r\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt3} - 2\right)\). (b) The tangent is horizontal at the points \(((2n-1)\pi r, 2r)\) and vertical at the cusps \((2n\pi r, 0)\).

Example 11.5 Area under one arch of the cycloid

Find the area under one arch of the cycloid \(x = r(\theta - \sin\theta),\ y = r(1 - \cos\theta)\).

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 5, Example 7 (Stewart §10.2))
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(x = r(\theta - \sin\theta)\), \(y = r(1 - \cos\theta)\)
  • One arch: \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\) (\(x\) runs from 0 to \(2\pi r\))
  • Asked: \(A = \int y\,dx\).
Step 1: Write dx in terms of dθ

\[ \frac{dx}{d\theta} = r(1 - \cos\theta) \;\Longrightarrow\; dx = r(1 - \cos\theta)\,d\theta \]

Step 2: Substitute into the area formula

\[ A = \int_0^{2\pi} r(1 - \cos\theta)\cdot r(1 - \cos\theta)\,d\theta = r^2\int_0^{2\pi} (1 - \cos\theta)^2\,d\theta \]

Step 3: Expand and use the half-angle identity

Recall \(\cos^2\theta = \frac12(1 + \cos 2\theta)\).

\[ (1 - \cos\theta)^2 = 1 - 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1 - 2\cos\theta + \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos 2\theta \]

\[ = \tfrac32 - 2\cos\theta + \tfrac12\cos 2\theta \]

Step 4: Integrate and substitute the limits

\[ A = r^2\left[\tfrac32\theta - 2\sin\theta + \tfrac14\sin 2\theta\right]_0^{2\pi} \]

Both sine terms are 0 at \(0\) and at \(2\pi\).

\[ A = r^2\left(\tfrac32 \cdot 2\pi\right) = 3\pi r^2 \]

Answer

The area under one arch is \(3\pi r^2\) square units: three times the area of the rolling circle.

Example 11.6 Surface area of a sphere

Show that the surface area of a sphere of radius \(r\) is \(4\pi r^2\).

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 5, Example 9 (Stewart §10.2))
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • A sphere of radius \(r\) is made by rotating the upper half of the circle of radius \(r\) about the \(x\)-axis.
  • Upper half: \(x = r\cos t\), \(y = r\sin t\), \(0 \le t \le \pi\)
  • Asked: \(S = \int 2\pi y\,ds\).
Step 1: Find ds

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = -r\sin t, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = r\cos t \]

\[ \left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2 = r^2\sin^2 t + r^2\cos^2 t = r^2 \]

\[ ds = \sqrt{r^2}\,dt = r\,dt \]

Step 2: Set up the integral

We rotate about the \(x\)-axis, so the radius of each band is \(y = r\sin t\).

\[ S = \int_0^{\pi} 2\pi (r\sin t)(r)\,dt = 2\pi r^2\int_0^{\pi}\sin t\,dt \]

Step 3: Integrate

\[ \int_0^{\pi}\sin t\,dt = \big[-\cos t\big]_0^{\pi} = -(-1) - (-1) = 2 \]

\[ S = 2\pi r^2 \cdot 2 = 4\pi r^2 \]

Answer

The surface area of a sphere of radius \(r\) is \(4\pi r^2\) square units, as claimed.

Check

Take note that we used \(0 \le t \le \pi\), not \(2\pi\). Over \([0, 2\pi]\) the lower half would be rotated too and \(y\) would be negative there, so the integral would give 0, which is clearly wrong.
