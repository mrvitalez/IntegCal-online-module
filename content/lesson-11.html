10.1Curves Defined by Parametric Equations

So far every curve you have met was the graph of a function, \(y = f(x)\). Many natural curves fail the vertical line test: a circle, a figure eight, the path of a point on a rolling wheel. Stewart §10.1 handles them by letting both coordinates depend on a third variable \(t\), called the parameter:

\[ x = f(t), \qquad y = g(t), \qquad a \le t \le b. \]

Each value of \(t\) gives a point \((f(t), g(t))\). As \(t\) increases, the point moves and traces a parametric curve. It helps to think of \(t\) as time: the equations tell you not only where the curve is, but also in which direction and how fast it is traced. The point \((f(a), g(a))\) is the initial point and \((f(b), g(b))\) is the terminal point.

Plotting by hand. Make a small table. For \(x = t^2 - 2t,\ y = t + 1\): \(t = 0\) gives \((0, 1)\), \(t = 1\) gives \((-1, 2)\), \(t = 2\) gives \((0, 3)\). Join the points in order of increasing \(t\) and add arrows for the direction of motion.

Eliminating the parameter. To see which familiar curve you have, remove \(t\). Here \(t = y - 1\), so \(x = (y-1)^2 - 2(y-1)\): a parabola opening to the right. Two common tricks:

  • Solve one equation for \(t\) and substitute into the other (works when one equation is easy to invert).
  • For sines and cosines, use an identity. If \(x = 3\cos t,\ y = 3\sin t\), then \(x^2 + y^2 = 9(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t) = 9\), a circle of radius 3.
Common mistake The Cartesian equation alone loses information. The parametrizations \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\) and \(x = \sin 2t,\ y = \cos 2t\) for \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\) both give \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\), but the first goes around once counterclockwise starting at \((1, 0)\), while the second goes around twice clockwise starting at \((0, 1)\). Also watch for restricted ranges: \(x = \sqrt{t}\) forces \(x \ge 0\), so only half of a parabola may be traced.

The cycloid. Roll a circle of radius \(r\) along the \(x\)-axis and follow one point on its rim. If \(\theta\) is the angle through which the circle has turned (starting with the point at the origin), the circle has rolled a distance \(r\theta\), and the point is at

\[ x = r(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad y = r(1 - \cos\theta). \]

One arch corresponds to \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\). The cycloid touches the axis with a sharp point (a cusp) at \(\theta = 2\pi n\). Writing \(y\) as a function of \(x\) here is hopeless, which is exactly why parametric equations are so useful.

Try it in Desmos Open the free graphing calculator at https://www.desmos.com/calculator and type (t^2 - 2t, t + 1) as an ordered pair; Desmos treats it as a parametric curve. Then type (a^2 - 2a, a + 1) and accept the slider Desmos offers for \(a\); the slider plays the role of \(t\). Drag it and watch the point travel along the curve, which shows you the direction of motion at a glance. You can also change the \(t\)-range under the curve to see only part of it. Repeat with the cycloid (t - sin t, 1 - cos t) and with the two circle parametrizations above.
Key equations
\[ x = f(t),\quad y = g(t),\quad a \le t \le b \]
\[ x = r(\theta - \sin\theta),\quad y = r(1 - \cos\theta) \]

10.2Calculus with Parametric Curves

Stewart §10.2 shows that you can do calculus on a parametric curve without eliminating the parameter. Everything comes from the Chain Rule, and every formula below has the same shape: rewrite the familiar \(x\)-integral or \(x\)-derivative, then trade \(dx\) for \(\frac{dx}{dt}\,dt\).

Slope. If \(y\) is (locally) a differentiable function of \(x\), then \(\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}\). Solving,

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}, \qquad \frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0. \]

This tells you where the tangents are special:

  • Horizontal tangent where \(dy/dt = 0\) and \(dx/dt \ne 0\).
  • Vertical tangent where \(dx/dt = 0\) and \(dy/dt \ne 0\).
  • If both are 0, the formula says nothing; look more carefully (a cusp is possible, as on the cycloid).

For example, on \(x = t^2,\ y = t^3 - 3t\) you get \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3t^2 - 3}{2t}\). Horizontal tangents occur at \(t = \pm 1\), the points \((1, \mp 2)\); a vertical tangent occurs at \(t = 0\), the origin (Figure 11.3).

Second derivative. Here is the step students most often get wrong. \(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\) is the derivative of \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) with respect to x, so apply the same rule again, with \(dy/dx\) playing the role of \(y\):

\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt}. \]

Common mistake \(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\) is not \(\frac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}\). Differentiate \(dy/dx\) with respect to \(t\), then divide by \(dx/dt\) one more time.

Area. The area under \(y = F(x)\) is \(\int y\,dx\). On a parametric curve substitute \(y = g(t)\) and \(dx = f'(t)\,dt\):

\[ A = \int_\alpha^\beta g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt, \]

where the limits are chosen so that \(x\) runs from left to right (if the curve is traced right to left, swap the limits or take the absolute value). For one arch of the cycloid this gives \(\int_0^{2\pi} r(1-\cos\theta)\cdot r(1-\cos\theta)\,d\theta = 3\pi r^2\): three times the area of the rolling circle.

Arc length. A tiny piece of the curve has horizontal change \(dx\) and vertical change \(dy\), so its length is \(\sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2}\). Dividing out \(dt\),

\[ L = \int_\alpha^\beta \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt, \]

valid when the curve is traced exactly once as \(t\) goes from \(\alpha\) to \(\beta\). (Tracing the circle \(x = \sin 2t,\ y = \cos 2t\) over \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\) would give \(4\pi\), twice the true length.) One cycloid arch has length \(8r\).

Surface area. Rotating the curve about the \(x\)-axis (with \(y \ge 0\)) gives \(S = \int 2\pi y\,ds\); about the \(y\)-axis (with \(x \ge 0\)), \(S = \int 2\pi x\,ds\), where \(ds\) is the same square root times \(dt\). The radius is the distance from the axis of rotation, so pick \(y\) or \(x\) accordingly.

Key equations
\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} \]
\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt} \]
\[ A = \int_\alpha^\beta g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt \]
\[ L = \int_\alpha^\beta \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt \]
\[ S = \int_\alpha^\beta 2\pi y \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt \]

★Key takeaways

  • A parametric curve x = f(t), y = g(t) records both the path and the direction in which it is traced.
  • Eliminating the parameter identifies the curve, but you must still check which part is traced and how often.
  • The slope is dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt): horizontal tangents where dy/dt = 0, vertical where dx/dt = 0.
  • For d²y/dx², differentiate dy/dx with respect to t and divide by dx/dt again.
  • Area is ∫ g(t) f′(t) dt, arc length is ∫ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt, and surface area multiplies the arc-length integrand by 2π times the radius.

Example 11.1 Eliminating the parameter and finding the direction

Describe the curve \(x = 2 + 3\cos t,\ y = -1 + 2\sin t\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\): find a Cartesian equation, the initial point, and the direction of motion.

(Similar to Stewart §10.1, Examples 1–3)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Sines and cosines suggest the identity \(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\). Isolate \(\cos t\) and \(\sin t\) first.

Solution:

\(\cos t = \frac{x-2}{3}\) and \(\sin t = \frac{y+1}{2}\), so

\[ \frac{(x-2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y+1)^2}{4} = 1. \]

This is an ellipse centred at \((2, -1)\) with horizontal semi-axis 3 and vertical semi-axis 2. At \(t = 0\) the point is \((5, -1)\). At \(t = \pi/2\) it is \((2, 1)\), so the point first moves up and to the left: the ellipse is traced counterclockwise, exactly once, ending back at \((5, -1)\).

Check:

Try \(t = \pi\): \((2 - 3, -1) = (-1, -1)\), and \(\frac{9}{9} + 0 = 1\). It is on the ellipse and is the leftmost point, as expected halfway round.

Example 11.2 Tangent line and concavity

For \(x = t^2 + 1,\ y = t^3 - 2t\), find the tangent line at \(t = 2\) and decide whether the curve is concave up or down there.

(Similar to Stewart §10.2, Example 1)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}\), then differentiate that result with respect to \(t\) and divide by \(dx/dt\) again.

Slope and tangent:

\(\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t\), \(\frac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2 - 2\), so \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3t^2 - 2}{2t}\). At \(t = 2\): the point is \((5, 4)\) and the slope is \(\frac{10}{4} = \frac{5}{2}\). Tangent line: \(y - 4 = \frac{5}{2}(x - 5)\), i.e. \(y = \frac{5}{2}x - \frac{17}{2}\).

Concavity:

Write \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3t}{2} - \frac{1}{t}\). Then \(\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac{3}{2} + \frac{1}{t^2}\), and

\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\frac{3}{2} + \frac{1}{t^2}}{2t} = \frac{3t^2 + 2}{4t^3}. \]

At \(t = 2\) this is \(\frac{14}{32} = \frac{7}{16} \gt 0\): concave up.

Check:

Do not divide \(\frac{d^2y}{dt^2} = 6t\) by \(\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = 2\); that gives 6 at \(t=2\), which is wrong. The sign of \(\frac{3t^2+2}{4t^3}\) matches the sign of \(t\), so the curve is concave up for every \(t \gt 0\).

Example 11.3 Arc length and surface area

Consider the arc \(x = 3t^2,\ y = 2t^3\), \(0 \le t \le 1\) (Figure 11.4). (a) Find its length. (b) Find the area of the surface obtained by rotating it about the \(y\)-axis.

(Similar to Stewart §10.2, Examples 4–6)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Compute the speed \(\sqrt{(dx/dt)^2 + (dy/dt)^2}\) once and reuse it. For rotation about the \(y\)-axis, the radius is \(x\).

Speed:

\(\frac{dx}{dt} = 6t\), \(\frac{dy}{dt} = 6t^2\), so \(\sqrt{36t^2 + 36t^4} = 6t\sqrt{1 + t^2}\) (since \(t \ge 0\)).

(a) Length:

\[ L = \int_0^1 6t\sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt = 2(1+t^2)^{3/2}\Big|_0^1 = 2(2\sqrt{2} - 1) = 4\sqrt{2} - 2 \approx 3.657. \]

(b) Surface area:

\[ S = \int_0^1 2\pi(3t^2)\cdot 6t\sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt = 36\pi\int_0^1 t^3\sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt. \]

Let \(u = 1 + t^2\), \(du = 2t\,dt\), \(t^2 = u - 1\): \(\int_0^1 t^3\sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt = \frac12\int_1^2 (u-1)\sqrt{u}\,du = \frac12\left[\frac25 u^{5/2} - \frac23 u^{3/2}\right]_1^2 = \frac{2\sqrt2 + 2}{15}\). So \(S = \frac{24\pi(\sqrt2 + 1)}{5} \approx 36.41\).

Check:

The straight segment from \((0,0)\) to \((3,2)\) has length \(\sqrt{13} \approx 3.606\), a little less than 3.657, as it must be since the arc curves.
