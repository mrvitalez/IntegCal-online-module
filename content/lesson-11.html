10.1Curves Defined by Parametric Equations

Up to now, every curve we worked with was the graph of a function \(y = f(x)\). But think about the path of a planet around the Sun, a circle, or a point on the rim of a rolling wheel. These curves fail the vertical line test, so they cannot be written as a single \(y = f(x)\). So, how do we describe them? We let both coordinates depend on a third variable.

Definition A parametric equation represents a curve by expressing both \(x\) and \(y\) as functions of a third variable, usually \(t\), called the parameter: \[ x = f(t), \qquad y = g(t). \qquad (1) \] The set of points \((x, y) = (f(t), g(t))\) obtained as \(t\) varies forms a parametric curve.

It helps to think of \(t\) as time. Then the equations tell us not only where the curve is, but also in which direction it is traced and in what order the points are visited. In physics, this is exactly how we describe motion: the position of a projectile is \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\), and the path is the parametric curve.

Sketching by a table. If we are not told the values of \(t\), we assume \(t\) can be any real number. We pick some values of \(t\), compute \(x\) and \(y\), plot the points, and join them in order of increasing \(t\). Take \(x = t^2 - 2t,\ y = t + 1\):

\(t\)\(-2\)\(-1\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)
\(x\)830\(-1\)0
\(y\)\(-1\)0123

Eliminating the parameter. To recognise the curve, we remove \(t\). From \(y = t + 1\) we get \(t = y - 1\), and substituting into \(x\) gives \(x = (y - 1)^2 - 2(y - 1)\). From this, we know that our curve is a parabola opening to the right (Figure 11.1). There are two common tricks:

  • Solve the easier equation for \(t\) and substitute it into the other one.
  • For sines and cosines, use the identity \(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\). If \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\), then \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\): the unit circle.

Initial and terminal points. Sometimes \(t\) is restricted to a finite interval. In general, the curve

\[ x = f(t), \qquad y = g(t), \qquad a \le t \le b \qquad (2) \]

has initial point \((f(a), g(a))\) and terminal point \((f(b), g(b))\). If we take the same parabola above but only for \(0 \le t \le 4\), we get just a piece of it, starting at \((0, 1)\) and ending at \((8, 5)\).

Same graph, different curve. Let's compare \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\) with \(x = \sin 2t,\ y = \cos 2t\), both for \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\). Both satisfy \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\), so both trace the unit circle. But if we input values of \(t\), we see that the first one starts at \((1, 0)\) and goes around once counterclockwise, while the second starts at \((0, 1)\) and circles back twice, clockwise. The Cartesian equation tells us the shape; the parametric equations tell us the motion.

Common mistake Take note that eliminating the parameter can lose information. Always check (1) which part of the Cartesian curve is traced, (2) the direction, and (3) how many times. For example, \(x = \sqrt{t},\ y = 2t + 1\) gives \(y = 2x^2 + 1\), but only the half with \(x \ge 0\), because a square root is never negative.

The cycloid. Roll a circle of radius \(r\) along the \(x\)-axis and follow one point on its rim. If \(\theta\) is the angle through which the circle has turned (with the point starting at the origin), the circle has rolled a distance \(r\theta\), and the point is at

\[ x = r(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad y = r(1 - \cos\theta). \]

One arch corresponds to \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\), and the curve touches the axis with a sharp point (a cusp) at \(\theta = 0, 2\pi, 4\pi, \ldots\) (Figure 11.2). Writing \(y\) as a function of \(x\) here is hopeless, which is exactly why we need parametric equations.

Then type (a^2 - 2a, a + 1) and accept the slider for \(a\). Drag the slider and watch the point travel along the curve; that is the direction of motion. Key equations
\[ x = f(t),\quad y = g(t),\quad a \le t \le b \]
\[ x = h + r\cos t,\quad y = k + r\sin t,\quad 0 \le t \le 2\pi \;\;\text{(circle)} \]
\[ x = r(\theta - \sin\theta),\quad y = r(1 - \cos\theta) \;\;\text{(cycloid)} \]

10.2Calculus with Parametric Curves

When we describe a curve using parametric equations, both \(x\) and \(y\) are expressed in terms of the parameter \(t\), which can represent time, an angle, or another quantity. The good news is that we can do calculus on the curve without eliminating the parameter. Everything comes from the Chain Rule.

Differentiation. To find the slope, or the rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\), recall the Chain Rule: \(\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}\). Solving for \(\frac{dy}{dx}\),

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}, \qquad \text{provided } \frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0. \qquad (3) \]

So, what does equation (3) mean? Basically,

  • \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) measures how "fast" the \(x\)-coordinate changes with respect to \(t\);
  • \(\frac{dy}{dt}\) measures how "fast" the \(y\)-coordinate changes with respect to \(t\);
  • their ratio measures how steeply \(y\) changes with \(x\): the slope of the curve at that instant.

Tangent lines. At the point \(P(x(t_0), y(t_0))\), the tangent line is

\[ y - y(t_0) = \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{t = t_0}\big(x - x(t_0)\big). \qquad (4) \]

This also tells us where the tangents are special:

  • Horizontal tangent where \(\frac{dy}{dt} = 0\) and \(\frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0\).
  • Vertical tangent where \(\frac{dx}{dt} = 0\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt} \ne 0\).
  • If both are 0, the formula says nothing, and we must look more carefully (a cusp is possible, as on the cycloid).

Second derivative. To determine concavity, we need \(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\), which is the derivative of \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) with respect to x. We apply equation (3) again, this time with \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) playing the role of \(y\):

\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac{\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt}. \qquad (5) \]
Common mistake \(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\) is not \(\frac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}\). Differentiate \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) with respect to \(t\), then divide by \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) one more time.

Area under a parametric curve. If the curve is traced once from \(t = a\) to \(t = b\) and does not loop back on itself, the area under it is \(A = \int y\,dx\). Since \(y = g(t)\) and \(dx = f'(t)\,dt\),

\[ A = \int y\,dx = \int_a^b g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt, \qquad (6) \]

where the limits are chosen so that \(x\) runs from left to right (if the curve is traced right to left, swap the limits or take the absolute value).

Arc length. A tiny piece of the curve has horizontal change \(dx\) and vertical change \(dy\). By the Pythagorean theorem its length is \(ds = \sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2}\). Dividing and multiplying by \(dt\),

\[ L = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt, \qquad (7) \]

valid when the curve is traced exactly once. For the unit circle \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\), the integrand is \(\sqrt{\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t} = 1\), so \(L = 2\pi\), the circumference, as expected. If we used \(x = \sin 2t,\ y = \cos 2t\) over the same interval, we would get \(4\pi\), because that curve goes around twice. For the arc \(x = 3t^2,\ y = 2t^3\), \(0 \le t \le 1\) (Figure 11.4), the integrand is \(\sqrt{36t^2 + 36t^4} = 6t\sqrt{1+t^2}\), and \(L = 2(1+t^2)^{3/2}\big|_0^1 = 4\sqrt2 - 2 \approx 3.657\).

Surface area. If the curve \(x = f(t),\ y = g(t),\ a \le t \le b\), is rotated, each tiny piece \(ds\) sweeps out a thin band. Each band is approximately the circumference \(2\pi(\text{radius})\) multiplied by \(ds\). So the general formula is \(S = \int 2\pi(\text{radius})\,ds\), and

\[ \text{about the } x\text{-axis: } S = \int_a^b 2\pi y\,\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt, \qquad \text{about the } y\text{-axis: } S = \int_a^b 2\pi x\,\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt. \]
Common mistake The radius is the distance from the axis of rotation. Rotating about the \(x\)-axis uses \(y\); rotating about the \(y\)-axis uses \(x\). Mixing them up is the most common error in surface-area problems.
Key equations
\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} \qquad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx/dt} \]
\[ A = \int_a^b g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt \]
\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt \qquad S = \int_a^b 2\pi y\, \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt \]

★Key takeaways

  • A parametric curve x = f(t), y = g(t) records both the path and the direction (and order) in which it is traced.
  • Eliminating the parameter tells us the shape, but we must still check which part is traced and how many times.
  • A circle with centre (h, k) and radius r is x = h + r cos t, y = k + r sin t, 0 ≤ t ≤ 2π.
  • The slope is dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt): horizontal tangents where dy/dt = 0, vertical tangents where dx/dt = 0.
  • For d²y/dx², differentiate dy/dx with respect to t, then divide by dx/dt again.
  • Area is ∫ y dx = ∫ g(t) f′(t) dt, arc length is ∫ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt, and surface area is ∫ 2π(radius) ds.

Example 11.1 Sketching and identifying a parametric curve

Sketch and identify the curve defined by the parametric equations \(x = t^2 - 2t\) and \(y = t + 1\). Then describe the part traced when \(0 \le t \le 4\).

Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(x = t^2 - 2t,\ y = t + 1\). Find: the shape of the curve, its direction, and the piece for \(0 \le t \le 4\).

Plan: Since no interval is stated at first, we assume \(t\) can be any real number. We make a table of values to see the direction, then eliminate \(t\) to recognise the shape.

Step 1. Make a table by substituting values of \(t\).

\(t\)\(-2\)\(-1\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)\(4\)
\(x = t^2 - 2t\)830\(-1\)08
\(y = t + 1\)\(-1\)01235

As \(t\) increases, \(y\) always increases, so the curve is traced from bottom to top.

Step 2. Eliminate the parameter. The \(y\)-equation is easy to solve for \(t\):

\[ y = t + 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; t = y - 1. \]

Step 3. Substitute \(t = y - 1\) into the \(x\)-equation and simplify:

\[ x = (y-1)^2 - 2(y-1) = y^2 - 2y + 1 - 2y + 2 = y^2 - 4y + 3. \]

Step 4. Identify the curve. An equation of the form \(x = (\text{quadratic in } y)\) is a parabola opening to the right. Completing the square, \(x = (y - 2)^2 - 1\), so the vertex is \((-1, 2)\), which matches the table at \(t = 1\).

Step 5. Restrict to \(0 \le t \le 4\). By equation (2), the initial point is at \(t = 0\) and the terminal point is at \(t = 4\):

\[ (x(0), y(0)) = (0, 1), \qquad (x(4), y(4)) = (16 - 8,\ 5) = (8, 5). \]

Answer: \(\boxed{x = (y-2)^2 - 1}\), a parabola opening to the right with vertex \((-1, 2)\), traced from bottom to top. For \(0 \le t \le 4\) only the piece from \((0, 1)\) to \((8, 5)\) is traced. That is our answer.

Check: Take \(t = 3\): \((x, y) = (3, 4)\). In the Cartesian equation, \((4 - 2)^2 - 1 = 3 = x\). ✓

Example 11.2 Parametric equations of any circle

Find parametric equations for the circle with centre \((h, k)\) and radius \(r\). Use them to write the circle with centre \((3, 4)\) and radius 2, and show that your equations really give that circle.

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: centre \((h, k)\), radius \(r\). Find: \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\).

Plan: Start from the unit circle \(x = \cos t,\ y = \sin t\) and build the general circle in two moves: stretch, then shift.

Step 1. Recall that \((\cos t, \sin t)\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\), traces the unit circle once, because \(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\).

Step 2. Stretch by the radius. Multiplying both coordinates by \(r\) makes the circle bigger or smaller:

\[ (r\cos t,\ r\sin t), \qquad x^2 + y^2 = r^2(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t) = r^2. \]

Step 3. Shift the centre. From algebra, we know that adding \(h\) to \(x\) and \(k\) to \(y\) moves the centre from the origin to \((h, k)\):

\[ x = h + r\cos t, \qquad y = k + r\sin t, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi. \]

Step 4. Substitute \((h, k) = (3, 4)\) and \(r = 2\):

\[ x = 3 + 2\cos t, \qquad y = 4 + 2\sin t. \]

Step 5. Prove it is that circle by eliminating \(t\). Isolate the trig functions, \(\cos t = \frac{x-3}{2}\) and \(\sin t = \frac{y-4}{2}\), then use \(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\):

\[ \left(\frac{x-3}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y-4}{2}\right)^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x-3)^2 + (y-4)^2 = 4. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{x = h + r\cos t,\ y = k + r\sin t,\ 0 \le t \le 2\pi}\); for our circle, \(x = 3 + 2\cos t,\ y = 4 + 2\sin t\). That is our answer.

Check: At \(t = 0\) the point is \((5, 4)\), exactly 2 units to the right of the centre \((3, 4)\). ✓ As \(t\) increases to \(\pi/2\) the point moves to \((3, 6)\), so the circle is traced counterclockwise.

Example 11.3 Two tangents at one point, horizontal and vertical tangents, concavity

A curve \(C\) is defined by \(x = t^2,\ y = t^3 - 3t\). (a) Show that \(C\) has two tangents at the point \((3, 0)\) and find their equations. (b) Find the points on \(C\) where the tangent is horizontal or vertical. (c) Determine where the curve is concave upward or downward. (See Figure 11.3.)

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(x = t^2,\ y = t^3 - 3t\). Find: the tangents at \((3, 0)\), the horizontal/vertical tangents, and the concavity.

Plan: Get \(\frac{dx}{dt}\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt}\) once, then use equation (3) for slopes, equation (4) for tangent lines and equation (5) for concavity.

Step 1. Find the values of \(t\) at the point \((3, 0)\). From \(x = t^2 = 3\), we get \(t = \pm\sqrt3\). Substitute into \(y\):

\[ y(\sqrt3) = (\sqrt3)^3 - 3\sqrt3 = 3\sqrt3 - 3\sqrt3 = 0, \qquad y(-\sqrt3) = -3\sqrt3 + 3\sqrt3 = 0. \]

Two different values of \(t\) give the same point, so the curve passes through \((3, 0)\) twice. That is why it can have two tangents there.

Step 2. Get the derivatives separately, then their ratio:

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = 2t, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = 3t^2 - 3, \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3t^2 - 3}{2t} = \frac{3}{2}\left(t - \frac{1}{t}\right). \]

Step 3. Substitute \(t = \pm\sqrt3\) to get the slopes:

\[ m_1 = \frac32\left(\sqrt3 - \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\right) = \frac32\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt3} = \sqrt3, \qquad m_2 = \frac32\left(-\sqrt3 + \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\right) = -\sqrt3. \]

Step 4. Use equation (4) with the point \((3, 0)\):

\[ y = \sqrt3\,(x - 3) \qquad\text{and}\qquad y = -\sqrt3\,(x - 3). \]

Step 5. Horizontal tangents need \(\frac{dy}{dt} = 0\) and \(\frac{dx}{dt} \ne 0\): \(3t^2 - 3 = 0\) gives \(t = \pm 1\), and \(2t \ne 0\) there, so both are valid.

\[ t = 1:\ (x, y) = (1,\ 1 - 3) = (1, -2), \qquad t = -1:\ (x, y) = (1,\ -1 + 3) = (1, 2). \]

Step 6. Vertical tangents need \(\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t = 0\), so \(t = 0\), where \(\frac{dy}{dt} = -3 \ne 0\). The point is \((0, 0)\).

Step 7. Concavity by equation (5). First differentiate \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) with respect to \(t\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac32\left(t - \frac1t\right)\right] = \frac32\left(1 + \frac{1}{t^2}\right) = \frac{3(t^2+1)}{2t^2}. \]

Then divide by \(\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t\):

\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{3(t^2+1)}{2t^2}\cdot\frac{1}{2t} = \frac{3(t^2+1)}{4t^3}. \]

The numerator is always positive, so the sign is the sign of \(t^3\).

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{y = \sqrt3(x-3)}\) and \(\boxed{y = -\sqrt3(x-3)}\). (b) Horizontal tangents at \((1, -2)\) and \((1, 2)\); vertical tangent at \((0, 0)\). (c) Concave upward for \(t \gt 0\) and concave downward for \(t \lt 0\). That is our answer.

Check: The two slopes \(\pm\sqrt3\) are opposites, which makes sense because the curve is symmetric about the \(x\)-axis (replacing \(t\) by \(-t\) keeps \(x\) and changes the sign of \(y\)). Figure 11.3 shows both tangents crossing at \((3, 0)\).

Example 11.4 Tangent to the cycloid

(a) Find the tangent to the cycloid \(x = r(\theta - \sin\theta),\ y = r(1 - \cos\theta)\) at the point where \(\theta = \pi/3\). (b) At what points is the tangent horizontal? When is it vertical?

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: the cycloid with parameter \(\theta\). Find: the tangent at \(\theta = \pi/3\), and where the tangent is horizontal or vertical.

Plan: The same steps as Example 11.3, with \(\theta\) in place of \(t\). Recall your trigonometric identities.

Step 1. Differentiate each coordinate with respect to \(\theta\):

\[ \frac{dx}{d\theta} = r(1 - \cos\theta), \qquad \frac{dy}{d\theta} = r\sin\theta, \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{r\sin\theta}{r(1 - \cos\theta)} = \frac{\sin\theta}{1 - \cos\theta}. \]

Step 2. Evaluate the slope at \(\theta = \pi/3\), where \(\sin\frac\pi3 = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and \(\cos\frac\pi3 = \frac12\):

\[ m = \frac{\sqrt3/2}{1 - 1/2} = \frac{\sqrt3/2}{1/2} = \sqrt3. \]

Step 3. Find the point at \(\theta = \pi/3\):

\[ x = r\left(\frac\pi3 - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right), \qquad y = r\left(1 - \frac12\right) = \frac r2. \]

Step 4. Use equation (4) and simplify:

\[ y - \frac r2 = \sqrt3\left[x - r\left(\frac\pi3 - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\right] \;\Longrightarrow\; y = \sqrt3\,x - \frac{\sqrt3\,\pi r}{3} + \frac{3r}{2} + \frac r2 = \sqrt3\,x + r\left(2 - \frac{\pi}{\sqrt3}\right). \]

Step 5. Horizontal tangents: \(\frac{dy}{d\theta} = r\sin\theta = 0\) and \(\frac{dx}{d\theta} = r(1 - \cos\theta) \ne 0\). The first gives \(\theta = n\pi\); the second rules out even multiples of \(\pi\). So \(\theta = (2n-1)\pi\), and there \(y = r(1 - (-1)) = 2r\): the tops of the arches, \(\big((2n-1)\pi r,\ 2r\big)\).

Step 6. Vertical tangents: \(\frac{dx}{d\theta} = 0\) when \(\cos\theta = 1\), i.e. \(\theta = 2n\pi\). But there \(\frac{dy}{d\theta} = 0\) too, so equation (3) gives \(\frac00\). Look closer: as \(\theta \to 0^+\), \(\frac{\sin\theta}{1-\cos\theta} \to +\infty\), and as \(\theta \to 0^-\) it goes to \(-\infty\). The tangent lines become vertical at the cusps \((2n\pi r, 0)\).

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{y = \sqrt3\,x + r\left(2 - \dfrac{\pi}{\sqrt3}\right)}\), equivalently \(\sqrt3\,x - y = r\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt3} - 2\right)\). (b) Horizontal at the tops of the arches \(\big((2n-1)\pi r, 2r\big)\); vertical (as limits) at the cusps \((2n\pi r, 0)\). That is our answer.

Check: Put \(x = r(\pi/3 - \sqrt3/2)\) into the line: \(\sqrt3\,r\pi/3 - 3r/2 + 2r - r\pi/\sqrt3 = r/2\) because \(\sqrt3/3 = 1/\sqrt3\). ✓ The point lies on its tangent.

Example 11.5 Area under one arch of the cycloid

Find the area under one arch of the cycloid \(x = r(\theta - \sin\theta),\ y = r(1 - \cos\theta)\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: the cycloid. Find: the area under one arch.

Plan: Since we only look for one arch, the interval is \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\). Use equation (6), \(A = \int y\,dx\), with \(dx\) written in terms of \(d\theta\). As \(\theta\) increases, \(x\) increases, so the limits are in the right order.

Step 1. Get \(dx\):

\[ \frac{dx}{d\theta} = r(1 - \cos\theta) \;\Longrightarrow\; dx = r(1 - \cos\theta)\,d\theta. \]

Step 2. Substitute \(y\) and \(dx\) into equation (6):

\[ A = \int_0^{2\pi} r(1 - \cos\theta)\cdot r(1 - \cos\theta)\,d\theta = r^2\int_0^{2\pi}(1 - \cos\theta)^2\,d\theta. \]

Step 3. Expand the square:

\[ (1 - \cos\theta)^2 = 1 - 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta. \]

Step 4. Use the half-angle identity \(\cos^2\theta = \frac12(1 + \cos 2\theta)\) so every term can be integrated:

\[ A = r^2\int_0^{2\pi}\left(\frac32 - 2\cos\theta + \frac12\cos 2\theta\right)d\theta. \]

Step 5. Integrate term by term and evaluate:

\[ A = r^2\left[\frac32\theta - 2\sin\theta + \frac14\sin 2\theta\right]_0^{2\pi} = r^2\left[\frac32(2\pi) - 0 + 0\right] = 3\pi r^2. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{A = 3\pi r^2}\), three times the area of the rolling circle. That is our answer.

Check: One arch fits in a rectangle of width \(2\pi r\) and height \(2r\), area \(4\pi r^2\). Our area \(3\pi r^2\) is less than that, and more than half of it, which matches the round shape of the arch in Figure 11.2.

Example 11.6 Surface area of a sphere

Show that the surface area of a sphere of radius \(r\) is \(4\pi r^2\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: a sphere of radius \(r\). Find: its surface area using a parametric curve.

Plan: A sphere is what we get when we rotate the upper half of a circle about the \(x\)-axis. Parametrize the half circle, then use \(S = \int 2\pi y\,ds\).

Step 1. Parametrize the upper semicircle (Example 11.2 with centre at the origin):

\[ x = r\cos t, \qquad y = r\sin t, \qquad 0 \le t \le \pi. \]

Here \(y \ge 0\), so \(y\) is the radius of each band.

Step 2. Compute \(ds\):

\[ \frac{dx}{dt} = -r\sin t, \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = r\cos t, \quad \sqrt{r^2\sin^2 t + r^2\cos^2 t} = \sqrt{r^2} = r. \]

Step 3. Set up the surface-area integral:

\[ S = \int_0^{\pi} 2\pi (r\sin t)\cdot r\,dt = 2\pi r^2\int_0^{\pi}\sin t\,dt. \]

Step 4. Integrate:

\[ \int_0^\pi \sin t\,dt = \big[-\cos t\big]_0^\pi = -(-1) - (-1) = 2, \qquad S = 2\pi r^2\cdot 2 = 4\pi r^2. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{S = 4\pi r^2}\). That is our answer, and it is the formula we memorised in geometry.

Check: For the Earth, \(r \approx 6371\) km gives \(S \approx 5.1\times10^8\ \text{km}^2\), the accepted value of the Earth's surface area. ✓
