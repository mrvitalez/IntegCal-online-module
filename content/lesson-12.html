10.3Polar Coordinates

Cartesian coordinates locate a point by going across and then up. Polar coordinates (Stewart §10.3) locate it by a distance and a direction instead. Choose a point \(O\), the pole, and a ray from it, the polar axis (normally the positive \(x\)-axis). A point \(P\) has polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\), where \(r\) is its distance from \(O\) and \(\theta\) is the angle from the polar axis to \(OP\), positive counterclockwise.

Two features feel strange at first:

  • Representations are not unique. \((r, \theta)\) and \((r, \theta + 2n\pi)\) are the same point.
  • \(r\) may be negative. \((-r, \theta)\) means: face direction \(\theta\), then step backwards a distance \(r\). So \((-r, \theta)\) is the same point as \((r, \theta + \pi)\).

Converting. From the right triangle with hypotenuse \(r\):

\[ x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}. \]

Common mistake \(\tan\theta = y/x\) does not fix \(\theta\): \((1, -1)\) and \((-1, 1)\) give the same tangent. Always check which quadrant the point is in. For \((-1, 1)\), \(\theta = 3\pi/4\), not \(-\pi/4\).

Polar curves. The graph of \(r = f(\theta)\) is the set of points with at least one representation satisfying the equation. Some basic ones: \(r = 2\) is a circle of radius 2 about the pole; \(\theta = \pi/4\) is a line through the pole. To identify \(r = 6\cos\theta\), multiply by \(r\): \(x^2 + y^2 = 6x\), that is \((x-3)^2 + y^2 = 9\), a circle through the pole. Other families you should recognise: cardioids \(r = a(1 \pm \cos\theta)\), limaçons \(r = a + b\cos\theta\) (with an inner loop when \(|b| \gt |a|\), Figure 12.1), and roses \(r = a\cos n\theta\) or \(a\sin n\theta\), which have \(n\) petals if \(n\) is odd and \(2n\) petals if \(n\) is even (Figure 12.2).

Symmetry saves half (or more) of the sketching work:

  • unchanged when \(\theta \to -\theta\): symmetric about the polar axis (for example, any function of \(\cos\theta\));
  • unchanged when \(r \to -r\) or \(\theta \to \theta + \pi\): symmetric about the pole;
  • unchanged when \(\theta \to \pi - \theta\): symmetric about the vertical line \(\theta = \pi/2\) (any function of \(\sin\theta\)).

Tangents. Treat \(\theta\) as a parameter: \(x = f(\theta)\cos\theta,\ y = f(\theta)\sin\theta\). Then by §10.2, with \(r' = dr/d\theta\),

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta}. \]

Horizontal tangents come from \(dy/d\theta = 0\), vertical ones from \(dx/d\theta = 0\). At the pole, if \(f(\alpha) = 0\) and \(f'(\alpha) \ne 0\), the formula reduces to \(\tan\alpha\): the curve passes through the pole along the line \(\theta = \alpha\).

Key equations
\[ x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta \]
\[ r^2 = x^2 + y^2,\quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} \]
\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\cos\theta - r\sin\theta} \]

10.4Areas and Lengths in Polar Coordinates

For polar curves, the basic building block is not a rectangle but a circular sector. A sector of radius \(r\) and angle \(\theta\) has area \(\frac12 r^2\theta\) (Stewart §10.4). Slice the region bounded by \(r = f(\theta)\) and the rays \(\theta = a,\ \theta = b\) into thin sectors of angle \(d\theta\); each has area about \(\frac12 r^2\,d\theta\). Adding them up,

\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12 r^2\,d\theta = \int_a^b \tfrac12 [f(\theta)]^2\,d\theta. \]

Finding the limits. The hard part is usually choosing \(a\) and \(b\) so that the region is swept out exactly once. For one petal of \(r = 2\cos 2\theta\), the petal on the polar axis starts and ends at the pole, where \(\cos 2\theta = 0\): \(\theta = \pm\pi/4\). So

\[ A = \int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4} \tfrac12 (2\cos 2\theta)^2\,d\theta = \int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4} (1 + \cos 4\theta)\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}. \]

Symmetry often helps: compute half a petal and double it. A good sanity check of the formula is the circle \(r = a\) for \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\): it gives \(\frac12 a^2\cdot 2\pi = \pi a^2\), as it should. Remember also that \(r^2 \ge 0\), so a polar area integral with the limits in increasing order can never come out negative; a negative answer means an algebra slip.

Common mistake Integrating over \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\) is not automatically right. The circle \(r = 2\sin\theta\) is traced once for \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\); over \([0, 2\pi]\) it is traced twice and the integral gives \(2\pi\) instead of \(\pi\). Sketch first, then choose the interval.

Area between two curves. If \(f(\theta) \ge g(\theta) \ge 0\) for \(a \le \theta \le b\), the region between them has area

\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12\left([f(\theta)]^2 - [g(\theta)]^2\right)d\theta. \]

Notice it is the difference of the squares, not the square of the difference. Find \(a\) and \(b\) by solving \(f(\theta) = g(\theta)\). Be careful: two polar curves can meet at a point that does not show up this way, because the point may have different representations on the two curves. The pole is the usual culprit, since \(r = 0\) at different angles on different curves. A quick sketch reveals such hidden intersections.

In practice: sketch both curves, shade the region, decide which curve is farther from the pole, and only then write the integral.

Arc length. Using \(\theta\) as the parameter, \(\left(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{d\theta}\right)^2\) simplifies (expand and use \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\)) to \(r^2 + (dr/d\theta)^2\), so

\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta. \]

For the cardioid \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\): \(r^2 + r'^2 = 2 + 2\cos\theta = 4\cos^2(\theta/2)\), so \(L = \int_0^{2\pi} 2|\cos(\theta/2)|\,d\theta = 8\). Without the absolute value you would get 0, a sure sign something went wrong.

Key equations
\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12 r^2\,d\theta \]
\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12\left([f(\theta)]^2 - [g(\theta)]^2\right)d\theta \]
\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta \]

10.6Conic Sections in Polar Coordinates

Quick review of the conics (Stewart §10.5).

  • Parabola: points equally far from a focus and a line (the directrix). \(x^2 = 4py\) has focus \((0, p)\), directrix \(y = -p\), vertex at the origin; \(y^2 = 4px\) opens sideways.
  • Ellipse: points whose distances to two foci add to \(2a\). \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) (\(a \ge b \gt 0\)) has vertices \((\pm a, 0)\) and foci \((\pm c, 0)\) with \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\).
  • Hyperbola: points whose distances to two foci differ by \(2a\). \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) has vertices \((\pm a, 0)\), foci \((\pm c, 0)\) with \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\), and asymptotes \(y = \pm\frac{b}{a}x\).

The eccentricity \(e = c/a\) measures how stretched the conic is: \(0 \lt e \lt 1\) for an ellipse (near 0 it is almost a circle), \(e \gt 1\) for a hyperbola.

Theorem Let \(F\) be a fixed point (focus), \(l\) a fixed line (directrix) not through \(F\), and \(e \gt 0\). The set of points \(P\) with \(\dfrac{|PF|}{|Pl|} = e\) is an ellipse if \(e \lt 1\), a parabola if \(e = 1\), and a hyperbola if \(e \gt 1\).

This one definition covers all three conics, and it is tailor-made for polar coordinates (Stewart §10.6). Put the focus at the pole and the directrix at \(x = d\) (\(d \gt 0\)). For \(P = (r, \theta)\): \(|PF| = r\) and \(|Pl| = d - r\cos\theta\). So \(r = e(d - r\cos\theta)\); solving for \(r\),

\[ r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\cos\theta}. \]

The four standard positions of the directrix give

DirectrixEquation
\(x = d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\cos\theta}\)
\(x = -d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 - e\cos\theta}\)
\(y = d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\sin\theta}\)
\(y = -d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 - e\sin\theta}\)

Reading off the conic. Take \(r = \dfrac{10}{3 - 2\cos\theta}\). First make the constant in the denominator equal to 1 by dividing top and bottom by 3: \(r = \dfrac{10/3}{1 - \frac23\cos\theta}\). Now \(e = \frac23 \lt 1\): an ellipse. From \(ed = \frac{10}{3}\), \(d = 5\), and the minus sign with cosine puts the directrix at \(x = -5\). The vertices lie on the axis through the focus: \(\theta = 0\) gives \(r = 10\), the point \((10, 0)\); \(\theta = \pi\) gives \(r = 2\), the point \((-2, 0)\). So the centre is \((4, 0)\), \(a = 6\), \(c = 4\), and indeed \(c/a = \frac23 = e\) (Figure 12.5).

Parabolas and rotations. When \(e = 1\), for example \(r = \frac{3}{1 + \cos\theta}\), the denominator is 0 at \(\theta = \pi\): the parabola has no point in that direction, so it opens away from its directrix \(x = 3\), with vertex halfway between focus and directrix at \((\frac32, 0)\). Finally, replacing \(\theta\) by \(\theta - \alpha\) in any of these equations rotates the conic counterclockwise through the angle \(\alpha\) about the focus, so \(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\cos(\theta - \pi/4)}\) is the same conic turned by \(45^\circ\).

Common mistake Reading \(e = 2\) straight from \(\frac{10}{3 - 2\cos\theta}\). The coefficient of \(\cos\theta\) is \(e\) only after the constant term is 1.
Key equations
\[ e = \frac{c}{a} \]
\[ \frac{|PF|}{|Pl|} = e \]
\[ r = \frac{ed}{1 \pm e\cos\theta} \]
\[ r = \frac{ed}{1 \pm e\sin\theta} \]

★Key takeaways

  • A point has many polar representations: (r, θ) = (r, θ + 2nπ) = (−r, θ + π).
  • Use x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ and r² = x² + y² to convert, checking the quadrant when you find θ.
  • Treat θ as a parameter to get tangent slopes: dy/dx = (r′ sin θ + r cos θ)/(r′ cos θ − r sin θ).
  • Polar area is ∫ ½ r² dθ; between curves use ½(r_outer² − r_inner²), and choose limits that sweep the region once.
  • Polar arc length is ∫ √(r² + (dr/dθ)²) dθ.
  • With a focus at the pole, every conic is r = ed/(1 ± e cos θ) or ed/(1 ± e sin θ); e < 1 ellipse, e = 1 parabola, e > 1 hyperbola.

Example 12.1 Tangent line to a limaçon

Find the Cartesian coordinates of the point on \(r = 1 + 2\cos\theta\) where \(\theta = \pi/2\), and the equation of the tangent line there.

(Similar to Stewart §10.3, Example 9)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Convert the point with \(x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta\), then use the polar slope formula with \(r' = dr/d\theta\).

The point:

At \(\theta = \pi/2\): \(r = 1 + 0 = 1\), so \((x, y) = (1\cdot 0, 1\cdot 1) = (0, 1)\).

The slope:

\(r' = -2\sin\theta = -2\) at \(\theta = \pi/2\). With \(\sin\theta = 1,\ \cos\theta = 0\):

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta} = \frac{(-2)(1) + (1)(0)}{(-2)(0) - (1)(1)} = \frac{-2}{-1} = 2. \]

Tangent line: \(y = 2x + 1\) (Figure 12.1).

Check:

A common slip is to use \(dr/d\theta = -2\) as the slope. That is the rate of change of distance from the pole, not the slope of the curve in the \(xy\)-plane.

Example 12.2 Area between two polar curves

Find the area of the region that lies inside the circle \(r = 2\cos\theta\) and outside the circle \(r = 1\).

(Similar to Stewart §10.4, Example 3)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Sketch (Figure 12.4), find where the curves meet, then integrate \(\frac12(r_{\text{outer}}^2 - r_{\text{inner}}^2)\). The region is symmetric about the polar axis, so you may also double the upper half.

Limits:

\(2\cos\theta = 1\) gives \(\cos\theta = \frac12\), \(\theta = \pm\pi/3\). Between these angles, \(2\cos\theta \ge 1\), so \(r = 2\cos\theta\) is the outer curve.

Integral:

\[ A = \int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3} \tfrac12\left(4\cos^2\theta - 1\right)d\theta = \int_{0}^{\pi/3}\left(2 + 2\cos 2\theta - 1\right)d\theta = \left[\theta + \sin 2\theta\right]_0^{\pi/3} = \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}. \]

So \(A \approx 1.913\).

Check:

The whole circle \(r = 2\cos\theta\) has area \(\pi \approx 3.14\), and the part outside the unit circle should be a bit more than half of it; 1.913 is reasonable. Writing \(\frac12(2\cos\theta - 1)^2\) instead of \(\frac12(4\cos^2\theta - 1)\) is the classic error.

Example 12.3 Identifying a conic from its polar equation

Identify the conic \(r = \dfrac{12}{2 + 6\sin\theta}\). Give its eccentricity, directrix and vertices.

(Similar to Stewart §10.6, Examples 2–3)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Divide numerator and denominator so the constant term in the denominator is 1, then compare with \(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\sin\theta}\).

Standard form:

Dividing by 2: \(r = \dfrac{6}{1 + 3\sin\theta}\). So \(e = 3 \gt 1\): a hyperbola. From \(ed = 6\), \(d = 2\); the form \(1 + e\sin\theta\) means the directrix is \(y = 2\).

Vertices:

The axis is vertical (sine), so use \(\theta = \pi/2\) and \(3\pi/2\). \(\theta = \pi/2\): \(r = \frac{6}{4} = \frac32\), point \((0, \frac32)\). \(\theta = 3\pi/2\): \(r = \frac{6}{1 - 3} = -3\), and \((-3, 3\pi/2)\) is the point \((0, 3)\). The vertices are \((0, \frac32)\) and \((0, 3)\).

Check:

Centre \((0, \frac94)\), \(a = \frac34\), and the focus at the pole gives \(c = \frac94\). Then \(c/a = 3 = e\). Also, at \((0, \frac32)\): distance to focus \(\frac32\), distance to \(y = 2\) is \(\frac12\), ratio 3.
