10.3Polar Coordinates

When we locate a point with Cartesian coordinates, we go across and then up. But that is not how an astronomer or a radar operator thinks. When we point a telescope, we give a direction and, if we know it, a distance. So, can we describe points that way? Yes, and that is what polar coordinates do.

Definition Choose a point \(O\), the pole, and a ray from it, the polar axis (normally the positive \(x\)-axis). A point \(P\) has polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\), where \(r\) is its distance from \(O\) and \(\theta\) is the angle from the polar axis to the segment \(OP\), positive counterclockwise.

Take note of two features that feel strange at first:

  • Representations are not unique. \((r, \theta)\) and \((r, \theta + 2n\pi)\) are the same point, because going around a full turn brings us back.
  • \(r\) may be negative. \((-r, \theta)\) means: face the direction \(\theta\), then step backwards a distance \(r\). So \((-r, \theta)\) is the same point as \((r, \theta + \pi)\).

Converting. Recall the right triangle with hypotenuse \(r\) and angle \(\theta\). From it,

\[ x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}. \qquad (1) \]
Common mistake \(\tan\theta = y/x\) does not fix \(\theta\) by itself: \((1, -1)\) and \((-1, 1)\) give the same tangent. Always check which quadrant the point is in. For \((-1, 1)\), \(\theta = 3\pi/4\), not \(-\pi/4\).

Polar curves. The graph of \(r = f(\theta)\) is the set of points that have at least one representation satisfying the equation. Let's start simple: \(r = 2\) is a circle of radius 2 about the pole, and \(\theta = \pi/4\) is a line through the pole. What about \(r = 6\cos\theta\)? If we multiply both sides by \(r\), we can use equation (1): \(r^2 = 6r\cos\theta\) becomes \(x^2 + y^2 = 6x\), that is \((x-3)^2 + y^2 = 9\), a circle through the pole. Other families we should recognise:

  • Cardioids \(r = a(1 \pm \cos\theta)\) or \(a(1 \pm \sin\theta)\), heart-shaped;
  • Limaçons \(r = a + b\cos\theta\), with an inner loop when \(|b| \gt |a|\) (Figure 12.1);
  • Roses \(r = a\cos n\theta\) or \(a\sin n\theta\), with \(n\) petals if \(n\) is odd and \(2n\) petals if \(n\) is even (Figure 12.2).

Symmetry saves us half (or more) of the sketching:

  • unchanged when \(\theta \to -\theta\): symmetric about the polar axis (for example, any function of \(\cos\theta\));
  • unchanged when \(r \to -r\) or \(\theta \to \theta + \pi\): symmetric about the pole;
  • unchanged when \(\theta \to \pi - \theta\): symmetric about the vertical line \(\theta = \pi/2\) (any function of \(\sin\theta\)).

Tangents. How do we get the slope of a polar curve? Again, we already know how to do this from parametric equations. We treat \(\theta\) as the parameter: \(x = f(\theta)\cos\theta\) and \(y = f(\theta)\sin\theta\). Then, with \(r' = dr/d\theta\) and the Product Rule,

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/d\theta}{dx/d\theta} = \frac{r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta}. \qquad (2) \]

Horizontal tangents come from \(dy/d\theta = 0\) and vertical ones from \(dx/d\theta = 0\). At the pole, if \(f(\alpha) = 0\) and \(f'(\alpha) \ne 0\), equation (2) reduces to \(\tan\alpha\): the curve passes through the pole along the line \(\theta = \alpha\).

Common mistake \(dr/d\theta\) is not the slope of the curve. It tells us how fast the distance from the pole changes, not how steep the curve is in the \(xy\)-plane. Always use equation (2).
Key equations
\[ x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta,\quad r^2 = x^2 + y^2,\quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} \]
\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\cos\theta - r\sin\theta} \]

10.4Areas and Lengths in Polar Coordinates

In Cartesian coordinates, we found areas by adding up thin rectangles. In polar coordinates, rectangles do not fit well; the natural building block is a circular sector, a slice of pizza. Recall that a sector of radius \(r\) and angle \(\theta\) has area \(\frac12 r^2\theta\).

So, let's slice the region bounded by \(r = f(\theta)\) and the rays \(\theta = a\) and \(\theta = b\) into thin sectors of angle \(d\theta\). Each one has area about \(\frac12 r^2\,d\theta\). Adding them up,

\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12 r^2\,d\theta = \int_a^b \tfrac12 [f(\theta)]^2\,d\theta. \qquad (3) \]

Finding the limits. The hard part is usually choosing \(a\) and \(b\) so that the region is swept out exactly once. For one petal of \(r = 2\cos 2\theta\), the petal on the polar axis starts and ends at the pole, where \(r = 0\): \(\cos 2\theta = 0\) gives \(\theta = \pm\pi/4\). So

\[ A = \int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4} \tfrac12 (2\cos 2\theta)^2\,d\theta = \int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4} (1 + \cos 4\theta)\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}. \]

A good sanity check of equation (3) is the circle \(r = a\), \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\): it gives \(\frac12 a^2\cdot 2\pi = \pi a^2\), as it should. Notice also that \(r^2 \ge 0\), so with the limits in increasing order a polar area can never come out negative; a negative answer means an algebra slip.

Common mistake Integrating over \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\) is not automatically right. The circle \(r = 2\sin\theta\) is traced once for \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\); over \([0, 2\pi]\) it is traced twice and the integral gives \(2\pi\) instead of \(\pi\). Sketch first, then choose the interval.

Area between two curves. If \(f(\theta) \ge g(\theta) \ge 0\) for \(a \le \theta \le b\), the region between them is the big sector region minus the small one:

\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12\left([f(\theta)]^2 - [g(\theta)]^2\right)d\theta. \qquad (4) \]

Take note that it is the difference of the squares, not the square of the difference. We find \(a\) and \(b\) by solving \(f(\theta) = g(\theta)\). Be careful, though: two polar curves can meet at a point that does not show up this way, because the point may have different representations on the two curves. The pole is the usual culprit, since \(r = 0\) at different angles on different curves. A quick sketch reveals such hidden intersections. In practice: sketch both curves, shade the region, decide which curve is farther from the pole, and only then write the integral.

Arc length. Again, using \(\theta\) as the parameter, \(\left(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{d\theta}\right)^2\) simplifies (expand and use \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\)) to \(r^2 + (dr/d\theta)^2\). So the arc length formula from parametric curves becomes

\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta. \qquad (5) \]
Key equations
\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12 r^2\,d\theta \qquad A = \int_a^b \tfrac12\left([f(\theta)]^2 - [g(\theta)]^2\right)d\theta \]
\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta \]

10.6Conic Sections in Polar Coordinates

This section matters a lot for us in astronomy. Kepler found that planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus, and comets can move along parabolas or hyperbolas. If we put the Sun at the pole, all of these orbits have one simple polar equation. Before we get there, let's recall the conics.

  • Parabola: points equally far from a focus and a line (the directrix). \(x^2 = 4py\) has focus \((0, p)\), directrix \(y = -p\) and vertex at the origin; \(y^2 = 4px\) opens sideways.
  • Ellipse: points whose distances to two foci add to \(2a\). \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) (\(a \ge b \gt 0\)) has vertices \((\pm a, 0)\) and foci \((\pm c, 0)\) with \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\).
  • Hyperbola: points whose distances to two foci differ by \(2a\). \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) has vertices \((\pm a, 0)\), foci \((\pm c, 0)\) with \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\), and asymptotes \(y = \pm\frac{b}{a}x\).

The eccentricity \(e = c/a\) measures how stretched the conic is: \(0 \lt e \lt 1\) for an ellipse (near 0 it is almost a circle; the Earth's orbit has \(e \approx 0.017\)), and \(e \gt 1\) for a hyperbola.

Theorem Let \(F\) be a fixed point (focus), \(l\) a fixed line (directrix) not through \(F\), and \(e \gt 0\). The set of points \(P\) with \(\dfrac{|PF|}{|Pl|} = e\) is an ellipse if \(e \lt 1\), a parabola if \(e = 1\), and a hyperbola if \(e \gt 1\).

This one definition covers all three conics, and it is made for polar coordinates. Put the focus at the pole and the directrix at \(x = d\) (\(d \gt 0\)). For \(P = (r, \theta)\): \(|PF| = r\) and \(|Pl| = d - r\cos\theta\). So \(r = e(d - r\cos\theta)\). Solving for \(r\),

\[ r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\cos\theta}. \qquad (6) \]

The four standard positions of the directrix give

DirectrixEquation
\(x = d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\cos\theta}\)
\(x = -d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 - e\cos\theta}\)
\(y = d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\sin\theta}\)
\(y = -d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 - e\sin\theta}\)

Reading off the conic. Take \(r = \dfrac{10}{3 - 2\cos\theta}\). First, we make the constant in the denominator equal to 1 by dividing top and bottom by 3: \(r = \dfrac{10/3}{1 - \frac23\cos\theta}\). Now we can read \(e = \frac23 \lt 1\): an ellipse. From \(ed = \frac{10}{3}\), \(d = 5\), and the minus sign with cosine puts the directrix at \(x = -5\). The vertices lie on the axis through the focus: \(\theta = 0\) gives \(r = 10\), the point \((10, 0)\); \(\theta = \pi\) gives \(r = 2\), the point \((-2, 0)\). So the centre is \((4, 0)\), \(a = 6\), \(c = 4\), and indeed \(c/a = \frac23 = e\) (Figure 12.5).

Parabolas and rotations. When \(e = 1\), for example \(r = \frac{3}{1 + \cos\theta}\), the denominator is 0 at \(\theta = \pi\), so the parabola has no point in that direction: it opens away from its directrix \(x = 3\), with vertex halfway between focus and directrix at \((\frac32, 0)\). Finally, replacing \(\theta\) by \(\theta - \alpha\) rotates the conic counterclockwise through the angle \(\alpha\) about the focus.

Common mistake Reading \(e = 2\) straight from \(\frac{10}{3 - 2\cos\theta}\). The coefficient of \(\cos\theta\) is \(e\) only after the constant term in the denominator is 1.
Key equations
\[ e = \frac{c}{a}, \qquad \frac{|PF|}{|Pl|} = e \]
\[ r = \frac{ed}{1 \pm e\cos\theta} \qquad r = \frac{ed}{1 \pm e\sin\theta} \]

★Key takeaways

  • A point has many polar representations: (r, θ) = (r, θ + 2nπ) = (−r, θ + π).
  • Use x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ and r² = x² + y² to convert, and check the quadrant when finding θ.
  • Treat θ as a parameter to get tangent slopes: dy/dx = (r′ sin θ + r cos θ)/(r′ cos θ − r sin θ).
  • Polar area is ∫ ½ r² dθ; between curves use ½(r_outer² − r_inner²), with limits that sweep the region once.
  • Polar arc length is ∫ √(r² + (dr/dθ)²) dθ.
  • With the focus at the pole, every conic is r = ed/(1 ± e cos θ) or ed/(1 ± e sin θ): e < 1 ellipse, e = 1 parabola, e > 1 hyperbola.

Example 12.1 Converting points and equations

(a) Convert the polar point \((2, 2\pi/3)\) to Cartesian coordinates. (b) Give polar coordinates with \(r \gt 0\) and \(0 \le \theta \lt 2\pi\) for the point \((1, -1)\). (c) Identify the curve \(r = 4\sin\theta\).

(Similar to Stewart §10.3, Examples 1–4)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: a polar point, a Cartesian point, a polar equation. Find: the other form of each.

Plan: Use the conversion formulas (1), and for \(\theta\) always check the quadrant.

Step 1. (a) Substitute \(r = 2\), \(\theta = 2\pi/3\), where \(\cos\frac{2\pi}3 = -\frac12\) and \(\sin\frac{2\pi}3 = \frac{\sqrt3}2\):

\[ x = 2\left(-\tfrac12\right) = -1, \qquad y = 2\left(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = \sqrt3. \]

Step 2. (b) Find \(r\) first: \(r = \sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt2\).

Step 3. Find \(\theta\): \(\tan\theta = \frac{-1}{1} = -1\). The point \((1, -1)\) is in the fourth quadrant, so \(\theta = \frac{7\pi}{4}\) (not \(\frac{3\pi}{4}\), which is in the second quadrant).

Step 4. (c) Multiply both sides by \(r\) so we can use \(r^2 = x^2 + y^2\) and \(r\sin\theta = y\):

\[ r^2 = 4r\sin\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 + y^2 = 4y. \]

Step 5. Complete the square in \(y\): \(x^2 + (y^2 - 4y + 4) = 4\), so \(x^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 4\).

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{(-1, \sqrt3)}\); (b) \(\boxed{(\sqrt2, 7\pi/4)}\); (c) a circle with centre \((0, 2)\) and radius 2, passing through the pole. That is our answer.

Check: For (a), \(\sqrt{(-1)^2 + (\sqrt3)^2} = 2 = r\). ✓ For (c), \(\theta = \pi/2\) gives \(r = 4\), the point \((0, 4)\), the top of that circle. ✓

Example 12.2 Tangent line to a limaçon

Find the Cartesian coordinates of the point on \(r = 1 + 2\cos\theta\) where \(\theta = \pi/2\), and the equation of the tangent line there.

(Similar to Stewart §10.3, Example 9)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(r = 1 + 2\cos\theta\), \(\theta = \pi/2\). Find: the point and the tangent line.

Plan: Convert the point with \(x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta\), then use the polar slope formula (2) with \(r' = dr/d\theta\).

Step 1. Find \(r\) at \(\theta = \pi/2\): \(r = 1 + 2\cos\frac\pi2 = 1 + 0 = 1\).

Step 2. Convert: \(x = 1\cdot\cos\frac\pi2 = 0\), \(y = 1\cdot\sin\frac\pi2 = 1\). The point is \((0, 1)\).

Step 3. Differentiate \(r\): \(r' = -2\sin\theta\), so at \(\theta = \pi/2\), \(r' = -2\).

Step 4. Substitute into equation (2) with \(\sin\theta = 1\) and \(\cos\theta = 0\):

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta} = \frac{(-2)(1) + (1)(0)}{(-2)(0) - (1)(1)} = \frac{-2}{-1} = 2. \]

Step 5. Write the tangent line through \((0, 1)\) with slope 2: \(y - 1 = 2(x - 0)\).

Answer: the point is \((0, 1)\) and the tangent line is \(\boxed{y = 2x + 1}\) (Figure 12.1). That is our answer.

Check: A common slip is to use \(dr/d\theta = -2\) as the slope. In Figure 12.1, the curve at \((0, 1)\) clearly rises to the right, so a positive slope is right.

Example 12.3 Area between two polar curves

Find the area of the region that lies inside the circle \(r = 2\cos\theta\) and outside the circle \(r = 1\).

(Similar to Stewart §10.4, Example 3)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: outer curve \(r = 2\cos\theta\), inner curve \(r = 1\). Find: the area between them.

Plan: Sketch (Figure 12.4), find where the curves meet, then use equation (4). The region is symmetric about the polar axis, so we can double the upper half.

Step 1. Find the intersections: \(2\cos\theta = 1\) gives \(\cos\theta = \frac12\), so \(\theta = \pm\frac\pi3\).

Step 2. Decide which curve is outer. For \(-\frac\pi3 \lt \theta \lt \frac\pi3\), \(\cos\theta \gt \frac12\), so \(2\cos\theta \gt 1\): the circle \(r = 2\cos\theta\) is farther from the pole.

Step 3. Set up equation (4), using symmetry:

\[ A = 2\int_0^{\pi/3} \tfrac12\left[(2\cos\theta)^2 - 1^2\right]d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/3}\left(4\cos^2\theta - 1\right)d\theta. \]

Step 4. Use \(\cos^2\theta = \frac12(1 + \cos 2\theta)\): \(4\cos^2\theta - 1 = 2 + 2\cos 2\theta - 1 = 1 + 2\cos 2\theta\).

Step 5. Integrate and evaluate:

\[ A = \big[\theta + \sin 2\theta\big]_0^{\pi/3} = \frac\pi3 + \sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac\pi3 + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{A = \dfrac\pi3 + \dfrac{\sqrt3}2 \approx 1.913}\). That is our answer.

Check: The whole circle \(r = 2\cos\theta\) has area \(\pi \approx 3.14\), and the part outside the unit circle should be a bit more than half of it; 1.913 is reasonable. Writing \(\frac12(2\cos\theta - 1)^2\) instead of \(\frac12(4\cos^2\theta - 1)\) is the classic error.

Example 12.4 Length of a cardioid

Find the total length of the cardioid \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\).

(Similar to Stewart §10.4, Example 4)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\), traced once for \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\). Find: its length.

Plan: Use equation (5). The trick is a half-angle identity that turns the square root into something we can integrate.

Step 1. Compute \(r'\) and the expression under the root:

\[ r' = -\sin\theta, \qquad r^2 + r'^2 = 1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 2 + 2\cos\theta. \]

Step 2. Use \(1 + \cos\theta = 2\cos^2\frac\theta2\): \(2 + 2\cos\theta = 4\cos^2\frac\theta2\), so \(\sqrt{r^2 + r'^2} = 2\left|\cos\frac\theta2\right|\).

Step 3. Use symmetry about the polar axis: the top half is traced for \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\), where \(\cos\frac\theta2 \ge 0\), so we can drop the absolute value there:

\[ L = 2\int_0^{\pi} 2\cos\frac\theta2\,d\theta. \]

Step 4. Integrate:

\[ L = 4\left[2\sin\frac\theta2\right]_0^{\pi} = 8\left(\sin\frac\pi2 - 0\right) = 8. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{L = 8}\). That is our answer.

Check: If we forgot the absolute value and integrated \(2\cos\frac\theta2\) over \([0, 2\pi]\), we would get \(4\sin\pi - 0 = 0\), which is impossible for a length. The cardioid fits between circles of circumference \(2\pi \approx 6.3\) and \(4\pi \approx 12.6\); 8 is in between. ✓

Example 12.5 Identifying a conic from its polar equation

Identify the conic \(r = \dfrac{12}{2 + 6\sin\theta}\). Give its eccentricity, directrix and vertices.

(Similar to Stewart §10.6, Examples 2–3)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(r = \frac{12}{2 + 6\sin\theta}\). Find: the type of conic, \(e\), the directrix and the vertices.

Plan: Divide so the constant term in the denominator is 1, then compare with \(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\sin\theta}\).

Step 1. Divide numerator and denominator by 2: \(r = \dfrac{6}{1 + 3\sin\theta}\).

Step 2. Read off \(e = 3 \gt 1\), so the conic is a hyperbola.

Step 3. From \(ed = 6\), \(d = \frac{6}{3} = 2\). The form \(1 + e\sin\theta\) means the directrix is horizontal and above the focus: \(y = 2\).

Step 4. The axis is vertical (sine), so the vertices are at \(\theta = \frac\pi2\) and \(\frac{3\pi}2\):

\[ \theta = \tfrac\pi2:\ r = \frac{6}{1 + 3} = \frac32 \Rightarrow \left(0, \tfrac32\right); \qquad \theta = \tfrac{3\pi}2:\ r = \frac{6}{1 - 3} = -3. \]

Step 5. Interpret the negative \(r\): \((-3, \frac{3\pi}{2})\) means face down, then step back 3 units, which lands at \((0, 3)\).

Answer: a \(\boxed{\text{hyperbola with } e = 3,\ \text{directrix } y = 2,\ \text{vertices } (0, \tfrac32) \text{ and } (0, 3)}\). That is our answer.

Check: At \((0, \frac32)\): distance to the focus (pole) is \(\frac32\), distance to \(y = 2\) is \(\frac12\), ratio \(3 = e\). ✓
