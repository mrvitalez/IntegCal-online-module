10.3Polar Coordinates

In Cartesian coordinates we locate a point by going across and then up. But what if we know only how far a point is and in which direction? That is what polar coordinates do (Stewart §10.3). We choose a point \(O\), the pole, and a ray from it, the polar axis (normally the positive \(x\)-axis). A point \(P\) has polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\), where \(r\) is its distance from \(O\) and \(\theta\) is the angle from the polar axis to \(OP\), positive counterclockwise.

Take note of two things that feel strange at first:

  • Representations are not unique. \((r, \theta)\) and \((r, \theta + 2n\pi)\) are the same point.
  • \(r\) may be negative. \((-r, \theta)\) means: face the direction \(\theta\), then step backwards a distance \(r\). So \((-r, \theta)\) is the same point as \((r, \theta + \pi)\).

Converting. From the right triangle with hypotenuse \(r\), we get

\[ x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}. \]

Common mistake \(\tan\theta = y/x\) does not fix \(\theta\): \((1, -1)\) and \((-1, 1)\) give the same tangent. So always check which quadrant the point is in. For \((-1, 1)\), \(\theta = 3\pi/4\), not \(-\pi/4\).

Polar curves. The graph of \(r = f(\theta)\) is the set of points with at least one representation that satisfies the equation. \(r = 2\) is a circle of radius 2 about the pole, and \(\theta = \pi/4\) is a line through the pole. What about \(r = 6\cos\theta\)? Let's multiply both sides by \(r\): \(r^2 = 6r\cos\theta\), that is \(x^2 + y^2 = 6x\), or \((x-3)^2 + y^2 = 9\). So it is a circle through the pole. Other families we should recognise: cardioids \(r = a(1 \pm \cos\theta)\), limaçons \(r = a + b\cos\theta\) (with an inner loop when \(|b| \gt |a|\), Figure 12.1), and roses \(r = a\cos n\theta\) or \(a\sin n\theta\), which have \(n\) petals if \(n\) is odd and \(2n\) petals if \(n\) is even (Figure 12.2).

Symmetry saves us half (or more) of the sketching work:

  • unchanged when \(\theta \to -\theta\): symmetric about the polar axis (for example, any function of \(\cos\theta\));
  • unchanged when \(r \to -r\) or \(\theta \to \theta + \pi\): symmetric about the pole;
  • unchanged when \(\theta \to \pi - \theta\): symmetric about the vertical line \(\theta = \pi/2\) (any function of \(\sin\theta\)).

Tangents. How do we get the slope of a polar curve? Recall Module 11: we treat \(\theta\) as the parameter, \(x = f(\theta)\cos\theta,\ y = f(\theta)\sin\theta\). Then, with \(r' = dr/d\theta\) and the product rule,

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/d\theta}{dx/d\theta} = \frac{r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta}. \]

Horizontal tangents come from \(dy/d\theta = 0\), vertical ones from \(dx/d\theta = 0\). Notice that at the pole, if \(f(\alpha) = 0\) and \(f'(\alpha) \ne 0\), the formula reduces to \(\tan\alpha\): the curve passes through the pole along the line \(\theta = \alpha\).

Key equations
\[ x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta \]
\[ r^2 = x^2 + y^2,\quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} \]
\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{\frac{dr}{d\theta}\cos\theta - r\sin\theta} \]

10.4Areas and Lengths in Polar Coordinates

For the area under \(y = f(x)\) we cut the region into thin rectangles. For a polar curve, rectangles do not fit well. So, what shape do we use? A thin circular sector. Recall that a sector of radius \(r\) and angle \(\theta\) has area \(\frac12 r^2\theta\) (Stewart §10.4). Let's slice the region bounded by \(r = f(\theta)\) and the rays \(\theta = a,\ \theta = b\) into thin sectors of angle \(d\theta\). Each has area about \(\frac12 r^2\,d\theta\). Adding them up,

\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12 r^2\,d\theta = \int_a^b \tfrac12 [f(\theta)]^2\,d\theta. \]

Finding the limits. The hard part is usually choosing \(a\) and \(b\) so that the region is swept out exactly once. For one petal of \(r = 2\cos 2\theta\), the petal on the polar axis starts and ends at the pole, where \(\cos 2\theta = 0\): \(\theta = \pm\pi/4\). So

\[ A = \int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4} \tfrac12 (2\cos 2\theta)^2\,d\theta = \int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4} (1 + \cos 4\theta)\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}. \]

Does the formula make sense? Let's test it on the circle \(r = a\) for \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\): it gives \(\frac12 a^2\cdot 2\pi = \pi a^2\), as it should. Take note also that \(r^2 \ge 0\), so a polar area can never come out negative; a negative answer means an algebra slip.

Common mistake Integrating over \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\) is not automatically right. The circle \(r = 2\sin\theta\) is traced once for \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\); over \([0, 2\pi]\) it is traced twice and the integral gives \(2\pi\) instead of \(\pi\). Sketch first, then choose the interval.

Area between two curves. If \(f(\theta) \ge g(\theta) \ge 0\) for \(a \le \theta \le b\), the region between them has area

\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12\left([f(\theta)]^2 - [g(\theta)]^2\right)d\theta. \]

Notice that it is the difference of the squares, not the square of the difference. We find \(a\) and \(b\) by solving \(f(\theta) = g(\theta)\). But be careful: two polar curves can meet at a point that does not show up this way, because the point may have different representations on the two curves. The pole is the usual culprit. A quick sketch reveals such hidden intersections. So, in practice: sketch both curves, shade the region, decide which curve is farther from the pole, and only then write the integral (Example 2).

Arc length. Using \(\theta\) as the parameter, \(\left(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{d\theta}\right)^2\) simplifies (expand and use \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\)) to \(r^2 + (dr/d\theta)^2\). So

\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta. \]

For the cardioid \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\): \(r^2 + r'^2 = 2 + 2\cos\theta = 4\cos^2(\theta/2)\), so \(L = \int_0^{2\pi} 2|\cos(\theta/2)|\,d\theta = 8\). Without the absolute value we would get 0. Does that mean the formula failed? No. A length of 0 is a sure sign that we dropped the absolute value of the square root.

Key equations
\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12 r^2\,d\theta \]
\[ A = \int_a^b \tfrac12\left([f(\theta)]^2 - [g(\theta)]^2\right)d\theta \]
\[ L = \int_a^b \sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\,d\theta \]

10.6Conic Sections in Polar Coordinates

Quick review of the conics (Stewart §10.5).

  • Parabola: points equally far from a focus and a line (the directrix). \(x^2 = 4py\) has focus \((0, p)\), directrix \(y = -p\), vertex at the origin; \(y^2 = 4px\) opens sideways.
  • Ellipse: points whose distances to two foci add to \(2a\). \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) (\(a \ge b \gt 0\)) has vertices \((\pm a, 0)\) and foci \((\pm c, 0)\) with \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\).
  • Hyperbola: points whose distances to two foci differ by \(2a\). \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\) has vertices \((\pm a, 0)\), foci \((\pm c, 0)\) with \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\), and asymptotes \(y = \pm\frac{b}{a}x\).

The eccentricity \(e = c/a\) measures how stretched the conic is: \(0 \lt e \lt 1\) for an ellipse (near 0 it is almost a circle), \(e \gt 1\) for a hyperbola. Three definitions for three curves is a lot. Is there one definition that covers all of them? Yes:

Theorem Let \(F\) be a fixed point (focus), \(l\) a fixed line (directrix) not through \(F\), and \(e \gt 0\). The set of points \(P\) with \(\dfrac{|PF|}{|Pl|} = e\) is an ellipse if \(e \lt 1\), a parabola if \(e = 1\), and a hyperbola if \(e \gt 1\).

This definition is tailor-made for polar coordinates (Stewart §10.6). Let's put the focus at the pole and the directrix at \(x = d\) (\(d \gt 0\)). For \(P = (r, \theta)\): \(|PF| = r\) and \(|Pl| = d - r\cos\theta\). So \(r = e(d - r\cos\theta)\). Solving for \(r\),

\[ r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\cos\theta}. \]

The four standard positions of the directrix give

DirectrixEquation
\(x = d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\cos\theta}\)
\(x = -d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 - e\cos\theta}\)
\(y = d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\sin\theta}\)
\(y = -d\)\(r = \frac{ed}{1 - e\sin\theta}\)

Reading off the conic. Take \(r = \dfrac{10}{3 - 2\cos\theta}\). First, we make the constant in the denominator equal to 1 by dividing top and bottom by 3: \(r = \dfrac{10/3}{1 - \frac23\cos\theta}\). Now \(e = \frac23 \lt 1\), so it is an ellipse. From \(ed = \frac{10}{3}\), \(d = 5\), and the minus sign with cosine puts the directrix at \(x = -5\). The vertices lie on the axis through the focus: \(\theta = 0\) gives \(r = 10\), the point \((10, 0)\); \(\theta = \pi\) gives \(r = 2\), the point \((-2, 0)\). So the center is \((4, 0)\), \(a = 6\), \(c = 4\), and indeed \(c/a = \frac23 = e\) (Figure 12.5).

Parabolas and rotations. When \(e = 1\), for example \(r = \frac{3}{1 + \cos\theta}\), the denominator is 0 at \(\theta = \pi\): the parabola has no point in that direction, so it opens away from its directrix \(x = 3\), with vertex halfway between focus and directrix at \((\frac32, 0)\). Finally, replacing \(\theta\) by \(\theta - \alpha\) rotates the conic counterclockwise through the angle \(\alpha\) about the focus, so \(r = \frac{ed}{1 + e\cos(\theta - \pi/4)}\) is the same conic turned by \(45^\circ\).

Common mistake Reading \(e = 2\) straight from \(\frac{10}{3 - 2\cos\theta}\). The coefficient of \(\cos\theta\) is \(e\) only after the constant term is 1.
Key equations
\[ e = \frac{c}{a} \]
\[ \frac{|PF|}{|Pl|} = e \]
\[ r = \frac{ed}{1 \pm e\cos\theta} \]
\[ r = \frac{ed}{1 \pm e\sin\theta} \]

★Key takeaways

  • A point has many polar representations: (r, θ) = (r, θ + 2nπ) = (−r, θ + π).
  • We convert with x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ and r² = x² + y², and we check the quadrant when we find θ.
  • Treat θ as a parameter to get tangent slopes: dy/dx = (r′ sin θ + r cos θ)/(r′ cos θ − r sin θ).
  • Polar area is ∫ ½ r² dθ; between curves use ½(r_outer² − r_inner²), with limits that sweep the region once.
  • Polar arc length is ∫ √(r² + (dr/dθ)²) dθ.
  • With a focus at the pole, every conic is r = ed/(1 ± e cos θ) or ed/(1 ± e sin θ); e < 1 ellipse, e = 1 parabola, e > 1 hyperbola.

Example 12.1 Tangent line to a limaçon

Find the Cartesian coordinates of the point on \(r = 1 + 2\cos\theta\) where \(\theta = \pi/2\), and the equation of the tangent line there.

(Stewart §10.3)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(r = 1 + 2\cos\theta\), at \(\theta = \pi/2\)
  • Asked: the point \((x, y)\) and the tangent line.
Step 1: Find r and the Cartesian point

Substitute \(\theta = \pi/2\), where \(\cos\theta = 0\) and \(\sin\theta = 1\).

\[ r = 1 + 2(0) = 1 \]

\[ x = r\cos\theta = 1\cdot 0 = 0, \qquad y = r\sin\theta = 1\cdot 1 = 1 \]

Step 2: Find dr/dθ

\[ r' = \frac{dr}{d\theta} = -2\sin\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; r' = -2 \ \text{at}\ \theta = \tfrac{\pi}{2} \]

Step 3: Substitute into the polar slope formula

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{r'\sin\theta + r\cos\theta}{r'\cos\theta - r\sin\theta} = \frac{(-2)(1) + (1)(0)}{(-2)(0) - (1)(1)} \]

\[ = \frac{-2}{-1} = 2 \]

Step 4: Write the tangent line

Use the point-slope form with the point \((0, 1)\).

\[ y - 1 = 2(x - 0) \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 2x + 1 \]

Answer

The point is \((0, 1)\) and the tangent line there is \(y = 2x + 1\) (Figure 12.1).

Check

A common slip is to use \(dr/d\theta = -2\) as the slope. That is how fast the distance from the pole changes, not the slope of the curve in the \(xy\)-plane.

Example 12.2 Area between two polar curves

Find the area of the region that lies inside the circle \(r = 2\cos\theta\) and outside the circle \(r = 1\).

(Stewart §10.4)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Outer curve \(r = 2\cos\theta\), inner curve \(r = 1\) (Figure 12.4)
  • Asked: the area inside the first and outside the second.
Step 1: Find where the curves meet

Set the two values of \(r\) equal.

\[ 2\cos\theta = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \cos\theta = \tfrac12 \;\Longrightarrow\; \theta = \pm\tfrac{\pi}{3} \]

Between these angles \(2\cos\theta \ge 1\), so \(r = 2\cos\theta\) is the outer curve.

Step 2: Set up the integral

\[ A = \int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3} \tfrac12\left[(2\cos\theta)^2 - 1^2\right]d\theta = \int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3} \tfrac12\left(4\cos^2\theta - 1\right)d\theta \]

The region is symmetric about the polar axis, so let's double the integral from 0 to \(\pi/3\).

\[ A = \int_0^{\pi/3}\left(4\cos^2\theta - 1\right)d\theta \]

Step 3: Use the half-angle identity

\[ 4\cos^2\theta = 2(1 + \cos 2\theta) = 2 + 2\cos 2\theta \]

\[ A = \int_0^{\pi/3}\left(1 + 2\cos 2\theta\right)d\theta \]

Step 4: Integrate and substitute the limits

\[ A = \Big[\theta + \sin 2\theta\Big]_0^{\pi/3} = \frac{\pi}{3} + \sin\frac{2\pi}{3} - 0 = \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \]

Answer

The area is \(\dfrac{\pi}{3} + \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 1.913\) square units.

Check

The whole circle \(r = 2\cos\theta\) has area \(\pi \approx 3.14\), and the part outside the unit circle should be a bit more than half of it, so 1.913 is reasonable. Writing \(\frac12(2\cos\theta - 1)^2\) instead of \(\frac12(4\cos^2\theta - 1)\) is the classic error.

Example 12.3 Arc length of a polar spiral

Find the length of the spiral \(r = \theta^2\) for \(0 \le \theta \le \sqrt5\).

(Stewart §10.4)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(r = \theta^2\), \(0 \le \theta \le \sqrt5\)
  • Asked: \(L = \int \sqrt{r^2 + (dr/d\theta)^2}\,d\theta\).
Step 1: Compute r² + (dr/dθ)²

\[ \frac{dr}{d\theta} = 2\theta \]

\[ r^2 + \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2 = \theta^4 + 4\theta^2 = \theta^2(\theta^2 + 4) \]

Step 2: Simplify the square root

Since \(\theta \ge 0\) on our interval, \(\sqrt{\theta^2} = \theta\).

\[ \sqrt{\theta^2(\theta^2 + 4)} = \theta\sqrt{\theta^2 + 4} \]

\[ L = \int_0^{\sqrt5} \theta\sqrt{\theta^2 + 4}\,d\theta \]

Step 3: Substitute u = θ² + 4

\[ u = \theta^2 + 4, \qquad du = 2\theta\,d\theta, \qquad \theta = 0 \to u = 4, \quad \theta = \sqrt5 \to u = 9 \]

\[ L = \frac12\int_4^9 u^{1/2}\,du = \frac12\cdot\frac23\Big[u^{3/2}\Big]_4^9 = \frac13\left(27 - 8\right) \]

Answer

The length of the spiral is \(L = \dfrac{19}{3} \approx 6.33\) units.

Check

The end point is at distance \(r = 5\) from the pole, and the spiral must be longer than the straight segment from the pole to it (5 units). \(6.33 \gt 5\), so the answer is reasonable.

Example 12.4 Identifying a conic from its polar equation

Identify the conic \(r = \dfrac{12}{2 + 6\sin\theta}\). Give its eccentricity, directrix and vertices.

(Stewart §10.6)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(r = \dfrac{12}{2 + 6\sin\theta}\), focus at the pole
  • Asked: the type of conic, \(e\), the directrix, the vertices.
Step 1: Make the constant in the denominator equal to 1

Divide the numerator and the denominator by 2.

\[ r = \frac{12/2}{2/2 + (6/2)\sin\theta} = \frac{6}{1 + 3\sin\theta} \]

Step 2: Compare with r = ed/(1 + e sin θ)

\[ e = 3 \gt 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{hyperbola} \]

\[ ed = 6 \;\Longrightarrow\; d = \frac{6}{3} = 2 \]

The form \(1 + e\sin\theta\) means the directrix is horizontal, above the pole: \(y = 2\).

Step 3: Find the vertices

With sine, the axis is vertical, so we use \(\theta = \pi/2\) and \(\theta = 3\pi/2\).

\[ \theta = \tfrac{\pi}{2}:\quad r = \frac{6}{1 + 3} = \frac32 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x, y) = \left(0, \tfrac32\right) \]

\[ \theta = \tfrac{3\pi}{2}:\quad r = \frac{6}{1 - 3} = -3 \]

Recall that \((-3, 3\pi/2)\) means: face straight down, then step back 3 units. That is the point \((0, 3)\).

Answer

The conic is a hyperbola with eccentricity \(e = 3\), directrix \(y = 2\), and vertices \(\left(0, \tfrac32\right)\) and \((0, 3)\).

Check

At \(\left(0, \tfrac32\right)\): distance to the focus is \(\tfrac32\), distance to \(y = 2\) is \(\tfrac12\), and the ratio is \(3 = e\).
