5.3Volumes by Cylindrical Shells

Some volumes are awkward with washers. Rotate the region under \(y = 3x - x^2\) (\(0 \le x \le 3\)) about the \(y\)-axis: to use washers you would have to solve \(y = 3x - x^2\) for \(x\), which gives two messy square-root branches. Stewart §5.3 offers a second slicing method that avoids this: cylindrical shells.

Take a thin vertical strip at position \(x\), of height \(f(x)\) and width \(\Delta x\), and rotate it about the \(y\)-axis. It sweeps out a thin cylindrical shell, like a can label. If you cut the shell and unroll it, you get a nearly flat slab whose dimensions are

  • length = circumference \(= 2\pi x\) (the radius is the distance \(x\) to the axis),
  • height \(= f(x)\),
  • thickness \(= \Delta x\).

So its volume is about \(2\pi x f(x)\,\Delta x\). Adding the shells and taking the limit gives

\[V = \int_a^b 2\pi x\, f(x)\,dx \qquad (0 \le a \lt b).\]

A helpful way to remember it: \(V = \int 2\pi\,(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,(\text{thickness})\).

For our region, \(V = \int_0^3 2\pi x(3x - x^2)\,dx = 2\pi\bigl[x^3 - \tfrac{x^4}{4}\bigr]_0^3 = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{27}{4} = \tfrac{27\pi}{2}\). No solving for \(x\) was needed.

Notice what made this easy: the height of each shell is just \(f(x)\), read straight off the graph. With washers the radii would have been the two solutions of \(x^2 - 3x + y = 0\), namely \(x = \tfrac{3 \pm \sqrt{9 - 4y}}{2}\), which are possible to use but much messier to integrate.

Shells versus washers. The key difference is the direction of the strip. Washers use strips perpendicular to the axis; shells use strips parallel to the axis. So for rotation about a vertical axis, shells integrate in \(x\); for rotation about a horizontal axis, shells use horizontal strips and integrate in \(y\): \(V = \int_c^d 2\pi y\, [x_R(y) - x_L(y)]\,dy\) about the \(x\)-axis.

Other axes. As with washers, the radius is the distance to the axis. Rotating the same region about the line \(x = -1\) gives radius \(x + 1\), so \(V = \int_0^3 2\pi (x + 1)(3x - x^2)\,dx = \tfrac{45\pi}{2}\). About \(x = 4\) the radius would be \(4 - x\). If the region lies between two curves, the height is (top − bottom).

Common mistake Students often write \(2\pi f(x)\) and forget the radius factor \(x\), or use \(x\) as the radius when the axis is not the \(y\)-axis. Draw the strip and the axis, and measure the radius on your sketch.
Note Both methods give the same volume when both can be applied. Choose the one whose integral is easier to set up and evaluate.
Key equations
\[ V = \int_a^b 2\pi x\, f(x)\,dx \]
\[ V = \int 2\pi\,(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,d(\text{thickness}) \]

5.4Work

In physics, if a constant force \(F\) moves an object a distance \(d\) in the direction of the force, the work done is \(W = Fd\). In SI units force is in newtons (N), distance in metres (m), and work in joules (J), where \(1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N·m}\). Remember that a mass \(m\) (kg) has weight \(mg\) newtons, with \(g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2\). (Stewart §5.4)

Variable force. If the force \(f(x)\) changes as the object moves from \(x = a\) to \(x = b\), break \([a,b]\) into tiny pieces. On each piece the force is nearly constant, so the work there is about \(f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x\). Summing and taking the limit,

\[W = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx.\]

So work is the area under the force-versus-position graph.

Springs: Hooke's law. The force needed to hold a spring stretched \(x\) metres beyond its natural length is \(f(x) = kx\), where \(k\) is the spring constant. Always measure \(x\) from the natural length. Example: if a force of 30 N holds a spring stretched 0.15 m, then \(k = 30/0.15 = 200\) N/m, and the work to stretch it from 0.1 m to 0.3 m beyond natural length is \(\int_{0.1}^{0.3} 200x\,dx = 100(0.09 - 0.01) = 8\) J.

Common mistake If a spring has natural length 0.2 m and is stretched to a length of 0.25 m, then \(x = 0.05\), not \(0.25\).

Lifting a cable. Here the force is constant but different pieces travel different distances. A 20 m cable with mass 2 kg/m hangs from the top of a building. A small piece of length \(\Delta x\) located \(x\) metres below the top weighs \(2(9.8)\Delta x\) N and must be lifted \(x\) m. So \(W = \int_0^{20} 19.6\,x\,dx = 3920\) J.

Pumping liquid. Slice the liquid into thin horizontal layers. For each layer, find (1) its volume, (2) its weight \(= \rho g \times\) volume, with \(\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3\) for water, and (3) the distance it must be lifted. Multiply and integrate. For a full cylindrical tank of radius 1 m and height 3 m, with water pumped out over the top, a layer at depth \(x\) below the top has volume \(\pi(1)^2\Delta x\) and is lifted \(x\) m, so

\[W = \int_0^3 1000(9.8)\pi\,x\,dx = 44\,100\pi \approx 1.39 \times 10^5\text{ J}.\]

Choosing where to put \(x = 0\) is up to you; just make sure the distance lifted is written correctly in terms of your variable.

Key equations
\[ W = Fd \]
\[ W = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]
\[ f(x) = kx \quad \text{(Hooke's law)} \]

5.5Average Value of a Function

You know how to average finitely many numbers: add them and divide by how many there are. But how do you average infinitely many values, such as the temperature at every instant during a day? (Stewart §5.5)

Sample \(f\) at \(n\) equally spaced points \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) in \([a,b]\). Since \(\Delta x = (b - a)/n\), we have \(n = (b-a)/\Delta x\), and

\[\frac{f(x_1) + \cdots + f(x_n)}{n} = \frac{1}{b - a}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)\,\Delta x.\]

As \(n \to \infty\), the sum becomes an integral. This motivates the definition of the average value of \(f\) on \([a,b]\):

\[f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.\]

For example, the average of \(f(x) = x^2\) on \([0,3]\) is \(\tfrac13\int_0^3 x^2\,dx = \tfrac13 \cdot 9 = 3\). Notice that this is not the average of the endpoint values, \(\tfrac{0 + 9}{2} = 4.5\): the integral takes every value in between into account, and \(x^2\) spends most of the interval at small heights.

Geometric meaning. Rearranging, \(f_{\text{ave}}\,(b - a) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\). If \(f \ge 0\), the rectangle with base \([a,b]\) and height \(f_{\text{ave}}\) has exactly the same area as the region under the graph. The parts of the graph above the line \(y = f_{\text{ave}}\) balance the parts below it.

The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then there is at least one number \(c\) in \([a,b]\) with

\[f(c) = f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.\]

In words: a continuous function actually attains its average value somewhere. Why is this true? By the Extreme Value Theorem, \(f\) has a minimum \(m\) and a maximum \(M\) on \([a,b]\), and the average must lie between them. The Intermediate Value Theorem then guarantees \(f\) hits that value. For \(x^2\) on \([0,3]\), solving \(c^2 = 3\) gives \(c = \sqrt3 \approx 1.73\), which is indeed in \([0,3]\).

Common mistake Forgetting the factor \(\frac{1}{b-a}\). The integral alone is a total (area, distance, accumulated amount), not an average. Also, the theorem needs continuity: a function with a jump may skip over its average value.

Average value has many interpretations. The average velocity over a time interval is \(\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b v(t)\,dt\), which equals displacement divided by elapsed time, exactly the familiar "average speed" from physics. For instance, a cart with velocity \(v(t) = 4t - t^2\) m/s for \(0 \le t \le 4\) has average velocity \(\tfrac14\int_0^4 (4t - t^2)\,dt = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{32}{3} = \tfrac83\) m/s. Solving \(4c - c^2 = \tfrac83\) gives \(c = 2 \pm \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\), so the cart's speedometer reads the average value twice, once while speeding up and once while slowing down.

Key equations
\[ f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]
\[ f(c) = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \ \text{ for some } c \in [a,b] \]

★Key takeaways

  • Shells: \(V = \int 2\pi(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,dx\), using strips parallel to the axis of rotation.
  • Washers use strips perpendicular to the axis, shells use strips parallel to it; pick whichever gives the easier integral.
  • Work by a variable force is \(W = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\); for springs, \(f(x) = kx\) with \(x\) measured from the natural length.
  • For cables and pumping, slice the object, find the weight of each slice and how far it moves, then integrate (weight × distance).
  • The average value is \(f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), and a continuous \(f\) attains it at some \(c\) in \([a,b]\).

Example 5.1 Shells between two curves

The region bounded by \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\) is rotated about the \(y\)-axis. Use cylindrical shells to find the volume.

(Similar to Stewart §5.3, Example 2)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

The axis is vertical, so shells use vertical strips and integrate in \(x\). Radius = distance to the \(y\)-axis = \(x\); height = top − bottom.

Set up:

The curves meet at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\). On \((0,1)\), \(x > x^2\), so the height is \(x - x^2\).

\[V = \int_0^1 2\pi x\,(x - x^2)\,dx.\]

Solution:

\(V = 2\pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^3)\,dx = 2\pi\bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac14\bigr) = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\).

Check:

With washers (horizontal strips, \(0 \le y \le 1\)): outer radius \(\sqrt{y}\), inner radius \(y\), so \(V = \pi\int_0^1 (y - y^2)\,dy = \pi\bigl(\tfrac12 - \tfrac13\bigr) = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\). The two methods agree.

Example 5.2 Pumping water from a hemispherical bowl

A tank in the shape of a hemisphere (bowl) of radius 2 m is full of water. How much work is required to pump all the water out over the rim? Use \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m³ and \(g = 9.8\) m/s².

(Similar to Stewart §5.4, Example 5)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Slice the water into thin horizontal disks. Let \(x\) be the depth of a layer below the rim, so \(0 \le x \le 2\), and each layer is lifted exactly \(x\) metres.

One layer:

In a vertical cross-section the bowl is a semicircle of radius 2 centred at the rim. At depth \(x\), the layer's radius is \(\sqrt{4 - x^2}\) (Pythagoras). Its volume is \(\pi(4 - x^2)\,\Delta x\), its weight is \(1000(9.8)\pi(4 - x^2)\,\Delta x = 9800\pi(4 - x^2)\,\Delta x\) N, and the work to lift it is \(9800\pi(4 - x^2)\,x\,\Delta x\) J.

Solution:

\[W = \int_0^2 9800\pi\,x(4 - x^2)\,dx = 9800\pi\Bigl[2x^2 - \tfrac{x^4}{4}\Bigr]_0^2 = 9800\pi(8 - 4) = 39\,200\pi \approx 1.23 \times 10^5 \text{ J}.\]

Check:

The water has mass \(1000 \cdot \tfrac23\pi(2)^3 \approx 16\,755\) kg. The work equals (total weight) × (how far its centre of mass rises), and here that distance is \(39\,200\pi / (9.8 \cdot 16\,755) = 0.75\) m, a sensible value between 0 and 2 m. A common error is to measure \(x\) from the bottom but still use \(x\) as the lifting distance; with that choice the distance would be \(2 - x\).

Example 5.3 Average temperature and when it occurs

On a certain day the temperature \(t\) hours after 6 a.m. is modelled by \(T(t) = 20 + 6\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\bigr)\) °C. Find the average temperature from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., and the times at which the temperature equals that average.

(Similar to Stewart §5.5, Example 1)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use \(T_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{12 - 0}\int_0^{12} T(t)\,dt\). The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals promises at least one time \(c\) with \(T(c) = T_{\text{ave}}\); solve for it.

Average:

\(\int_0^{12} \sin\bigl(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\bigr)dt = \Bigl[-\tfrac{12}{\pi}\cos\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\Bigr]_0^{12} = \tfrac{24}{\pi}\). So

\[T_{\text{ave}} = \tfrac{1}{12}\Bigl(240 + 6\cdot\tfrac{24}{\pi}\Bigr) = 20 + \tfrac{12}{\pi} \approx 23.8 \text{ °C}.\]

When:

Set \(20 + 6\sin\tfrac{\pi c}{12} = 20 + \tfrac{12}{\pi}\), so \(\sin\tfrac{\pi c}{12} = \tfrac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.6366\). Then \(\tfrac{\pi c}{12} = \sin^{-1}(2/\pi) \approx 0.690\) or \(\pi - 0.690\), giving \(c \approx 2.64\) h and \(c \approx 9.36\) h, i.e. about 8:38 a.m. and 3:22 p.m.

Check:

The temperature runs from 20 °C to a peak of 26 °C at noon, so an average of 23.8 °C is reasonable. Two answers for \(c\) is fine: the theorem guarantees at least one.
