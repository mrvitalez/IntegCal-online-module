5.3Volumes by Cylindrical Shells

Let's start with a problem. Rotate the region under \(y = 3x - x^2\) (\(0 \le x \le 3\)) about the \(y\)-axis. If we try washers, we need horizontal strips, so we must solve \(y = 3x - x^2\) for \(x\). That gives \(x = \tfrac{3 \pm \sqrt{9 - 4y}}{2}\), two messy branches. So, is there an easier way? Yes: cylindrical shells (Stewart §5.3).

This time, let's take a thin vertical strip at \(x\), with height \(f(x)\) and width \(\Delta x\), and rotate it about the \(y\)-axis. It sweeps out a thin hollow cylinder, like the label of a can. If we cut the label and unroll it, we get an almost flat slab with

  • length = circumference \(= 2\pi x\) (the radius is the distance \(x\) to the axis),
  • height \(= f(x)\),
  • thickness \(= \Delta x\).

So one shell has volume about \(2\pi x f(x)\,\Delta x\). We add the shells and take the limit:

Shell method The volume of the solid obtained by rotating the region under \(y = f(x)\), \(0 \le a \le x \le b\), about the \(y\)-axis is \[V = \int_a^b 2\pi x\, f(x)\,dx.\]

An easy way to remember it: \(V = \int 2\pi\,(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,(\text{thickness})\).

Let's go back to our region. \(V = \int_0^3 2\pi x(3x - x^2)\,dx = 2\pi\bigl[x^3 - \tfrac{x^4}{4}\bigr]_0^3 = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{27}{4} = \tfrac{27\pi}{2}\). Notice that we never had to solve for \(x\). The height of each shell is just \(f(x)\), read straight from the graph.

Shells or washers? The difference is the direction of the strip. Washers use strips perpendicular to the axis; shells use strips parallel to the axis. So for a vertical axis, shells integrate in \(x\). For a horizontal axis, shells use horizontal strips and integrate in \(y\); about the \(x\)-axis, \(V = \int_c^d 2\pi y\, [x_R(y) - x_L(y)]\,dy\).

Other axes. Recall that a radius is always a distance to the axis. If we rotate the same region about \(x = -1\), the radius is \(x + 1\), so \(V = \int_0^3 2\pi (x + 1)(3x - x^2)\,dx = \tfrac{45\pi}{2}\). About \(x = 4\) the radius would be \(4 - x\). And if the region is between two curves, the height is (top − bottom).

Common mistake Writing \(2\pi f(x)\) and forgetting the radius \(x\), or using \(x\) as the radius when the axis is not the \(y\)-axis. Draw the strip and the axis, and measure the radius on your sketch.
Note When both methods can be used, they give the same volume. So choose the one whose integral is easier to set up and evaluate.
Key equations
\[ V = \int_a^b 2\pi x\, f(x)\,dx \]
\[ V = \int 2\pi\,(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,d(\text{thickness}) \]

5.4Work

Recall from physics that if a constant force \(F\) moves an object a distance \(d\) in the direction of the force, the work done is \(W = Fd\). In SI units, force is in newtons (N), distance in metres (m) and work in joules (J), where \(1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N·m}\). Take note that a mass \(m\) in kg has weight \(mg\) newtons, with \(g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2\). (Stewart §5.4)

But what if the force is not constant? Let's do what we always do: cut the motion from \(x = a\) to \(x = b\) into tiny pieces. On each piece the force is almost constant, so the work there is about \(f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x\). We add these and take the limit:

Definition The work done by a variable force \(f(x)\) in moving an object from \(a\) to \(b\) is \[W = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx.\]

So the work is the area under the force-versus-position graph.

Springs: Hooke's law. The force needed to hold a spring stretched \(x\) metres beyond its natural length is \(f(x) = kx\), where \(k\) is the spring constant. We always measure \(x\) from the natural length. For example, if a force of 30 N holds a spring stretched 0.15 m, then \(k = 30/0.15 = 200\) N/m. The work to stretch it from 0.1 m to 0.3 m beyond natural length is \(\int_{0.1}^{0.3} 200x\,dx = 100(0.09 - 0.01) = 8\) J.

Common mistake If a spring has natural length 0.2 m and is stretched to a length of 0.25 m, then \(x = 0.05\), not \(0.25\).

Lifting a cable. Here the force on each piece is constant, but the pieces travel different distances. A 20 m cable with mass 2 kg/m hangs from the top of a building. A small piece of length \(\Delta x\), located \(x\) metres below the top, weighs \(2(9.8)\Delta x\) N and must be lifted \(x\) m. So \(W = \int_0^{20} 19.6\,x\,dx = 3920\) J.

Pumping liquid. Let's slice the liquid into thin horizontal layers. For each layer we find (1) its volume, (2) its weight \(= \rho g \times\) volume, with \(\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3\) for water, and (3) the distance it must be lifted. Then we multiply and integrate. For a full cylindrical tank of radius 1 m and height 3 m, pumped out over the top, a layer at depth \(x\) below the top has volume \(\pi(1)^2\Delta x\) and is lifted \(x\) m, so

\[W = \int_0^3 1000(9.8)\pi\,x\,dx = 44\,100\pi \approx 1.39 \times 10^5\text{ J}.\]

Where do we put \(x = 0\)? That is up to us. We just need to write the distance lifted correctly in terms of our variable.

Key equations
\[ W = Fd \]
\[ W = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]
\[ f(x) = kx \quad \text{(Hooke's law)} \]

5.5Average Value of a Function

We know how to average finitely many numbers: add them and divide by how many there are. But how do we average infinitely many values, like the temperature at every instant during a day? (Stewart §5.5)

Let's sample \(f\) at \(n\) equally spaced points \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) in \([a,b]\). Since \(\Delta x = (b - a)/n\), we have \(n = (b-a)/\Delta x\), and

\[\frac{f(x_1) + \cdots + f(x_n)}{n} = \frac{1}{b - a}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)\,\Delta x.\]

Do you recognize the sum? It is a Riemann sum. As \(n \to \infty\) it becomes an integral, and this gives us the definition:

Definition The average value of \(f\) on \([a,b]\) is \[f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.\]

For example, the average of \(f(x) = x^2\) on \([0,3]\) is \(\tfrac13\int_0^3 x^2\,dx = \tfrac13 \cdot 9 = 3\). Notice that this is not the average of the endpoint values, \(\tfrac{0 + 9}{2} = 4.5\). Why? The integral takes every value in between into account, and \(x^2\) stays small for most of the interval.

What does it look like? Rearranging, \(f_{\text{ave}}\,(b - a) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\). If \(f \ge 0\), the rectangle with base \([a,b]\) and height \(f_{\text{ave}}\) has exactly the same area as the region under the graph. The parts of the graph above the line \(y = f_{\text{ave}}\) balance the parts below it.

Mean Value Theorem for Integrals If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then there is at least one number \(c\) in \([a,b]\) with \[f(c) = f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.\]

In words: a continuous function actually reaches its average value somewhere. Why is this true? By the Extreme Value Theorem, \(f\) has a minimum \(m\) and a maximum \(M\) on \([a,b]\), and the average must be between them. Then the Intermediate Value Theorem says \(f\) hits that value. For \(x^2\) on \([0,3]\), solving \(c^2 = 3\) gives \(c = \sqrt3 \approx 1.73\), which is in \([0,3]\).

Common mistake Forgetting the factor \(\frac{1}{b-a}\). The integral alone is a total (area, distance, accumulated amount), not an average. Also, the theorem needs continuity: a function with a jump may skip over its average value.

Take note that the average velocity over a time interval is \(\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b v(t)\,dt\), which is displacement divided by elapsed time, the same "average velocity" from physics. For instance, a cart with velocity \(v(t) = 4t - t^2\) m/s for \(0 \le t \le 4\) has average velocity \(\tfrac14\int_0^4 (4t - t^2)\,dt = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{32}{3} = \tfrac83\) m/s. Solving \(4c - c^2 = \tfrac83\) gives \(c = 2 \pm \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\). So the cart's speedometer shows the average value twice, once while speeding up and once while slowing down.

Key equations
\[ f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]
\[ f(c) = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \ \text{ for some } c \in [a,b] \]

★Key takeaways

  • Shells: \(V = \int 2\pi(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,dx\), using strips parallel to the axis of rotation.
  • Washers use strips perpendicular to the axis, shells use strips parallel to it; we pick whichever gives the easier integral.
  • Work by a variable force is \(W = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\); for springs, \(f(x) = kx\) with \(x\) measured from the natural length.
  • For cables and pumping, slice the object, find the weight of each slice and how far it moves, then integrate (weight × distance).
  • The average value is \(f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), and a continuous \(f\) reaches it at some \(c\) in \([a,b]\).

Example 5.1 Shells between two curves

The region bounded by \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\) is rotated about the \(y\)-axis. Use cylindrical shells to find the volume.

(Similar to Stewart §5.3, Example 2)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Curves: \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\).
  • Axis: the \(y\)-axis.
  • Method: cylindrical shells. Asked: the volume \(V\).
Step 1: Find where the curves meet

\[x^2 = x \;\Rightarrow\; x(x - 1) = 0\]

So \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\).

Step 2: Find the radius and the height of a shell

The axis is vertical, so shells use vertical strips. The radius is the distance to the \(y\)-axis, \(x\). On \((0, 1)\) we have \(x > x^2\), so the height is (top − bottom).

\[\text{radius} = x, \qquad \text{height} = x - x^2\]

Step 3: Set up the integral

\[V = \int_0^1 2\pi x\,(x - x^2)\,dx = 2\pi\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^3)\,dx\]

Step 4: Integrate

\[V = 2\pi\Bigl[\tfrac{x^3}{3} - \tfrac{x^4}{4}\Bigr]_0^1\]

\[V = 2\pi\Bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac14\Bigr) = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{1}{12} = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\]

Answer

So the volume is \(\tfrac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.524\) cubic unit.

Check

With washers (horizontal strips, \(0 \le y \le 1\)): \(R = \sqrt{y}\), \(r = y\), and \(\pi\int_0^1 (y - y^2)\,dy = \pi\bigl(\tfrac12 - \tfrac13\bigr) = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\). The two methods agree.

Example 5.2 Shells about a line other than an axis

The region under \(y = x^2\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 1\) is rotated about the line \(x = 2\). Use cylindrical shells to find the volume.

(Similar to Stewart §5.3, Example 4)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Region: under \(y = x^2\), above \(y = 0\), \(0 \le x \le 1\).
  • Axis: the vertical line \(x = 2\).
  • Asked: the volume \(V\).
Step 1: Find the radius of a shell

A vertical strip at \(x\) is to the left of the axis \(x = 2\). Its distance to the axis is

\[\text{radius} = 2 - x\]

Step 2: Find the height of a shell

The strip goes from \(y = 0\) up to \(y = x^2\).

\[\text{height} = x^2\]

Step 3: Set up the integral

\[V = \int_0^1 2\pi(2 - x)\,x^2\,dx = 2\pi\int_0^1 (2x^2 - x^3)\,dx\]

Step 4: Integrate

\[V = 2\pi\Bigl[\tfrac{2x^3}{3} - \tfrac{x^4}{4}\Bigr]_0^1\]

\[V = 2\pi\Bigl(\tfrac23 - \tfrac14\Bigr) = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{5}{12} = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}\]

Answer

So the volume is \(\tfrac{5\pi}{6} \approx 2.618\) cubic units.

Check

With washers about \(x = 2\), \(0 \le y \le 1\): \(R = 2 - \sqrt{y}\), \(r = 2 - 1 = 1\), and \(\pi\int_0^1 \bigl[(2 - \sqrt{y})^2 - 1\bigr]dy = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}\) too. A common error is to use \(x\) as the radius; that measures from the \(y\)-axis, not from \(x = 2\).

Example 5.3 Work to stretch a spring

A force of 30 N is needed to hold a spring stretched from its natural length of 12 cm to a length of 18 cm. How much work is done in stretching the spring from 18 cm to 22 cm?

(Similar to Stewart §5.4, Example 2)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Natural length: 12 cm = 0.12 m.
  • A force of 30 N holds it at length 18 cm.
  • Asked: the work from length 18 cm to length 22 cm.
Step 1: Change lengths to stretches in metres

Hooke's law measures \(x\) from the natural length, so let's subtract 12 cm and change to metres.

\[18\text{ cm} - 12\text{ cm} = 6\text{ cm} = 0.06\text{ m}\]

\[22\text{ cm} - 12\text{ cm} = 10\text{ cm} = 0.10\text{ m}\]

Step 2: Find the spring constant

Use \(f(x) = kx\) with \(f = 30\) N at \(x = 0.06\) m.

\[30 = k(0.06)\]

\[k = \frac{30}{0.06} = 500 \text{ N/m}\]

Step 3: Set up the work integral

\[W = \int_{0.06}^{0.10} 500x\,dx\]

Step 4: Integrate and substitute

\[W = \Bigl[250x^2\Bigr]_{0.06}^{0.10}\]

\[W = 250(0.01) - 250(0.0036)\]

\[W = 2.5 - 0.9 = 1.6\]

Answer

So the work done is \(1.6\) J.

Check

If we had used the lengths 0.18 and 0.22 m in the integral, we would get \(250(0.0484 - 0.0324) = 4\) J, which is wrong. Always subtract the natural length first.

Example 5.4 Pumping water from a hemispherical bowl

A tank in the shape of a hemisphere (bowl) of radius 2 m is full of water. How much work is required to pump all the water out over the rim? Use \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m³ and \(g = 9.8\) m/s².

(Similar to Stewart §5.4, Example 5)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Tank: hemisphere of radius 2 m, full of water, pumped over the rim.
  • \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m³, \(g = 9.8\) m/s².
  • Asked: the work \(W\) in joules.
Step 1: Choose the variable

Let's slice the water into thin horizontal layers and let \(x\) be the depth of a layer below the rim, so \(0 \le x \le 2\). With this choice, each layer is lifted exactly \(x\) metres.

Step 2: Find the radius of a layer

In a vertical cross-section the bowl is a semicircle of radius 2 centred at the rim. By Pythagoras, the layer at depth \(x\) has radius \(r\) with

\[r^2 + x^2 = 2^2\]

\[r^2 = 4 - x^2\]

Step 3: Find the volume and weight of a layer

\[\text{volume} = \pi r^2\,\Delta x = \pi(4 - x^2)\,\Delta x \text{ m}^3\]

\[\text{weight} = 1000(9.8)\pi(4 - x^2)\,\Delta x = 9800\pi(4 - x^2)\,\Delta x \text{ N}\]

Step 4: Multiply by the distance and integrate

Work for one layer = weight × distance = \(9800\pi(4 - x^2)\,x\,\Delta x\).

\[W = \int_0^2 9800\pi\,(4x - x^3)\,dx\]

\[W = 9800\pi\Bigl[2x^2 - \tfrac{x^4}{4}\Bigr]_0^2\]

\[W = 9800\pi(8 - 4) = 39\,200\pi\]

Answer

So the work required is \(39\,200\pi \approx 1.23 \times 10^5\) J.

Check

The water weighs \(1000(9.8)\cdot\tfrac23\pi(2)^3\) N, and \(W\) divided by that weight is \(0.75\) m, a sensible average lifting distance between 0 and 2 m. If we had measured \(x\) from the bottom, the distance lifted would be \(2 - x\), not \(x\).

Example 5.5 Average temperature and when it occurs

On a certain day the temperature \(t\) hours after 6 a.m. is modelled by \(T(t) = 20 + 6\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\bigr)\) °C. Find the average temperature from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., and the times at which the temperature equals that average.

(Similar to Stewart §5.5, Example 1)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(T(t) = 20 + 6\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\bigr)\) °C, \(t\) in hours after 6 a.m.
  • Interval: 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., so \(0 \le t \le 12\).
  • Asked: \(T_{\text{ave}}\), and the times \(c\) with \(T(c) = T_{\text{ave}}\).
Step 1: Write the average-value formula

\[T_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{12 - 0}\int_0^{12}\Bigl[20 + 6\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\bigr)\Bigr]dt\]

Step 2: Integrate the sine term

Let \(u = \tfrac{\pi t}{12}\), so \(dt = \tfrac{12}{\pi}\,du\).

\[\int_0^{12} \sin\bigl(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\bigr)dt = \Bigl[-\tfrac{12}{\pi}\cos\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\Bigr]_0^{12}\]

\[= -\tfrac{12}{\pi}\cos\pi + \tfrac{12}{\pi}\cos 0 = \tfrac{12}{\pi} + \tfrac{12}{\pi} = \tfrac{24}{\pi}\]

Step 3: Compute the average

\[T_{\text{ave}} = \tfrac{1}{12}\Bigl(20 \cdot 12 + 6 \cdot \tfrac{24}{\pi}\Bigr)\]

\[T_{\text{ave}} = \tfrac{1}{12}\Bigl(240 + \tfrac{144}{\pi}\Bigr) = 20 + \tfrac{12}{\pi} \approx 23.8\text{ °C}\]

Step 4: Solve T(c) = average

\[20 + 6\sin\tfrac{\pi c}{12} = 20 + \tfrac{12}{\pi}\]

\[\sin\tfrac{\pi c}{12} = \tfrac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.6366\]

\[\tfrac{\pi c}{12} \approx 0.690 \quad\text{or}\quad \tfrac{\pi c}{12} \approx \pi - 0.690 = 2.452\]

\[c \approx 2.64 \text{ h} \quad\text{or}\quad c \approx 9.36 \text{ h}\]

Answer

So the average temperature is \(20 + \tfrac{12}{\pi} \approx 23.8\) °C, and the temperature equals it at about \(t \approx 2.64\) h and \(t \approx 9.36\) h, that is, about 8:38 a.m. and 3:22 p.m.

Check

The temperature goes from 20 °C up to 26 °C at noon, so 23.8 °C is reasonable. Two values of \(c\) is fine: the theorem promises at least one.
